# Projection

This describes agent projection's first slice (issue #82): text panels and code panels the agent projects onto walls in a space, which participants pull off, leave floating, put back and dismiss, and point at as they speak. It belongs to the space and the agent stage, not to any environment, and works in every environment. Later slices add the other content formats, manipulation and MCP tools #82 describes.

## Requirement

**Two kinds of wall.** Projection is spatially registered, and environments differ in shape, so there are two kinds of wall:

- **The Tomori wall.** Every space has exactly one. It belongs to the space and the central Tomori interface, not to an environment, and what is on it stays as the space changes environment. It is curved: its six slots stand in one row along an arc, each turned to face the point the arc curves about, 2.3 m ahead of the row's middle. By default it stands below the swarm, sized for the swarm whatever avatar a client shows (see `avatars.md`), the middle of its row 2.3 m straight ahead of home at 0.52 m, so that the arc curves about home and every slot faces a participant standing there; its slots are drawn at 0.7 of a flat wall's, about 42 by 25 cm, 3 cm apart, and its label, `TOMORI`, is below the row. There, in the default layout, it hides neither the swarm, whose flock stays above it, nor any worker's station, which stand at least 40° to either side of straight ahead, while the row spans less than 34° either side; its top edge is more than 22° below the eyes from home, below the status pill, which stands about 20° below them (see `interaction.md`); and it is clear in Sky, the grid and the farmyard. **Its home: beneath the agent.** The Tomori wall's home is beneath the agent's home, wherever the environment puts it: the middle of its row 0.88 m directly below the swarm's middle, facing the way the agent faces, curving about the point 2.3 m before it, with a clear gap between the swarm and the top of its row, and never lower than 0.52 m above the floor where participants arrive (`tomori_protocol::entourage::wall_beneath`, see `environments.md`), which in the default layout is the default above; or, overriding it, the environment's `stage` anchor, when its registration gives one (`tomori_server::Environment::with_stage`), in its own coordinates, carried into the space's as every anchor is, the row's middle standing there, facing as the anchor faces. It stays at its home, where it starts and where it is whenever the space opens, and never moves of its own accord. **Travelling with a summoned agent.** The Tomori wall belongs to the space and follows the agent when it is summoned, since it is where things are put during a direct conversation: when a participant summons the agent with the trigger, or the agent goes to someone with `move_agent`, the server stands the wall at a travel spot beneath the agent, before whoever it went to, `SpaceState::summoner`, as its home stands beneath the agent's home: the middle of its row 0.88 m directly below the swarm's middle, never lower than 0.52 m above the floor, facing them, the agent standing 2.3 m from them and a little above their eyes (see `environments.md`), so that the wall reads below their eyes as from home; where that is not clear it stands 0.3 m nearer them or 0.4 m further, at the same height, then turns aside from the agent, 15°, 30° then 45° either way, at the first spot within 4 m of their eyes at which no slot, along its edges or across its diagonals, passes through the environment's solids (`tomori_server::Environment::with_solids`, the boxes its sightline tests check), every other wall shown or the swarm's ring, so that a thin board or a wall seen edge on standing across a slot counts too, with none of the solids or walls between their eyes and any slot's middle, every slot's bottom edge at least 0.3 m above the floor, and every slot seen beneath the swarm, the top of each lower than any part of the swarm, as far as it reaches, by at least 1° as seen from their eyes, so that the swarm never stands in front of what is on the wall (`tomori_protocol::entourage::seen_beneath`); where none is clear, it stays at its home. Its panels keep their slots, pinned and arranged as they were, and the space's edits to its grid and curve apply wherever it stands; changed while it is away, they are the space's, for its home too. Nobody carries it, at home or away, so it has no pose of its own to keep. Sent home, by `move_agent home`, **Send Tomori home**, another environment or a restart, it goes home with the agent. The travel spot is the summons', for this run and this environment, never kept, so after a restart the agent and its wall are home. Every client sees it fold, travel and unfold: when the Tomori wall's pose changes within the same space and environment, it folds where it stood, its slots and their panels drawing in over 0.3 s to a bundle at its middle, each at 45% of its size, travels to its new pose at 1.5 m/s, taking 0.4 s to 2.5 s, rising a little on the way, and unfolds there over 0.3 s; sent back on the way, it turns round from where it is drawn, folded as far as it is. Meanwhile nothing is put on it by hand, a wall taken hold of is let go, and its panels are not pointed at; a panel the agent projects meanwhile goes onto it, in its slot, and is drawn folded with the rest, unfolding with it. A wall that appears elsewhere, as a space is joined or changes environment, stands there at once. An older client, which knows nothing of travel, draws the wall where the projection says, so it sees the wall move at once rather than fold; an older server never moves it. Each environment's tests check that its home is within comfortable reading distance of the eyes where participants arrive, 4 m, as `READING` is for the agent's panels below, faces them, and stands clear of the scenery (see `environments.md`). A space's own edits to the Tomori wall, its grid and shape (see adjusting walls, below), stand over its home, in every environment, and are all the space keeps of it: the home is the environment's alone, so the wall stands at each environment's home, with the space's edits, and nothing moves it by itself. The server uses a `stage` only if, carried, it is finite, within 20 m of home and has an orientation, made upright, as walls are. With an environment's homes or a placement of its own, what the wall hides is the environment's to arrange. When the space changes environment, it moves to where the new one puts it, with its panels. The Tomori wall's pose is never kept, nor can an owner move it, so a space kept before the curved wall stands it at the new default, its panels in the same slots. Its participants and agent may change its grid and shape, within limits that keep it clear of the swarm, the stations and the pill (see adjusting walls, below).
- **Environment walls.** These belong to the environment the space is in now: those its registration declares, with `Environment::with_walls`, a general hook that names no particular environment, of which the server uses at most six, each a pose or an `EnvironmentWall`, which says its grid and the size of its slots too; and those its owners add. An environment's own walls are part of the place, and locked until a space's owner unlocks them (see locked walls, below). Tomori's own server gives the farmyard one, two slots wide and one high, on its notice board against the right of the fence (see `farmyard.md`); Sky and the grid declare none.

**Notice boards and consoles.** The intended pattern for an environment's own walls is a notice board or a console: a wall that is part of the place, drawn by the environment's scenery as something that stands there, such as a board on posts in a yard or a console on a bridge, which people walk up to and put content on, the agent's panels and their own, and organise as they like. An environment may have several, each where its kind of work is done, locked so that they stay where the place puts them, while what is on them is the space's. The farmyard's notice board is the first; a downstream environment's consoles, beside the data they show, are expected to follow it.

**A wall's grid, size and shape.** A wall's slots are a grid of columns and rows (`Grid`), numbered from its top left as seen from in front, left to right and then down: from one to eight columns and one to four rows, and at most 24 slots. Its slots are the size of a flat wall's, about 60 by 36 cm, scaled by its `scale`, from 0.5 to 5, so that a panel in one is drawn larger or smaller and is legible from further or nearer, and a wall is as large as its slots and the gutters between them. **A big wall**, of a scale above 2, is for reading from far away, such as a dashboard across a yard: its text is as many times larger, and as legible from as many times further, so that a wall of five times a standard slot's 15 m away reads as a standard one 3 m away. Text is drawn from the glyph atlas's distance fields at any size (see `text.md`), so a big wall's is as sharp as a standard one's at the same angular size, and its panels hold no more than a standard wall's, so a big wall costs the frame and the walls' bound of 78 slots nothing more; an image panel's image, at most 2,048 pixels, is as sharp on a big wall far away as on a standard one near. An older client, which takes a scale of at most 2, draws a bigger wall at 2, smaller than its slots, but legibly. **Its slots may be any shape:** its `aspect`, their width over their height, from 1:4, tall, to 4:1, wide, makes them taller or wider than a standard slot's, about 5:3, at the same `scale`, which is then the size of their text, so that a tall slot holds more rows of a list and a wide one more columns of code, and the wall grows to hold them. A tall slot is as wide as a standard one at its scale and as tall as its aspect makes it; a wide one as tall and as wide as its aspect makes it, to a whole number of text cells, so that a flat wall's board stays a whole number of them. A panel in a slot of its own shape is laid out to fill it at that text size: a text panel's body wraps at the slot's width and shows as many rows as its height holds, a code panel's code wraps at as many columns as its width holds and shows as many rows, never fewer than a standard slot's 34 columns of 12 rows, or 48 of 16 of code; it fills the slot to within a cell, centred. An image panel, where a client draws one, fits its image within the slot, as within a standard one. It is flat, or curved (`WallShape`): its columns along an arc about a vertical line a radius ahead of it, from 1 m to 50 m, spanning at most half a circle, each slot turned to face that line, its rows one above another about its middle. **It may tilt** (`Wall::tilt`) about its horizontal middle line, the line through its pose along its own x, from 45° with its top away from the people in front of it, facing up, as for a wall below them, to 45° with its top towards them, facing down, as for a wall above them; a curved wall tilts its whole arc about that line, the line it curves about tilting with it. Its pose stays upright, turned only about the vertical, as every wall's is, and its tilt is kept beside it, so that an older client, which knows no tilt, draws it upright, and the server's making walls upright keeps its meaning. A wall in a group of walls snapped together (see below) stands upright, since walls each tilted about their own middle lines would no longer meet as one board: a tilt asked for one is refused, saying to take it out of its group first; a tilted wall has none snapped to it; and a tilted wall snapped to another stands upright, as a curved one takes the other's radius. Unless it says otherwise, a flat wall is three columns of two rows at scale 1, with the label `WALL` above them, upright, about 2 m by 0.9 m, translucent, with its empty slots faintly marked; and a curved wall is six in one row at 0.7, each slot a faint board of its own, its label below them, as the Tomori wall is by default. A flat wall's board is measured in a panel's font pixels, 1.9 mm at scale 1: a gutter of 24 pixels between its slots and around them, its label's row above them, and a whole number of rows of text tall, scaled about its middle. A wall is at most 16 m wide and 9 m tall, a cinema's screen.

**One layout, shared.** Where each slot of a wall stands, given its pose, grid, scale, shape and tilt, is `tomori_protocol::wall` (`Wall::slot_frames`, `Wall::board`, `Wall::size`, `Wall::frame`, its pose tilted, which its slots and board stand in, and `Wall::slot_pose`), which the server and every client share: the server lays panels out in it, tells the agent where slots are and what people look at from it, and floats a displaced panel where its slot stood from it; clients draw slots and aim at them from it. So the two cannot drift apart. A client draws a grid or scale it is sent beyond these bounds as its shape's own, and a curve too tight or around too much flat.

**Adding and removing walls.** An owner adds a wall where they stand, from the hand menu's Space section (see `hand-menu.md`): it stands 1.8 m ahead of their head, level, a little below their eyes, upright and facing them. An owner removes the added wall nearest them from the same section. Each panel on it is left floating where its slot stood, facing as it faced, rather than going with it, so that removing a wall loses nothing. Only walls an owner added are removed; at most six are added in each environment of a space, each within 20 m of the space's middle. A collaborator or visitor is refused both, and their menu does not offer them.

**Adjusting walls.** Participants, owners and collaborators alike, adjust walls, as they rearrange panels; a visitor adjusts none, and the server refuses them, as it refuses every action their role does not permit (see `security.md`). `SetWall` changes a wall's grid, the size of its slots, their shape and its own, flat or curved at a radius, its tilt, and, for a wall an owner added, its pose; what it leaves out stays. A change is the space's, shown to everyone in it at once and kept with it across restarts:

- **The Tomori wall** keeps its grid, shape and tilt for the space, in every environment, as it keeps its panels. It stays one row of one to six slots, at its own 0.7 and of a standard slot's shape, flat or curved, tilted or not, since tilting about its middle line keeps it within where it stands, and stands where the environment or the default puts it, so that it stays below the swarm, within the 40° either side of straight ahead the workers' stations leave clear, and below the pill.
- **An environment's own walls** are adjusted for the space in that environment, kept with what the space keeps for it and shown again on returning: their grid, slots' size and shape and tilt, but not where they stand or whether they are there, which are the environment's.
- **An added wall's** grid, slots' size, shape, tilt and pose are its own. Moved, it stands anywhere within 20 m of the space's middle, made upright as every wall is, and its panels move with it, in their slots.
- **Panels keep their places.** A panel keeps its column and row as a wall's grid changes, so that nothing moves that need not, even where its slot is numbered anew. A panel in a column or row the wall no longer has goes into the free slot nearest it, by column and row, those a participant put where they are first and then the newest; with no free slot, it floats where its slot stood, facing as it faced. No change to a wall takes a panel away, pinned or not.
- **Bounds.** The walls shown in one environment hold at most 78 slots together, as many as the Tomori wall and twelve walls of six, and at most twelve panels float in it, with one more for each slot the walls hold fewer than 78, so that a projection at every limit still fits in one frame. Panels a wall gives up therefore float beyond the twelve; leaving another floating takes away the one floating longest, as ever, of which they are the newest. A change, or a new wall, that would take the walls past 78 slots, or give them more slots while that many float that they would go past the bound together, is refused, saying so, as is a grid, size or curve a wall cannot have.

**Locked walls.** Every wall is locked or unlocked, for the space:

- **A locked wall changes for nobody.** It is neither moved, resized, reshaped, tilted, bent, given columns or rows, nor removed, by a participant, an owner or the agent, until an owner unlocks it: the server refuses every `SetWall` and `RemoveWall` of it, whatever it asks, saying `That wall is locked: ask an owner to unlock it.`, and the agent's tools refuse it too (see `mcp.md`). Panels still go onto it, are rearranged on it, come off it and are thrown away, and the agent projects onto it, as onto any wall: the lock is the wall's, not its content's.
- **Who locks.** Only owners lock and unlock walls, with `LockWall`, from the headset's padlock or by asking the agent, since it changes what everyone else may do; collaborators adjust walls that are unlocked as before, and visitors adjust none. The server checks the role.
- **What is locked by default.** An environment's own walls are locked, since they are part of the place; walls an owner adds, and the Tomori wall, are unlocked.
- **Kept with the space.** Whether a wall is locked is kept as the rest of its adjustments are: the Tomori wall's for the space, in every environment; an environment's own wall's, and an added wall's, for the space in that environment, across restarts. Only what differs from the default is written.
- **An environment's own wall, unlocked,** is moved as an added wall is, carried on the headset or placed by the agent, within 20 m of the space's middle and made upright, its panels moving with it, and where it stands is kept for the space in that environment, over where the environment puts it, as its grid and slots are. It is never removed, unlocked or not, since it is part of the place, and the environment's scenery still draws what it stands on there.
- **Locked again,** it stays where it was moved and as it was changed, so that an owner may move a board and fix it there. **Putting it back** is a choice of its own: `LockWall`'s `reset`, the padlock's companion `↺` on the headset, or `set_wall`'s `reset`, puts an environment's own wall back where and as the environment has it, its grid, slots and curve the environment's, and locks it, the space keeping nothing of its own for it; its panels keep their column and row, or go into the nearest free slot, or float where they were, as for any change to a wall. A wall already as the environment has it changes nothing.
- **The Tomori wall** takes the lock as any wall does, unlocked by default: locked, its grid and shape change for nobody. Its place is never the space's, locked or not: it stands at the home its environment gives it (see two kinds of wall, above).
- **Snapping.** A locked wall is no snap target, and is never snapped, attached or detached, as it is never carried: `Wall::is_locked` says which, for the code that snaps walls together. A change to a wall of a group that would move or reshape a locked wall of it, as carrying, bending or resizing a wall the rest stand from does, is refused, saying a wall of its group is locked, and the headset carries no group with a locked wall in it (see snapping walls together, below).

**The agent adjusts walls.** The agent changes, adds and removes walls through its tools when someone in the space asks (see `mcp.md`), as an owner may: `set_wall` changes a wall's columns and rows, the size of its slots, or a width in metres that sets it, their shape, as an aspect such as `9:16`, or their width and height in metres instead, its curve, its tilt, in degrees, and the place in front of someone of a wall someone added or of an environment's own unlocked, and, only when an owner asks, locks or unlocks it, or puts an environment's own back, locked, so that "tilt this wall down a bit" is the wall the speaker looks at, tilted about 10° further down; `add_wall` adds a wall in front of someone, laid out as it says, a big wall, of a scale above 2, as many times further than 1.8 m as its scale is above 2, so that its text looks as large as a wall of twice a standard slot's does there, and one so tall that it would reach below the floor where people arrive raised until its bottom is 10 cm above it; `remove_wall` removes a wall someone added, leaving its panels floating. It names a wall by its identity or name, as `presence` lists it, or as the wall someone is looking at or is nearest, `looking [NAME]` or `near [NAME]`, so that "make this wall four panels wide and one high" means the wall the speaker was looking at as they began speaking. Each change it makes is shown to everyone at once, logged with the space, and told: its result asks it to tell everyone what it changed, and a notice titled "Walls", from Tomori, on the Tomori wall says it, replacing the last such notice unless a participant put that one somewhere.

**What the server keeps.** The server keeps every space's walls and panels with the space, in its record of spaces, in a format of its own with a schema version, so that they survive a restart:

- the panels on the Tomori wall, for the space;
- for each environment the space has been in, its added walls, each with its grid, slots' size and shape where changed, how the space has adjusted that environment's own walls, and the panels on its walls or floating in it, keyed by the space and the environment;
- how the space has adjusted the Tomori wall, where changed.

What a wall leaves at its default is not written, so that a space kept before walls could be adjusted reads as it was, in the same schema version.

Walls and floating panels of an environment are hidden, not deleted, while the space is in another, and shown again, where they were, when it returns. At most eight environments are kept for a space; going to a ninth forgets the one left longest ago. At most 120 panels are kept for a space in all, more than it can show in one environment, at most 90; beyond that, the hidden panel put where it is longest ago is forgotten, so that what a space keeps stays bounded. Panels on a wall the environment no longer declares are kept, but not shown. The server makes every wall and floating panel a client places upright, turning one sent tilted only about the vertical.

**Where the agent's panels go.** Panels are shared, so a panel goes where everyone in the space sees it, on a wall or floating in the space's scene coordinates, and the agent chooses a spot good for whoever asked:

- **By default, where whoever asked was looking, near enough to read.** When a participant asks something, each panel the agent projects in answer goes onto the wall they were looking at as their utterance began, from where their head was and the way it faced in the shared space, as their presence last said, if it is near enough for them to read. Only walls within **4 m** of their eyes, measured to the wall's nearest slot or board, or as many times further as a big wall's slots are larger than a standard slot's, so that a wall five times as large is taken within 20 m, are taken: within it, a flat wall's text, at its 1.9 mm font pixel, is about as sharp as the curved Tomori wall's, at seven tenths of that, from home, about 2.6 m away, as far as walls are designed to be read from; beyond it, as on a wall 7 m away, a slot's text is too small to read without pulling the panel off. Comfortable reading, an x-height of about 0.45° (see `text.md`), would put a slot's text within about 1.4 m, nearer than any wall stands from home, so the reading distance follows the walls' design instead, and is one distance for every wall of a standard slot's size or smaller rather than computed from each slot's text; a big wall's text is as legible as many times further away as it is larger, so its distance grows with it. Of those walls:
  - **The nearest wall looked at.** A wall is looked at when the ray from their eyes meets one of its slots, its board or a panel on it, or when one of its slots' middles is within **10°** of that ray, since a head faces a little above what the eyes look at and passes just over a low wall, such as the Tomori wall below the swarm, that they plainly look towards. Of the walls looked at, the nearest is chosen: into the empty slot the ray meets, when it meets one of that wall's, or else into its most prominent free slot.
  - **Else the nearest wall in view.** Looking at none, as between two of a curved wall's slots, at a floating panel, past a person or at a wall too far away, it goes onto the nearest wall, to their eyes, whose slots are in their field of view, as `presence` measures it (see `mcp.md`);
  - **else the nearest wall**, of the Tomori wall and the current environment's walls;
  - **else, with no wall within 4 m, floating in front of them**, as `front` puts it.

  Panels the agent projects without anyone asking, such as in a reply after a restart, go onto the Tomori wall.
- **Where the agent says.** A panel block's header may say where its panel goes with `place:`, and a code block's info string with a `place=` attribute, as below, in one of these forms, its form in any case and separated from what it names by spaces or a colon:
  - `near NAME`: onto the wall nearest that participant, as they last reported;
  - `looking NAME`: where that participant is looking, as by default, near enough to read;
  - `front NAME`: floating in front of them, 1.2 m ahead, level with where they face, 15 cm below their eyes and facing them, as a participant's panel left floating does, within the twelve that float in an environment;
  - `wall WALL`: onto the wall with that identity or name, as `presence` names it, such as `Tomori wall`, `environment wall 1` or `added wall 2`, in any case, with or without `the`;
  - `wall WALL slot N`: into that wall's slot `N`, numbered from 0 as `presence` and `create_layout` number them, `slot` in any case and separated from the wall and the number by spaces or colons, as in `wall added wall 2 slot 3` or `wall:wall-4:slot:0`, so that the agent fills a layout it built precisely; a slot the wall does not have is refused by the agent's tools, saying how its slots are numbered, and, in a block, ignored as a wall not here is;
  - `near`, `nearest`, `looking` or `front` alone: the same, for whoever asked, as they began speaking.

  A name is a participant's display name, in any case. A form the server does not know, a name of nobody in the space who has reported where they are, a visitor's, and a wall not here are each ignored, and the panel goes where it would by default. Each block is placed alone: a block without a `place` goes where the default puts it, whatever its reply's other blocks say.
- **The layout decides the rest.** A panel put on a wall takes the slot chosen if it is free, or else the wall's most prominent free slot, and a full wall gives up a panel for it as the layout below says, so that what a participant arranged stays while the agent's own panels make room; a panel placed where nothing is free never floats instead. Since there is always the Tomori wall, there is always a wall.
- **Moving panels.** The agent may move any panel in the space with its `move_panel` tool, by its identity or title, to a place in the same forms (see `mcp.md`), when someone asks or when it serves what they asked for, such as gathering the details someone asked to see where they can read them, and says what it moved. A form that names nobody is about whoever asked for what the agent is doing now: the speaker of the input it was last given, as they began speaking.
  - **A panel someone placed stays theirs.** A panel a participant put where it is, which is pinned, moves too, and stays pinned where it goes, and arranged on a wall, so that what the agent projects next, a full wall's room for it and the next reply's code panels treat it as they treat any panel a participant placed. A notice on the Tomori wall, titled "Moved", says what moved and where, such as "Tomori moved 'Merging now…' to the Tomori wall.", its title on one line and cut at 48 characters, so that whoever placed it, and everyone else, sees why it is no longer where they left it; one such notice replaces the last unless a participant has moved it, as Tomori's other notices do. Moving the agent's own panels gives no notice, since they were never anyone's.
  - **A panel carried stays in the hand.** A panel a participant is carrying is refused, saying so, until they let it go. A client says which panel it carries in its presence (`ClientMessage::Presence`'s `carrying`), which the server keeps with the rest of what it reports and relays to nobody; a report gone stale, 5 seconds old, says nothing, and an identity over 1024 bytes names no panel. An older client never says, so its participant's panel can be moved out of their hand, and they then carry nothing.
  - **Only what was asked moves.** The agent's moves never displace another panel a participant placed, but as the layout below makes room on a full wall; nothing else moves automatically.

**Layout.** The server lays walls out, and a client draws each panel in the slot it is given, in that slot's own plane on a curved wall. A wall's slots are numbered from its top left, as seen from in front of it, left to right and then down; a curved wall's, left to right.

- **The agent's panels** take their wall's most prominent free slot.
- **A participant chooses the slot.** A panel put on a wall by hand goes into the slot the participant put it in, and is then **arranged**. When that slot holds another panel, the two **change places**: the other goes into the slot the one put there came from, on the same wall; or, when it came from elsewhere, into the wall's most prominent free slot; or, with none free, to where it came from, on another wall or floating where it floated. A panel put back in its own slot stays, arranged.
- **A full wall** gives up a panel for a newcomer: the one that has been on it longest of those not arranged, or, when every one is arranged, the one there longest. It leaves, dismissed, and the newcomer takes its slot. So the agent's new panels never move what a participant arranged while there are others to make room, and arranged panels keep their slots.
- **Nothing else ever moves:** a panel pulled off leaves its slot empty until the next one fills it, so that nobody's reading is disturbed. The newest panel on each wall is framed in blue, so that it is found at once.
- An older client, which names no slot, has its panel put on the wall as the agent's are, in the most prominent free slot, though arranged, and kept in its slot when put back on the wall it is on.

**Floating panels.** Squeezing the right grip while pointing at a panel pulls it off its wall; it follows the ray at the distance it was picked up from, facing the participant, as a GitHub card does (see `github.md`). **Where it will go.** While a panel is carried, the slot it would go into if let go is outlined, framed brightly and faintly filled, at the slot's size and in its plane, over any panel there: the slot the ray meets first, within 8 m and 3 cm of its edges, in each slot's own plane, so that a curved wall's slots are aimed at as they stand; or else, as while a teleport is aimed, when the ray chooses none, the slot it is carried within 0.4 m of the plane of and 0.25 m of the edges of, the nearest. The ray then ends on the slot. Let go, it goes into that slot, as the layout says, and is shown there at once, at the slot's size, until the server agrees; anywhere else it floats where it was left, upright and facing the participant, within 20 m of the space's middle, at its own size. A floating panel belongs to the environment it was left in, since where it floats means something only in that environment's geometry; one pulled off the Tomori wall stays with that environment, and is hidden when the space changes environment, unless it is put back on the Tomori wall first. At most twelve float in each environment of a space; leaving another floating takes away the one floating longest. A panel let go is shown where it was let go until the server agrees, or for 3 seconds. While the hand menu, the right wrist's transcript or a card carried takes the ray, a panel carried is let go where it is. Travel does not: a panel carried stays in the hand through a teleport, a turn and a backstep (see `locomotion.md`). When a card and a panel are both on the ray, the grip picks up the nearer, as the ray points at the nearer.

**Moving a panel carried.** While a panel is carried, the right stick moves it, and nothing else:

- **Forward and back** push it further along the ray or pull it closer, smoothly, for as long as the stick is pushed, at up to 1.2 of its distance a second, by the same proportion of its distance near or far, so that it feels alike at arm's length and across the room, from 0.3 m to 5 m.
- **Left and right** turn it 10° about the vertical, clockwise for right as seen from above, once a flick, as a snap turn turns the participant; it stays upright. Its turn is always a whole number of 10° snaps from facing the participant square, so square is always exactly reachable, by flicking back as many times, or at once by **clicking the right stick**, which, while a panel is carried, turns it square rather than playing the latest reply again.
- Each push is one gesture, owned by the axis it first passes its threshold in, forward or back at 0.3 from the middle and sideways at 0.6, until the stick returns within 0.2 of the middle: a push along the ray that rolls sideways turns nothing, and a flick that turned moves it nowhere.
- A push belongs to the panel if one is carried as the stick leaves the middle, and is then nobody else's until it returns there, so that the right wrist's transcript and the result cards never take a push that moved the panel, nor the panel one that moved them. The transcript does not show while a panel is carried, since turning the wrist to see it would let the panel go.
- The left stick still travels, carrying the panel along (see `locomotion.md`). Panels are carried only with the right grip, so the left stick is always travel's, or, in an environment that allows no travel, nothing's, since the ray is on the panel, which is Tomori's and comes before the environment's client side (see `interaction.md`), and the right stick the panel's.
- Let go, the panel floats as far along the ray and as turned as it was carried, and the server is sent that pose, which it makes upright; put on a wall, the slot decides its pose.

A 3D object carried later is to be moved the same way, by the same gestures, gaining its further axes as further snapped turns, without changing what a panel does.

**Dismissing.** A panel is dismissed, for everyone, only by throwing it away while carrying it: pressing **B** while it is carried with the grip, and letting B go within 0.4 seconds while it is still carried, dismisses it as B is let go. **B on its own never dismisses a panel**, whatever the ray points at. A press of B while a panel is carried is the panel's, until B is let go, as a push of the right stick is: it backs out of nothing else, and a hold that began on a panel carried, however long, neither dismisses it nor leaves the space, so that letting go of B after a hold changes nothing. A panel let go of the grip before B is put down where it was, not dismissed. A panel thrown away is no longer carried, nor put down as the grip is let go, and is drawn nowhere from that moment, until the server's projection agrees, or for 3 seconds. Otherwise B backs out of what is open as ever, the list, a selected queue entry, waiting for a name, the card saying no speech was heard and a problem shown, in that order, and holding it for a second leaves the space (see `interaction.md`). Pointing at a panel, with nothing else to back out of, a participant's press of B says, on the status pill, "Grip it and press B to throw away." A dismissal cannot be undone; the panel's text stays in the transcript. A visitor carries no panel, so their **B** dismisses nothing, and says to hold B to leave, as it always does. In the desktop window, holding **G**, the right grip, carries the panel pointed at, and **Backspace**, B, throws it away as B does (see `desktop.md`).

**The hint while carrying.** While a participant carries a panel, a small hint stands just below it, or above it where below would be under the floor, 1.5 m below the eyes, or more than 40° below the eye line. It faces the participant, moves with the panel, and shows only while a panel is carried and the panels take the ray and grip, from the frame after it is pulled off, once where it would go is known. It says, for someone carrying a panel for the first time, what each control does to it, in five short rows of the control, bold, and what it does, the throw in a warm colour of its own, as it is for everyone:

```text
Stick ↕  further / closer
Stick ↔  turn
Click    square up
B        throw away, for all
Let go   leave it floating
```

The last row says what letting go of the grip would do now: "put it in the slot" where a slot is outlined and empty or the panel's own, "swap with that one" where another panel is in it, and "leave it floating" otherwise. The desktop window names its keys instead: `PgUp PgDn`, `[ ]`, `R`, `Backspace` and `Let go G`. Its font pixel takes 0.0015 radians from the eyes, whatever its distance, so that its x-height is about 0.48°, above the 0.45° text reads comfortably at (see `text.md`), alike at arm's length and across the room, and it is at most about 25° wide. It is always shown in full: a hint that grows shorter once a participant has used each control a few times on a headset is an idea for later, which would need what each headset has learned kept on it.

**Drawn by distance.** Walls and panels, the outline of a slot, the label on the ray and the hint are drawn as `rendering.md` requires: whatever is nearer the eye is in front, so that the hand menu, the pill, cards or a controller in front of a panel cover it, and a panel covers whatever is behind it, in both eyes. A panel's face, the label and the hint hide what is behind them; a wall's board and the outline of a slot are see-through. A panel carried over a slot, about as far away as the panel in it, is drawn over it and over the outline, with its hint whole below it.

**Shared.** Everything here is the space's, the same for everyone in it, participants and visitors alike. Any participant pulls off, puts back and dismisses any panel, and the server sends the result to everyone. Visitors see the walls and panels, as they see the transcript that holds the same text, but change nothing: their grip pulls nothing off.

**Text panels.** A panel has a title on one line, a body of Markdown-lite text, and a little metadata: up to four keys and values, and how long ago it was projected. Its face shows the title, bold, the metadata and age on the next row, a rule, and the body on twelve rows, 34 cells wide, styled as the right wrist's transcript styles a message, with the same renderer and the same glyph atlas (see `wrist-transcript.md` and `text.md`), ending with "…" where it is longer. Its font pixel is 1.9 mm, so a panel is about 60 by 36 cm, legible from about 3 m.

**Checklists.** A task item, as GitHub-flavoured Markdown writes one, `- [ ]` or `- [x]`, with `*` or `+` for `-`, `[X]` for `[x]`, or after a number, as `1. [ ]`, shows a box in place of its bullet, after its number where it has one: `☐`, empty and bright, for an item not done, and `☑`, ticked and green, for one done, whose text is dimmed, so that what is done recedes and what is left stands out. Both boxes are bold, one cell each, drawn from Noto Sans Symbols 2, so they are as legible as the text beside them at a panel's font pixel; a ticked item is dimmed rather than struck through, since text has no such style, and the dim colour is the one metadata uses, legible as it is. A nested item stands under the text of the item it is in, however wide that item's number, a later paragraph of an item under its own text, and a wrapped item hangs under its text, past its box. The right wrist's transcript shows task items the same way, as it shares the renderer.

**Tables.** A GitHub-flavoured Markdown table, with its header, its delimiter row and its rows, is drawn as a grid, never as its pipes and never cut. Each column is as wide as its widest cell where the table fits; otherwise each keeps at least 10 cells, or its widest cell where that is narrower, then grows to fit its longest word, then shares the room left in proportion to what it still lacks, and a cell's text wraps at words within its column onto as many rows as it needs, a row of the table being as tall as its tallest cell. A word is broken only where it alone is wider than its column, and nothing in a table ends with "…". A cell keeps its strong text and inline code, and is aligned as its column's delimiter says, `:---` or `---` left, `:---:` centred and `---:` right, its header too. The header is bold on a faint blue bar, with a rule below it; columns are two cells apart, a faint thin line at the left edge of the second, a cell's width from the text either side; and every other row of the body is faintly striped, so that a row that wraps reads as one. Widths are counted in cells as drawn, a CJK character taking two and an emoji, drawn `?`, one, so that columns stay aligned. A `<br>` in a cell, or anywhere, breaks the line. Where the columns do not all fit at their least, as a table of many columns in a slot or on the wrist may not, it is drawn as a card for each row instead: a line of `Header: value` for each of its cells that is not empty, the header dim and bold and a wrapped value hanging two cells under it, with a faint rule between cards. On a wall a table takes the slot's 34 cells; floating, a body with a table grows to as many as 60 (below). Scrolled into a table's body, its header and the rule below it stay at the top of the face, over the rows they cover, so that every column stays named, where the face has room for two rows below them, until what follows the table pushes them up, a row at a time, as its last row passes beneath them. The right wrist's transcript draws tables the same way, at its own width.

**Floating panels fit their text.** A panel floating or carried is sized to show all of its text: as wide as its widest row of body, its title and its metadata, from 24 to 48 cells, a comfortable reading width, or to 60 for a body with a table, about a metre, 38° from 1.5 m, so that a table shows more of its columns side by side and a row of it is still read by moving the eyes rather than the head, its body wrapped there by the same rule, and as many body rows as that takes, from 3 to 30, ending with "…" beyond that. Put on a wall, it is a slot's size again, its text cut with "…" as before. Its size is pointed at and aimed with as it is drawn. How a panel is sized is worked out from the rows its body makes, the one place its content becomes rows, so that a content type added later sizes the same way. A code panel floating or carried fits its code in the same way, keeping its slot's width (below).

**Scrolling.** A panel with more rows than its face shows scrolls, for each participant alone: how far down they have scrolled it is theirs, kept on their own device, never sent, and never moves what anyone else sees, so that two people reading the same panel never fight over it.

- **The right stick, pointing.** A push of the right stick that leaves the middle while the ray points at such a panel, nothing being carried and the right wrist's transcript not showing, scrolls it, and belongs to it, as every push belongs to what took it, until the stick returns within 0.2 of the middle: back scrolls down and forward up, as forward moves the transcript's focus up, smoothly, from none at 0.2 from the middle to 15 rows a second, about a slot's rows of code, pushed all the way, as the square of how far past 0.2, so that a little push reads slowly. The push goes on scrolling the panel it began on if the ray wanders off it, and pages no cards and moves no focus. A push that began elsewhere never scrolls, even once the ray comes to a panel. While a panel is carried the stick is the panel's, pushing, pulling and turning it (above), and scrolls nothing, nor does a push begun scrolling move the panel if the grip then pulls it off; pointing without carrying is otherwise free, since the stick only pages result cards there, which a push begun on a panel that scrolls no longer does. Pointing at a panel, the push is never an environment's client side's, even with nothing to scroll, since the panel is Tomori's and comes first (see `interaction.md`).
- **The mouse's wheel.** In the desktop window, the wheel scrolls the panel the mouse points at, three rows a notch, down for a turn towards the participant, a touchpad's 40 pixels counting as a notch, unless a panel is carried or the transcript shows (see `desktop.md`).
- **Where it stops.** A panel scrolls from its top until its last row is its face's last, and no further, however far it is pushed; scrolling back starts at once from there. Each scroll is kept while the panel is projected, as its size changes: a code panel pulled off and grown to show all of its code shows it from the top, and put back on a wall is scrolled as it was. A panel no longer projected is forgotten.
- **What it shows.** A code panel scrolls by rows of code, so that a line taller than its face is read whole: scrolled partway through a wrapped line, its first row shown is that line's continuation, marked `↪`. Its row below the code says how many lines start above the rows shown, as `↑ 3 above`, and how many below are not shown whole, counting those it was not sent, as `↓ 4 more`; once the last line it holds is shown, it says how many lines of its block it was not sent, as `180 more lines in the transcript`, or nothing where there are none. A text panel scrolls by rows of its body, its last row shown ending with "…" while there is more below, after its text where there is room for it, never over its last character nor on a table's row, and keeping a table's header at its top (see Tables). Either, while it has more than it shows, has a slim bar at the right edge of its rows, in its padding: a faint track as tall as the rows shown and a brighter thumb as tall, and as far down, as the rows shown are of them all.
- **Saying so.** The label on the ray, while it points at a panel that scrolls, ends with a row of how to scroll it, `Stick ↕  scroll`, or `Wheel  scroll` in the desktop window, so that someone pointing at one for the first time learns it where they need it, as the hint while carrying teaches carrying; the hint while carrying says nothing of scrolling, since the stick then moves the panel.

**Pointing.** In a space, the ray points at the nearest panel it meets where nothing else it meets, a card, a worker or what the environment shows, is nearer; a panel carried is pointed at all along. It highlights the panel and ticks lightly, as for any new target (see `feedback.md`). A small label on the ray, just in front of where it meets the panel, names the panel, its metadata, how long ago it was projected and where it is. A panel pointed at as **X** is pressed is what the words are about: the client sends only its identity, `{"kind": "panel", "panel": "panel-3"}` (`Resource::panel`), with the utterance's beginning, and the server describes it from its own store, never from what a client says, before the words, as `Ada: [Pointing at panel "Build status" (on the Tomori wall, projected 2 minutes ago; source: "CI")] explain this one`. The title, or the start of the body, and each value are in double quotes, since they are the agent's text and may come from what it read. Everything in the description is cleaned onto one line, with double quotes and square brackets made single quotes and parentheses, so that nothing in it can end the block (see `spaces.md`). A panel the server does not show in the space's environment, or an oversized identity, is dropped and the words kept. The recording indicator and status line name the panel by its title, or the start of its body.

**Pointing at an item.** The description names a panel's id, where it has one, and when the agent last updated it, as `panel "Agenda" (id agenda, on the Tomori wall, projected 5 minutes ago, updated just now)`. Where the ray meets a row of a list item of a text panel's body as X is pressed, a row of the item's text, wrapped or nested, the client sends with the identity the line of the body, from 0, that the item begins on, as its own face lays the body out, `{"kind": "panel", "panel": "panel-3", "line": 4}` (`Resource::panel_at`), and the server describes the item from its own copy of the body, never from the client: its number of how many, whether it is ticked, for a task item, and its text, in double quotes, cleaned as the title is, as `Ada: [Pointing at panel "Agenda" (id agenda, on the Tomori wall, projected 5 minutes ago; item 3 of 5, not ticked: "Decide the pairing window")] this one's done`, so that "this one" ticks the right item. Items are numbered from 1 in the order they are written, nested ones included, not counting lines in a fenced code block or a rule such as `- - -`, the same numbers `update_panel` takes (see `mcp.md`). A line that begins no item, one past the body, or a code panel's, adds nothing, and the panel is described as before. An older server ignores `line`. Pointing anywhere else on a panel, or at a code panel, sends its identity alone, as before.

**How the agent creates panels.** The agent projects a panel by writing a fenced code block whose language is `panel` in its reply:

````text
```panel
title: Build status
source: CI
---
**3 crates** failed.
```
````

- **The format.** An optional header of `key: value` lines, each key a letter and up to 23 more letters, digits, `-` or `_`, ended by a line of `---`; then the body. Without such a header the whole block is the body, so prose with a rule in it stays prose. `title` is the panel's title; `id` is the agent's own name for it, by which it updates it in place (below); `place` says where it goes, as above; of `id` and `place`, the first a block gives decides, and neither is ever metadata; every other key is its metadata, lower case, the first of each kept, at most four.
- **Bounds.** Everything in a block is untrusted. A block larger than 8 KiB is not projected, nor one with neither a title nor a body, nor more than four from one reply. A title is cut to 80 characters, a key to 24 and a value to 80, each on one line as text others choose is cleaned (see `security.md`), without format characters, and a body to 30 lines and 1,200 characters, each line cleaned the same way; each ends with "…" where it is cut. The body is drawn as text, never as HTML.
- **Where it is found.** The server finds a reply's panel blocks when the reply completes, not while it streams, from its Markdown: a fence of three or more backticks or tildes, indented at most three spaces, whose info string starts with `panel`, closed by a fence of the same character at least as long, or else running to the end of the reply, as CommonMark reads it. A block of another language is skipped whole, so that a panel block quoted inside one is not projected. Each reply is projected once, as it completes, or, for one that completed while the backend was reconnecting, as the session is read again or the message restored; the history a space reads when it is bound again after a restart is not projected again.
- **Speech and the transcript.** A panel block is a code block, so speech skips it, as it skips every code block (see `speech.md`), while the transcript keeps the reply as the agent wrote it, the block shown as code.
- **The agent's instructions.** Tomori's own layer of the agent's instructions (see `spaces.md`) says that it may project a text panel when something is better read than heard, such as a list, steps, a summary or figures, in a fenced block of language `panel`, with its header, a line of dashes and a short Markdown body; that Tomori shows it without reading it aloud and keeps it in the transcript, so it says in a sentence what it shows; that it uses at most one or two in a reply, and only when they help; and that a participant may point at one as they speak. It says that a Markdown table in a panel is drawn as a grid, its long cells wrapped, but to keep it compact, with few columns and short cells, since a panel too narrow for its columns shows each row as lines of `Header: value` instead, and that a list of label: detail lines often reads better in a headset. It says to give a panel it will revise an `id` header line, that a later block with the same id, or `update_panel`, updates that panel where it is, rather than projecting another, and to project an agenda, questions to cover or a to-do list as a checklist and tick its items off as the conversation covers them; and that when someone points at an item of a list, Tomori names the panel's id and which item it is.

**Panels the agent updates.** A text panel may have an id, the agent's own name for it, by which the agent updates it in place rather than projecting it again, so that, for example, an agenda stays where everyone is reading it while its items are ticked off as a conversation covers them:

- **Ids.** A header line `id: agenda` gives one: from 1 to 32 ASCII letters, digits, `-` and `_`, read in lower case, scoped to the space. A value that is not one names nothing, and the panel is projected without an id. Only text panels take ids; code panels, which a reply's next code panels replace, do not. The server keeps the id with the panel, and sends it as `Panel::slug`.
- **Updating in place.** A later panel block with the id of a text panel the space shows in its current environment, on the Tomori wall, on the environment's walls or floating in it, replaces that panel's title, body and metadata, bounded and cleaned as any block's, and keeps everything else: its identity, its wall and slot or where it floats, whether a participant put it there, its place in its wall's order of which leaves first, and its age, since it was projected. So it is not a new panel: it takes no slot, makes no room, is not framed as its wall's newest, and moves nothing else. The server keeps when it last updated it. A block the same as what the panel shows changes nothing. Two blocks with the same id in one reply project it, then update it.
- **Moving it.** An update whose block says `place:` moves the panel there, as `move_panel` does, unless a participant put it where it is, which always stays, or is carrying it, which stays in their hand; without one, it stays where it is, wherever whoever asked stood or looked.
- **Dismissed, or elsewhere.** A block with the id of no panel shown projects a new panel, placed as any new one is, so that a list dismissed comes back where new panels go. A panel with that id kept hidden with another environment the space has been in is forgotten as the new one is projected, so that an id names at most one panel in a space.
- **Clients.** The change reaches every client as any change of the projection does, the panel with the same identity and its new text, which a client draws in place of the old, laying its body out again, with no flicker, no new frame and nothing moved; a client keeps what it holds for a panel by its identity, such as one let go but not yet confirmed. An older client, which knows no ids, draws the new text the same way.
- **Through the tools.** `panels` reads back each panel's id, body, items and revision, and `update_panel` changes a text panel's title or body, or ticks and unticks its items by number or text, without resending it, keeping it where it is in the same way (see `mcp.md`).
- **Revisions.** Each text panel has a revision, a count of the changes to its content, 1 as it is projected, kept with the space across restarts, 1 for a panel kept before revisions were, and sent with it as `Panel::revision`. It counts one each time its title, body or items change: by a block with its id, by `update_panel` or by a participant's tick. A change that leaves its content as it was, and moving it, count none. A participant's tick, and the agent's `update_panel`, may say the revision they saw; **one that says a revision the panel has changed since is refused, and changes nothing**, so that a tick or an item named by its number never lands on another item of a body changed since it was read. Tomori does not look for the item elsewhere in the body, by its text or otherwise: the participant, or the agent, reads the panel as it is now and does it again.

**Code panels.** Every other fenced code block in a reply, of any language but `panel`, is projected as a code panel, onto the same wall as the reply's text panels, with them, in the order they are written:

- **Where it goes.** As a text panel goes: by default where whoever asked was looking, or where a `place=` attribute in its info string says, as a word of its own, `place=FORM:NAME`, or, to hold spaces, `place="FORM NAME"`, such as `rust place=near:Ada` or `python place="looking Ada Lovelace"`. The attribute is taken out of the info string, whether the server knows its form or not, so that it is neither the panel's title nor its language.
- **What it shows.** Its title is the block's info string, such as `rust` or `python title=area.py`, cleaned and cut as a text panel's title is, or `code` when it has none; its language is the info string's first word, in lower case. Its body is the block's first 200 lines, from the first that is not blank and without the blank lines it ends with, numbered from 1, each with its tabs expanded to stops of four, format characters removed, control characters and other spaces made plain spaces, and trailing spaces dropped, which a participant scrolls through (above). Its lines are kept whole, however long, and cut with "…", the lines after left out, only where they reach 8,000 characters together. It says how many lines the block has, so that one longer than it holds says how many more lines are only in the transcript. Its face shows the title, bold, a row of its language, how many lines and how long ago it was projected, a rule, and the lines in monospace, each after its number, dim, in a calm palette on the panel's dark face, at a font pixel of 1.36 mm, so that the same 60 by 36 cm holds 48 columns and 16 rows of code, legible from about 2 m, and a row below them saying what is above and below them.
- **Long lines wrap.** A line longer than the panel's 45 columns of code wraps, softly, rather than running off its edge or being cut: it breaks after a space, or after punctuation that ends or opens a part of it, such as a comma, an operator or an opening bracket, but never before a closing one, at the last such place in the latter half of the row and after its indentation, and otherwise, as for a token longer than a row, at the row's end, never through a wide character. The spaces at a break are dropped. Each later row of the line hangs under it, at its own indentation and one indent step more, the least indentation of the block's lines, of two to four spaces, or four where none is indented, keeping at least 16 columns for code; it has no number, but a dim `↪` in the number's place, so that it reads as the same line. A line wraps onto as many rows as it needs, every one after the first marked so, and is never cut short with "…" while there are rows for it; a token longer than a row breaks at the row's end rather than being cut. Every character keeps its kind, so highlighting carries across a break. A line that fits is drawn as before. A code panel on a wall keeps its slot's size, wrapped or not: below the first line shown, a line that does not fit whole in what is left of its 16 rows is left for the row below them, which counts it among those below, as `↓ N more`, counting every line not shown in full; and a first line shown that alone is taller than what is left shows what fits, ending with a dim "…", the rest of it reached by scrolling.
- **Floating, it fits its code.** A code panel floating or carried keeps its 48 columns and is as tall as its rows of code, wrapped as above, from 3 to 40 rows, so that at most it is about 75 cm tall, as tall as a text panel floating at most, about 28° from 1.5 m, comfortably within view at the distances panels are carried; it has a row below its code only where it does not show all of its block, then scrolling as on a wall. The 17 lines of the block the maintainer could not see, which wrap onto about 25 rows, show whole. Put back on a wall, it is a slot's size again, scrolled as it was. Its size is pointed at, aimed with and hinted beside as it is drawn.
- **Highlighting, on the server.** The server highlights the code; a headset never parses it. `tomori-highlight` classifies Rust, Ruby, JavaScript, TypeScript and TSX, Python, JSON, YAML, Markdown, Bash and TOML with tree-sitter, each with its official grammar crate and that grammar's own highlights query, Markdown by its block structure alone; every other language syntect's default syntaxes know, such as C, Go, Java, HTML or SQL, with syntect; and any other, or none, is plain. Both map what they find onto one small set of token kinds, `TokenKind`: keyword, string, comment, number, function, type, variable, operator, punctuation, constant, attribute and plain. Only the first 200 lines are highlighted, up to 8,080 characters of them together, more than are held, so that a line cut where it is held is still highlighted as it continues, within 16 KiB, which they always fit in but for characters of more than two bytes, and 50 ms from when its grammar or syntax is ready, which happens once, after which the rest is plain; the server prepares the grammars on a thread of their own as it starts serving, rather than as the first code panel arrives.
- **Highlighting holds nothing up but its own space.** The server never highlights while it holds the hub's lock, which every space and connection shares, nor on a scheduler thread. It reads a completed reply's blocks under the lock, which is quick, and highlights their code afterwards on a thread of its own, then projects the reply's panels, all together, under the lock again. Only the space's own next agent event waits for it, so that each reply's panels are projected in the order the replies completed, and a later reply's code panels are never overtaken by an earlier one's slow highlighting. One event highlights at most four blocks, each bounded as above, so at most about 200 ms: when one event completes several replies, as reading the session again after a gap may, only the code panels of the last of them with any are highlighted, since those replace the others' as they are projected together, and the others' are projected plain. A code panel whose highlighting cannot start, because the system will not give it a thread, or fails, is projected plain; the panels of a reply that completes as its space stops, while they are highlighted, as when the server shuts down, are not projected.
- **What a client is sent.** The text and its spans, never colours: each span `[start, length, kind]`, in characters of the body, inside one line, in order and none overlapping, at most 2,048. A client draws code whose spans are not valid as plain text, and maps each kind onto the one palette every client shares, drawing coloured text with the glyph atlas, as it draws every panel.
- **Fitting in one frame.** A projection is sent in one frame of at most 1 MiB, which ninety code panels, as many as a space shows, could not all fill with 200 lines each. The server sends every code panel whole where the projection, as JSON, fits in a frame less 4 KiB; where it would not, it sends as many as need be **short**, the least recently put where they are first, so that those most likely being read are whole: a short panel holds its first 16 lines, cut where they reach 720 characters together, with at most 256 spans over them, as much as a slot shows, as code panels held before they scrolled, and keeps its block's length, so that it says how many lines are only in the transcript. A projection of every code panel short at every limit fits in a frame, about 826 KB. It checks cheaply first, from a bound on each panel's JSON, and measures the JSON only where that bound passes the frame's, so that a projection of a few panels costs nothing more. What it keeps of each panel is unchanged, so that a panel sent short is sent whole again once there is room.
- **Clutter.** A reply's code panels replace the code panels of the replies before it, on every wall, but those a participant has pulled off and left floating, put back on a wall or moved onto another, which are pinned where they are; a reply without code panels replaces none. Text panels are never replaced, and persist until dismissed. A block of a single line, such as a command, is not projected, and stays in the transcript: it reads as well there, and agents write many, so a panel for each would push useful panels off the walls and replace the reply before's code panels with a command. At most four code panels are projected from one reply, as at most four text panels are; later blocks stay only in the transcript. A pinned code panel says so on its face and its label.
- **Pointing.** A code panel is pointed at as a text panel is, its label naming its title, its language and how many lines, and when and where it was projected, and attended to as `Resource::panel`, which the server describes from its own store with its language and the lines it holds, as `Ada: [Pointing at code panel "rust" (on the Tomori wall, projected just now; language: "rust"; lines 1–200 of 240)] what does this do?`. How far a participant has scrolled is their own, so it is not said.
- **Speech and the transcript.** Unchanged: speech skips every code block, and the transcript keeps the reply as written.
- **The agent's instructions.** Tomori's layer adds that any other fenced code block of more than one line, with its language after the fence, appears as a code panel on the same wall, highlighted and titled with its language, which the code panels of its next reply with any replace unless a participant moved it; so that it shows code only when seeing it helps, in a few short blocks, and says in a sentence what each is. It also says that a panel goes where whoever asked for it was looking, or else onto the wall nearest them, so that they can see it, and how to place it elsewhere, with a `place` header line in one of the forms above, or a `place=` attribute after a code block's language.

**Image panels.** The agent shows an image, such as a render, a diagram, a screenshot or a preview, with its `show_image` tool (see `mcp.md`), as an image panel, which is laid out, carried, swapped, pinned, thrown away and pointed at as every panel is, and kept until dismissed, as a text panel is: code panels never replace it.

- **The source.** A file on the server's machine, by a path absolute or relative to the space's directory, which is the agent's working directory: any readable regular file, its symbolic links followed, since the agent can already read whatever the server can with its own tools, so confining it to some directories would protect nothing. What the path names is checked before it is opened, so that a device, even one a link points to, is never opened, and the file is opened without waiting and read only so far, so that a pipe or a device put in its place never holds the thread reading it, and is refused as too large or not an image; and refused if missing, a folder, unreadable, over 20 MB, or not a PNG, JPEG, WebP or GIF by its content, whatever its name. Nothing is fetched from the network. A refusal is the tool's result, saying why, so the agent learns of it; the path is at most 4,096 bytes.
- **Made safe on the server.** The server decodes the file with the `image` crate's pure Rust decoders, a GIF's first frame only, within 16,384 pixels on a side, 50 megapixels and 512 MB of memory, so that a small file claiming an enormous image is refused before it is decoded; prepares one image at a time, on a thread of its own, never holding the hub's lock or a scheduler thread; turns it as its EXIF orientation says; lays it over white where it is transparent, since most diagrams and screenshots with transparency are drawn for a light page; scales it down, keeping its shape, to at most 2,048 pixels on its longer side; and encodes it again as a PNG of 8-bit RGB without metadata, halving it until it is at most 8 MB. That PNG is the only format a client decodes. Its title is the agent's, or else the file's name, and its caption the agent's, each cleaned onto one line and cut, the caption at 240 characters.
- **Kept by content.** The server keeps each image as `HASH.png`, named by its SHA-256, in the `images` folder of its state directory, readable only by its user, and the latest 64 MB in memory; an image read again is checked against its name. A panel keeps the image's hash, size and length with the space, never the original path, so that it survives a restart. An image no kept panel shows, in any space and environment, is removed once it is a minute old, as the kept spaces are written, so that one just made for a panel not yet projected is not removed under it.
- **Sent apart, on request, by hash.** A panel names its image, `Panel::image`, by hash, size and length; a client fetches it with `FetchImage` and keeps it by its hash, so that each image crosses once however many panels show it, and a client that reconnects keeps those it has. The server sends an image only to a client whose `Hello` sets `images`, and only one a panel shown in the client's own space has, visitors included: what a client may fetch is what its space shows, and a request for any other, or for one no longer kept, is dropped without an error, since it happens innocently, as when a panel is dismissed while its image is on its way. It sends the image after everything else waiting on the connection, control, agent speech and participant audio, in `Image` frames of at most 32 KiB, one image's in order before the next's, so that an image never holds up anything else for longer than a chunk takes to send; at most 32 images wait for one connection. A client asks for at most two at a time, asks again after 20 seconds, at most three times until it reconnects, checks each image against its hash, dropping one that is not what it says, and keeps the latest 64 MB, never forgetting one shown.
- **Older clients.** A client whose `Hello` does not set `images` is sent each image panel as a text panel of its caption, followed by "*An image, W × H pixels: this headset needs an update to show it.*", and is never sent an `Image` frame, which it would refuse.
- **Drawn.** Its face shows its title, its size, metadata and age, a rule, the image and its caption, below it, on at most two rows in a slot and three floating. In a wall's slot it is a slot's size, the image as large as fits in the rows above its caption, keeping its shape, centred, with space either side or above and below. Floating or carried, it grows to the image's own shape, as large as fits within 48 columns and 30 rows, as wide as its title and metadata, and no narrower than 24 columns. Until its image arrives, the face shows where it will be, a little lighter than the face. The image is drawn over the face, in its plane, as opaque, before any panel in front of it, so that what is nearer still covers it (see `rendering.md`).
- **Sharp, steady and bounded on the headset.** Each image is decoded on a thread of the renderer's own, never on a frame, with every mipmap level made in linear light, each pixel the average of all it covers in the level above, so that it neither darkens as it shrinks nor shimmers far away, nor loses its right or bottom edge where a level's width or height is odd, and uploaded, at most one a frame, as an sRGB texture, sampled trilinearly and anisotropically; into a target that is not sRGB, as stored. One texture serves every panel showing the image. Textures are kept within 128 MB: one no panel has shown for about ten seconds, 900 frames, is let go, as a dismissed panel's is; when a new one does not fit, those not shown this frame go first, the least recently shown first, and then the new one is uploaded without its largest levels, down to 256 pixels on its longer side, and otherwise waits, decoded, for room, its panel showing where it will be. The image is sent at its own size, up to 2,048 pixels, whatever the slot, and is never cut down otherwise, so that floating, carried or grown it stays sharp; but in a wall's slot it is drawn far smaller than that: in a 1×1 wall's slot at scale 1.6, 1.5 m away, a 960 × 1440 image is about 132 × 197 of a Quest 3's eye's pixels, at about 13 pixels per degree, and floating 0.7 m away about 466 × 700, a tall image narrower than a wide one. Its detail is then averaged, not lost by aliasing or drawn as blocks, and what is finer than the eye's pixels, such as a render's small text, cannot be read however sharp the image is; `show_image`'s description therefore asks the agent to crop to what matters and keep text large (see `mcp.md`).
- **Pointing.** An image panel is pointed at as a text panel is, its label naming its size, and attended to as `Resource::panel`, which the server describes from its own store with its size and caption, as `Ada: [Pointing at image panel "Session browser" (on the Tomori wall, projected just now; 1600 × 900 pixels; caption: "The layout, rendered")] make the folders narrower`.

**Highlighting stays on the server.** No client builds tree-sitter, its grammars, syntect or `tomori-highlight`: `tomori-server` highlights with its default `highlight` feature, and the clients' tests build it without, as `bake/tests/dependencies.rs` checks for every client crate, every feature and every target, the headset's included. In November 2026 the grammars and syntect's syntaxes added 11.4 MB to the `tomori-server` release binary, from 52.7 to 64.1 MB, and about 15 seconds of compilation to its first build, most of it in parallel with the rest, and nothing to any client or the APK.

**The protocol.** Projection has messages of its own rather than the environment state channel, which belongs to one environment's binding and is forgotten when the space changes environment, and cannot yet keep state across restarts (#202):

- `ServerMessage::Projection` carries the space's projection as seen in its current environment, complete each time: the Tomori wall first, then the environment's walls and the added ones, and the panels on them or floating. It is sent on joining, after the space's state, and whenever it changes, coalesced so that a burst of changes sends one, only to a client whose `Hello` sets `projection`, so an older client, which would refuse a message it does not know, is never sent it.
- `SpaceState::projection` says the server projects, and takes `AddWall` and `RemoveWall` from owners and `PlacePanel`, onto a wall, in the `slot` it names, or floating at a pose, and `DismissPanel` from participants. A `slot` past a wall's last is refused. A client sends them only to such a server, so an older server is never sent them. The server checks each against its own store and refuses one it cannot do, saying why.
- `Wall` says its `grid`, as `[columns, rows]`, and `scale` only where they are not its shape's own, so that a wall nobody adjusted is sent as before; an older client, which knows neither, draws its shape's grid and scale, and no panel in a slot past them. It says its slots' `aspect` only where they are not a standard slot's shape; an older client, which knows none, draws standard slots at its `scale`, so that each panel is drawn at its text's size, as a standard panel smaller than its slot, and the wall smaller than newer clients draw it. `SetWall` says an `aspect` to change it, `0` for a standard slot's, which an older server ignores, leaving the slots their shape. `SpaceState::walls` says the server takes `SetWall` from participants and leaves a removed wall's panels floating; a client offers adjusting walls only to such a server, and an older server, which says nothing of it, is never sent `SetWall`. A slot past the last of a wall's grid is refused. These are additions an older client or server ignores, so the protocol version does not change.
- The new types, `Projection`, `Wall`, `WallKind`, `WallShape`, `Panel`, `Place` and `Meta`, are non-exhaustive, with constructors, and `WallKind` and `Place` read a newer server's values as `Unknown`, which a client does not draw. `WallShape` reads a newer shape as `Unknown`, and a wall without a shape, as an older server sends, as a flat grid; a client draws either flat, as it does an arc too tight for its slots.
- A code panel is a `Panel` with `code`, a `Code` of its language, how many lines its block has and its `Span`s, each of a `TokenKind`, which reads a newer server's kinds as `Plain`; `Panel::pinned` says a participant has put it where it is. An older client, which knows neither, draws a code panel as a text panel of its code. `CODE_LINES`, `CODE_CHARS` and `CODE_SPANS`, 200 lines, 8,000 characters and 2,048 spans, bound it, and `valid_spans` checks its spans. An older client, which took at most 16 lines and 256 spans, draws a code panel's first 16 lines as before, but plain where it has more than 256 spans.

- `ClientMessage::CheckPanelItem { panel, line, checked, revision }` ticks or unticks a checklist item, from participants only, sent only to a server whose `SpaceState::checks` says it takes it, which an older client ignores and an older server never sends; its `revision`, the panel's that the client saw, is absent from an older client. `Panel::revision` is the panel's revision, absent, and read as 0, from an older server. `ServerMessage::Error::panel` names the panel of a refused tick, absent for other errors and ignored by older clients. `tomori_protocol::checklist` reads a body's items and ticks one, the same rule for the server and every client.
- `Panel::slug` is the agent's id for a text panel, left out where it has none, and `Resource::Panel`'s `line`, made with `Resource::panel_at`, the line of the body the item pointed at begins on, left out elsewhere; an older client ignores `slug`, and an older server `line`, as each ignores a field it does not know.
- A wall of a group says its `group`, the identity of its group's first wall, and its `joined` edges, each a `Join` of an `Edge` and a wall, left out for a wall alone, so that an older client, which knows neither, draws each wall alone, its board whole. `SpaceState::groups` says the server snaps walls; a client offers snapping and carries a group only for such a server, and then sends `ClientMessage::AttachWall { wall, attach, alone, pose }`, which attaches `wall` across `attach.edge` of `attach.to`, its group with it unless `alone`, or, without `attach`, takes it out of its group, standing at `pose`. An older server, which says nothing of groups, is never sent `AttachWall`, and the walls it keeps are read where they stood. `Edge` and `Align` read a newer peer's values as `Unknown`, which the server refuses in a message and forgets in what it keeps.
- An image panel is a `Panel` with `image`, an `Image` of its SHA-256 as 64 lower-case hexadecimal digits, its width, height and length, which `Image::is_valid` bounds by `IMAGE_SIDE` and `IMAGE_BYTES`, and which a client fetches only if it is valid. `Hello::images` says a client shows image panels; it then sends `ClientMessage::FetchImage` with an image's hash, and reads frames of a third kind, `FrameKind::Image`, each an `ImageChunk`: the image's hash, where the chunk starts, the image's length, each a little-endian `u32`, and at most `IMAGE_CHUNK_BYTES` of it, which a client refuses if it reaches past its image or its image is larger than `IMAGE_BYTES`. None of these changes the protocol version: an older client neither says `images` nor is sent an image frame, and an older server sends no image panel to fetch from.

**Not in this slice.** The other content formats, such as charts, images from the network, an image panel in a `panel` block, animated images and zooming into an image, items as #82's general model, with history or merging of concurrent changes, scaling, rotating, pinning other than of code panels, grouping and attaching to anchors, scrolling shared between participants, selecting lines or spans of one (#147), a time-to-live, a control to clear the agent's content, refusals reported to the agent, MCP tools beyond `move_panel`, `panels` and `update_panel` (with #100), panels in the spectator and the console, panels a participant creates, and ticking an item with the trigger, which keeps attracting the swarm.

**Editing walls on the headset.** A participant who may adjust walls, in a space whose server takes wall changes, edits a wall with the right grip, as they carry a panel:

- **Taking hold.** Squeezing the grip while the ray meets a wall's board, one of its slots without a panel, or its bar, nearer than anything else the grip would take, takes hold of the wall: a panel the ray meets takes the squeeze first, as it is in front of its slot, and a card nearer than either takes it, as ever. A wall taken hold of shows its handles, and a hint of its controls above it, until B, a squeeze at nothing, or taking hold of another wall lets go of it; it is let go too when it is no longer shown, or the participant may no longer adjust it, as on leaving the space.
- **The bar.** Every wall shown has a grab bar, and a group of walls snapped together one, above its top left wall (see below), after the bar below a window in the Quest's own interface, so that a wall whose every slot holds a panel, curved or not, is as easy to take hold of as an empty one: a thin bar, about 0.8° thick, drawn subtly and highlighted while the ray meets it or its wall is carried, at the middle of the wall's top edge, a handle's outset above it, facing the way the wall faces there, or, for the Tomori wall, whose top the status pill stands just above from home, as far below its label. It is a third of the wall long, but never shorter than three handles nor longer than five, as seen at the wall's middle, and is met as a handle is, by the ray passing within 0.8 of a handle's size of it, at least about 2.7° across as seen; the grip on it takes hold of the wall exactly as on its board, carrying a wall someone added, and the ray ends on it. It stands a handle's spacing, as seen, from every handle, shorter, then a row further out, where they would come nearer, whether the wall is held or not, so that it is never confused with a handle and stays where it was as the wall is taken hold of: held, a wall is carried again by its bar, and where the bar stands above it, the knob and the column steppers stand a row beyond it. While the ray meets the bar of a wall not held, a label beside it says what the grip does, `Grip  carry and edit this wall` for a wall someone added and `Grip  edit this wall` otherwise, or `G` in the desktop window. A panel the ray meets nearer than a bar takes the grip, and a bar nearer than a panel is pointed at in its place, as a handle is. Bars show only to a participant who may adjust walls, the clutter being worth nothing to anyone else, and, as a wall's board and handles are, a bar is Tomori's own interface where an environment's client side may take the ray (#233).
- **Carrying an added wall.** A wall someone added, or, where the server locks walls, an environment's own wall that is unlocked, is carried while the grip is held, as a panel is: the point it was taken hold of stays on the ray, at the distance it was taken from, and it keeps facing the way it faced, and its tilt, so that taking hold of it moves nothing; the right stick pushes it further or pulls it closer, from 0.3 m to 5 m, and turns it 10° a flick about the vertical, and its click turns it square to the participant. Let go, its new pose is sent, unless it is where it was. The Tomori wall and an environment's walls stand where Tomori or the environment puts them, so they are taken hold of but not carried.
- **Handles.** Small square handles stand just beyond the wall's edges, facing the participant, each about 1.7° across from the eyes at the wall's farthest corner, and larger nearer, so that they are as easy to hold with the ray near and far. **No two overlap:** each handle's middle stands at least 1.8 handles from every other's as the participant sees it, leaving a gap of most of a handle, about 1.4°, between them, whatever the wall's grid, size, shape of slot or curve, from about a metre to across the space and from the side, so long as the wall is within view as its middle is looked at. Spacing is measured as seen, longer along the wall where it is seen obliquely, and a curved wall's handles follow its arc, continuing along its last direction beyond its edges; where an edge has no room for its handles, the knob and steppers move along it, then to a row or column of their own beyond it, and where a wall so curved that it folds as seen still brings two together, the one that is not a corner moves on beyond its edge until none does. **The nearest is taken:** the ray meets a handle it passes within 0.8 of a handle's size of, never as far as halfway to a neighbour, and of those it meets, the one whose middle it passes nearest, as seen from where it begins; it highlights it, ticks lightly and ends on it, and the grip holds it while held. The ray meets a handle before a panel or board behind it. The handles are:
  - **corners**, which size and shape the wall's slots: dragged in its plane, each slot's width follows how far across from the wall's middle the ray is against where it took hold, and its height how far up or down, apart, each as the square of that, so that dragging down makes tall slots and across wide ones, and twice as far makes them four times as large: from a standard slot to the largest, five times as large, is one drag to a little over twice as far from the middle, and back is one drag to under half as far. The slots' text is as large as the narrower of the two allows against a standard slot, in steps of 5%, from half to five times its size, and their shape, from 1:4 to 4:1, snaps to 1:1, 4:3, 3:4, 16:9, 9:16 or a standard slot's within about 3.5% of it, and is otherwise kept to a hundredth;
  - **its right edge**, `↔`, which gives it as many columns as put its right edge nearest the ray, and **its bottom edge**, `↕`, as many rows, a whole slot at a time, the wall growing about its middle;
  - **steppers**, `-` and `+`, along its top edge towards its right corner for a column fewer or more and down its right edge towards its bottom corner for a row, which change it at once, a squeeze each;
  - **a knob**, `●`, above the middle of its top edge, a row beyond its bar where the bar stands above the wall, which bends it, measured from where it was taken hold of: the hand moved down, the wall curves around the participant, and moved back up towards where it was taken, and beyond, it straightens, its curvature, one over its radius, changing evenly with the distance, from flat to as tightly as the wall may curve, never around more than half a circle nor tighter than 1 m, over **the wall's own height**, within 20 cm and 1 m: one movement as tall as the wall, down, bends a flat wall fully, and back up as far flattens it, never beyond either end, where it stops with a tick. Within 1 cm of where it was taken, up or down, the wall is as it was, so that a hand's drift and shake change nothing, and beyond it the curvature follows the movement past that centimetre, without a jump; bending less than 6% of the most it may, it is flat, unless it was bent less than twice that to begin with; and moving the hand across or towards the wall bends nothing. The radius is a whole number of centimetres. In the desktop window, whose ray begins at the eyes, dragging the knob down bends the wall, and up straightens it;
  - **a hinge**, a level line with the wall's face tilted across it, drawn as shapes, beside the middle of its left edge, on the line the wall tilts about, so that it stays under the ray as a flat wall tilts, or a column beyond the edge where the left corners leave it no room, which tilts it, measured from where it was taken hold of: the hand moved down, the wall's top comes towards the participant, so that it faces down, and moved up, it tilts back, evenly, from 45° one way to 45° the other over the wall's own height, within 20 cm and 1 m, as the knob does, so that one movement as tall as the wall takes it from one end to the other, and half of one from upright to either, stopping at each with a tick. Within 1 cm of where it was taken, it is as it was; within 3° of upright, it is upright, with a tick, so that upright is always exactly reachable; and moving the hand across or towards the wall tilts nothing. The tilt is a whole number of degrees. In the desktop window, dragging it down tilts the wall down.
  
  A locked wall shows none of these, the hinge included, but its padlock (see the padlock, below); a wall in a group, which stands upright, shows no hinge.
  
  The Tomori wall has only its right edge, the column steppers, the knob and the hinge, since it stays one row of at most six at its own size and shape. A change the server would refuse is not shown. While a handle is held, the wall is drawn as it would be, its panels with it, and in place of the hint, what it makes of the wall: a corner its slots' size and shape, such as `60 × 107 cm · 9:16`, the knob its radius, such as `◠ radius 2.4 m`, or `flat`, the hinge its tilt, such as `tilted 12° down`, `tilted 8° up` or `upright`, and an edge its grid, such as `4 × 2 slots`; let go, the change is sent, and shown so until the server's projection agrees or for 3 seconds.
- **The padlock.** Where the server locks walls (`SpaceState::wall_locks`), a wall taken hold of shows its padlock, a handle as the others are, a row beyond its top left corner, level with the knob, drawn of shapes: closed and warm while the wall is locked, open and bright while it is not. A locked wall shows its padlock alone, no other handle, is carried by neither its board nor its bar, and its hint says only `Grip lock  unlock it to change it`, for an owner, or `Locked  an owner can unlock it`, for anyone else, and `B done`; while the ray meets its bar, the bar's label says `locked: take hold to unlock it`, or `locked by an owner`, so that someone trying to move a wall that will not move is told why where they try. An owner's squeeze on the padlock unlocks the wall, for everyone, shown unlocked at once with every handle back, and, on an unlocked wall, locks it again, as it is; anyone else's squeeze on it does nothing. Beside the padlock of an environment's own wall unlocked, a row beyond it, an owner has `↺`, whose squeeze puts the wall back where and as the environment has it, locked, and lets go of it, since it then stands where the server says. The hint of an unlocked wall adds, for an owner, `Grip lock  lock it as it is` and, for an environment's own, `Grip ↺  put back, locked`. The padlock is squeezed with the grip, as every handle and stepper is, rather than pressed with A or the trigger, so that the grip means take hold, and changes a wall, everywhere on a wall.
- **Removing.** For an owner, a press of B while carrying an added wall, let go within 0.4 seconds while it is still carried, removes it, for everyone, as throwing a panel away does; when it holds panels, the first press only asks, the hint saying, in the warm colour, that B again removes it and how many panels float, and a second press within 4 seconds removes it, its panels floating where they were. A press of B is the wall's while it is carried, as it is a panel's, and never leaves the space nor backs out of anything else. A collaborator's B removes nothing, and their hint does not offer it.
- **B lets go.** With a wall taken hold of and nothing carried, B lets go of it, and backs out of nothing else.
- **The hint.** A wall taken hold of shows, above it and its handles, clear of the status pill, at the angle the hint beside a panel carried is drawn, what each control does: `Grip ↔ ↕` more or fewer slots, `Grip corner` size and shape, but for the Tomori wall, `Grip - +` one fewer or more, `Grip ●` move down to bend, `Grip hinge` move down to tilt, or, in the desktop window, drag down to bend and to tilt, `Grip bar` carry it, for an added wall, and `B` done; while it is carried, `Stick ↕` further or closer, `Stick ↔` turn, `B` remove, for all, for an owner, and `Let go` leave it here. The desktop window names its keys instead, as it does for a panel carried: `G`, `PgUp PgDn`, `[ ]`, `Backspace` and `Let go G`.
- **Visitors**, and participants where the server takes no wall changes, take hold of no wall, and see no bars.
- **Older servers and clients.** A wall says it is locked with `Wall::locked`, sent only where it is, and a server that locks walls says so with `SpaceState::wall_locks` and takes `LockWall` from owners. A client shows the padlock, and carries an environment's own wall, only where the server says it locks walls, so that against an older server nothing changes; an older client, which knows no lock, shows a locked wall's handles, and the server refuses what they ask, saying why, and sends the wall again as it is.

**Snapping walls together.** Walls stay simple rectangles and arcs, with no merged cells or internal seams within one wall; larger arrangements are composed by snapping several walls someone added together, so that a 2×2 wall and a large 1×1 wall beside it read as one piece. Where walls stand together is `tomori_protocol::group`, which the server and every client share, as they share `tomori_protocol::wall`:

- **Which walls snap.** Only walls someone added, in the environment the space is in: the Tomori wall and an environment's own walls stand where Tomori or the environment puts them, so they neither snap nor are snapped to, and a wall locked in place (`Wall::locked`) is no target either, nor snaps to anything, refused where `tomori_protocol::group::snaps_to` and `snap_within` decide, which the server and every client share. Both flat, or both curved, whatever their radii; a flat wall and a curved one never snap.
- **Snapping while carrying is magnetic.** While a participant who may adjust walls carries a wall, in a space whose server snaps them, a wall carried, or a wall of its group carried with it, snaps to another when it is within reach of where it would stand against that wall's facing edge, the two meeting along it, or within reach of it: one of its edges within 30 cm of there across the edge, or as far as 6° seen from the participant where that is more, so that a far wall is as easy as a near one; within 25 cm in front of or behind the other's surface, less than a firm pull takes a wall out of its group; turned from facing as the other's surface faces there by up to 30° about the vertical, three of the stick's 10° turns, and tilted by up to 15°. It need not be lined up first: where it would stand is outlined lined up with the other, each of its slots framed brightly and faintly filled at the pose it will take, as a slot is for a panel carried, so that the participant sees it straighten, with a tick as one is shown or another takes its place; the hint says `Let go  snap it there` and, above it, `Hold trigger  don't snap`, or `Hold click` in the desktop window. Let go, it is sent to the server, and drawn there at once, exactly where it was outlined, until the server agrees or for 3 seconds.
- **Which snap is shown.** Of every wall carried against every other wall, and of each other wall's edges, the one that ranks first: the nearest, by how far the wall carried would move across, along and out of the surface, ranked a little further for each degree it would turn, 0.2 m a radian, and 5 cm further across a top or bottom than a side, so that walls go side by side unless one is clearly above or below the other. A snap shown is held until it is 25% beyond each limit, and ranks 20% nearer against another, so that the outline neither flickers at the edge of reach nor between two neighbours. A wall pulled out of its group snaps back where it was only once pushed back within 10 cm of the surface, measured against where the walls it left stand, so that the pull is not undone at once; elsewhere it snaps as any wall does.
- **Placing a wall near another without snapping.** Holding the trigger while carrying a wall, the left mouse button in the desktop window, shows no snap and attracts no swarm, so that let go it stays exactly where it is, beside another or not; letting go of the trigger while still carrying it snaps as before.
- **Where a wall snaps.** Against the other's edge, `left`, `right`, `top` or `bottom`, the gap between their slots one gutter at the larger of their two scales, a flat wall's gutter or a curved wall's gap, so that spacing is even across the join; aligned along it at its `start`, its top beside a side or its left above or below, its `middle`, or its `end`, whichever is nearest where it was let go, its top first where two are as near, as two walls as tall are; lined up with it, in its plane facing as it faces; and on an arc, the arc continued, about the same line, at the other's radius, which a curved wall snapped to it takes, so that the group reads as one curve, unless that would take it round more than half a circle, when it does not snap.
- **Groups.** Walls snapped together are a group, kept by the server with the space: each wall but the group's first is kept attached to another, across that wall's edge and aligned along it, `Attach { to, edge, align }`, its pose following from the first wall's, and kept as it stands too, so that a server that knows no groups reads every wall where it stood. So a group stays joined as its walls change: a wall given more columns, rows or larger slots pushes the walls attached beyond it along, and bending one wall bends every wall of its group alike, along one arc, while each keeps its own grid and slots. A curved wall of a group takes exactly the radius of the wall it is attached to, whatever its slots' size, so that every wall of a curved group stands on one arc about one line. Carrying any of its walls carries the group, every wall keeping where it stood from it, and the server moves the group as the wall carried moves. A wall snapped to another brings its own group with it, the two becoming one group; snapped to a wall of its own group, it comes alone.
- **Taking a wall out.** Pulled firmly towards the participant while carried, more than 30 cm nearer than it was taken hold of, with the right stick or the desktop window's Page Down, a wall comes out of its group alone, with a tick, the rest returning to where they stand, and the hint, while it is still in its group, says `Stick ↓ far  take it out alone`, or `PgDn far`; let go, it leaves its group where it is, or snaps where it is outlined. Taken out, or removed, a wall's neighbours stand where they are and stay together, attached to another wall of the group they still touch as they touched it, or else as groups of their own.
- **What the server checks.** The server, not the client, decides: both walls someone added, in this environment of this space, not the same wall, an edge and an alignment it knows, every wall of the group within 20 m of the space's middle, all flat or all curved, each curved wall then bent to its group's radius without going round more than half a circle, and no two walls' slots reaching into each other, where either has changed, and no locked wall attached, detached or moved, nor any wall of a group a locked wall of which would move; otherwise it refuses, saying why, and nothing moves. A group's walls count towards the six added in an environment and the 78 slots shown, as walls do. Participants snap and take out walls as they move them; a visitor does neither.
- **Drawing a group.** The server sends with each wall of a group of more than one its group, named by its first wall, and which of its edges are joined to which of the group's walls, as `tomori_protocol::group::joined` finds them, standing edge to edge a gap apart. A client draws a flat wall of a group edge by edge, as quads: its translucent board reaches, across a joined edge, only to the seam halfway across the gap between their slots, so that two boards meet without overlapping, and elsewhere as far as the margins of the group's largest wall, so that the group's outer edge is even; its border is drawn only along what no board of its group meets, so that the union reads as one board; and its group's label is shown once, on its top left wall, or, curved, as a curved wall's label is below its slots, its bottom left. An edge the server says is joined is drawn joined only while the wall across it still stands there, as the client draws it.
- **A group's controls cover none of its slots.** A group shows one grab bar, its top left wall's, whose slots reach highest, by a centimetre, then leftmost, as `bears_bar` in `tomori-scene`'s `projection/boards.rs` chooses it, standing above that wall as a wall's bar does, so that it stands above every wall of the group; its other walls show none. It carries the whole group, as any of its walls does, and a wall of the group is still taken hold of alone by its board or an empty slot, to be edited or pulled out. A wall of a group taken hold of keeps its handles off what another wall of the group shows: those along an edge another wall of its group is joined across stand along the opposite edge, the knob and the column steppers a row below the handle of its rows, the handle of its columns and the row steppers along its left, and the handle of its rows a row beyond its knob; where the opposite edge is joined too, they are left out, as is a corner beside a joined edge; and any still standing over another wall's slots, as large as it is drawn, is left out, but for the handle being dragged. The bar is chosen clear of the group's slots as thick as it is drawn, and the hint stands above the group's slots and its bar. Handles and the bar are sized by the angle they take, so that far away they are large: since each is placed, or left out, by where it would stand at that size, none covers a slot at any distance or scale, and a wall that has lost a handle to its neighbours is edited from nearer, or pulled out first. A lock control on a wall, once walls have one, is to keep clear of a group's slots by the same rule; a wall in a group has no hinge, since a group's walls stand upright.
- **The agent snaps walls.** `set_wall` attaches a wall someone added to another, `attach: { to, edge, align }`, alone, after any other change it asks for, or takes it out of its group with `detach`; `presence` names each wall's group and the walls joined to it, across which edge (see `mcp.md`).

**Layouts the agent composes.** When someone asks the agent to visualise or lay out information, it builds a layout of walls in one step with `create_layout` (see `mcp.md`), and fills it:

- **What it asks for.** From one to six walls, as many as an environment takes added, each with the agent's own id for it, a slug as a panel's id is, and laid out as `add_wall` lays a wall out, its columns and rows, its slots' size as a scale, a width in metres or their width and height, their shape and its curve; every wall after the first attached to an earlier one by the agent's id, across its `left`, `right`, `top` or `bottom` edge, aligned at its `start`, `middle` or `end`, by default its start, and built as one snapped group, as a participant's snapping builds one. A wall attached without a size of its own fills the edge it is attached to, as far along it as the other's slots reach, within the bounds of a slot's size, so that a big 1×1 beside a 2×2 is as tall as it; and each wall is curved as the first is, unless it says otherwise, a layout's walls being all flat or all curved at one radius.
- **Where it stands.** `place`, `looking`, by default, `front` or `near`, with a name or for whoever asked, as a panel's are: the layout's middle stands in front of that person, upright and facing them, level with where they face a little below their eyes, or, `looking`, as high as they look, from 0.8 m to 0.6 m above their eyes; a flat layout as far away as it takes to be seen whole within about 60°, at least 1.5 m, and a curved one at its radius, so that its arc curves about them, either no further than 3.5 m. It must be **readable**: every slot within 4 m of their eyes for a standard slot's text, the reading distance panels go by, and as much nearer or further as its text is smaller or larger, so that a slot at half the size is read within 2 m. Where it is not, or where it would overlap a wall already there, it comes nearer, to 2.4, 2, 1.6 and then 1.2 m, and is refused where it is readable nowhere. `wall WALL` places no layout.
- **All of it or nothing.** The whole layout is checked first, built apart from the space and then where it stands, and refused whole, saying why, as for an attachment to no earlier wall, two walls of one id, walls of different shapes, an edge it does not know, beyond the walls an environment takes or the slots it shows, overlapping a wall already there wherever it would stand, or readable nowhere; nothing is built until all of it is.
- **What it says.** Its result names each wall, the agent's id, its identity and its name, such as `added wall 2`, its grid, its slots, numbered from 0, their size and its own, and asks the agent to fill each slot it should hold, with a panel block's `place: wall NAME slot N`, a code block's `place="wall NAME slot N"` or `show_image`'s `place`, and to tell everyone what it built; a notice titled "Walls" on the Tomori wall says it, as for its other changes to walls, and it is logged and kept.
- **Taking it down.** `remove_layout` removes every wall of the group of a wall it names, as `remove_wall` names one, each wall's panels floating where they were, and says how many.
- **Its instructions.** Tomori's layer says that when someone asks it to visualise or lay out information it builds a layout with `create_layout`, with as few walls as the information needs, fills each slot it lists with `place: wall NAME slot N`, and says what it built; `tomori-tools` says what `create_layout` and `remove_layout` do.

**Ticking an item with A.** A participant ticks or unticks a checklist item themselves, for everyone, by pointing at it and pressing **A**, or, in the desktop window, pointing the mouse at it and pressing **Enter**, A's key:

- **Which items.** A task item, one with a box, `- [ ]` or `- [x]`, on any text panel, whoever projected it, pointed at on any row of its text as attention finds it (above), on a panel pointed at rather than carried. An item without a box, the panel's title, a line of no item, a code panel and an image panel tick nothing. It is A's rung after the right wrist's transcript and the list, as `interaction.md` orders it; the trigger still attracts the swarm.
- **Who.** Participants, owners and collaborators, as they rearrange panels; a visitor only watches, so their A is refused, saying so, and the server refuses their message too.
- **The message.** The client sends `CheckPanelItem { panel, line, checked }`, the line of the body the item begins on, as attention names it, and whether it is to be ticked, the opposite of what it shows, only to a server whose `SpaceState::checks` says it takes it. It shows the change at once, ticking its own copy by the same rule, until the server's projection says how it is. Saying `checked`, rather than toggling, means two people ticking an item at once both leave it ticked, though the second, from a revision the first changed, is refused and cued. It also says the panel's revision the server last sent, where it sends one, and never counts its own tick in it: a guess at the revision its tick makes could equal that of another change the tick raced, such as the agent's rewrite, and so let a second tick land on another item. A second tick before the projection comes back is therefore refused, for the participant to tick again once it shows.
- **On the server.** The server checks the panel is a text panel shown in the space's current environment, that it is at the revision the tick says, where it says one, and that the line begins an item with a box, as `update_panel` reads items, ticks it there in place, keeping the panel's identity, wall and slot or where it floats, and when the agent last updated it, since a participant's tick is not the agent's update, and sends the projection to everyone. An item already so changes nothing; one refused says why, naming the panel in its error, and either sends the projection again, so that the client that showed it at once shows it as it is.
- **A tick racing a change.** A tick whose revision the panel has changed since, as when the agent rewrote the body, reordering, adding or removing items, or another participant ticked an item, is refused, saying `That panel changed as you ticked it; look again.`, rather than ticking whatever item is on that line now. The participant's client takes the error naming the panel as a cue, not a problem to show, and the projection sent again undoes its tick at once and shows the panel as it is, so that they look again and tick the item where it is now. A refused tick reaches no note to the agent. **An older client**, which says no revision, ticks by its line alone, as before: its tick racing a change of the body may still land on whichever item begins on that line by then, a residual race that ends once it says the revision it saw.
- **Telling the agent.** The agent is not given a turn for it, so that it does not reply to every tick: `panels` shows it at once, and the space keeps a note of it, the latest for each item, at most eight, told in a block of its own before the next words spoken in the space, whoever speaks them, after the speaker's name and before the note of which message they answer and what they point at (see `spaces.md`), as `Bob: [Ada ticked item 2 of 5 of panel "Agenda" (id agenda): "Panel ids"; Ada unticked item 1 of 5 of panel "Agenda" (id agenda): "Pairing window"] [Pointing at …] next one`. Names, titles and texts are cleaned as the context block's descriptions are, and the block keeps the latest notes within the context block's 1,000 characters. Notes are kept only in memory. The agent's instructions say that participants tick items so, and to take one as done without remarking on it unless it matters.
- **Saying so.** The label on the ray, while it meets such an item, ends with how to tick it, `A  tick`, or `A  untick` for an item ticked, `Enter` in the desktop window, after how to scroll a panel that scrolls, for a participant in a space whose server takes ticking.
- **Feedback.** A that ticks or unticks an item clicks and taps as choosing does (see `feedback.md`); one the client refuses, as a visitor's, does not. One the server refuses, as one racing a change, is cued as not done, with two falling notes and two taps on the right hand, when its error arrives.

**Environment-specific content, later.** Generic content, text and later charts, may stand on any wall. Content types an environment defines will stand only on that environment's walls, and be hidden with it. Whether moving such content onto the Tomori wall degrades it to a text summary is an open question on #82.

## Rationale

The maintainer asked, in November 2026, for a simple first slice of #82 that shows how projection looks and works, without overfitting for later content types: projection walls with text panels, created from fenced blocks in the agent's replies, which participants rearrange and point at. The maintainer then refined it: projection is spatially registered, and environments differ in shape, so the space has one Tomori wall that travels with Tomori across environments, while each environment's walls and the panels left floating in it are kept for that environment, hidden and restored with it.

Fenced blocks are what agents already write, and keep a reply useful in the transcript as well as in the space; projecting when the reply completes means a block is never projected half written. The nearest wall to whoever asked puts the answer in front of them, as #82's section on presentation suggests, and capturing where they stood as their words begin follows how attention is captured (see `spaces.md`). A calm layout, where nothing moves but the panel that leaves, follows the maintainer's brief that panels must not shuffle while someone reads them; dropping the panel there longest, rather than showing "+N more", keeps a wall from growing a second page nobody can reach, and its text stays in the transcript. Visitors see panels because they see the transcript, which holds the same text; they change nothing, as they change nothing else.

Code panels were agreed with the maintainer in November 2026, after the text panels, as the first part of #147's shared code toolkit: agents already write code in fenced blocks, and code is far better read than heard. Highlighting on the server keeps headsets running only Tomori's own fixed renderers on bounded declarative data, as the trust boundary in #82 requires, and keeps the grammars out of the APK; tree-sitter for the languages Tomori is used with most gives the most faithful classification, and syntect covers the long tail without a grammar each. Kinds rather than colours let every client share one palette and change it without the server, and let both highlighters agree. Replacing the last reply's code panels, unless someone pinned one, keeps a conversation about code from filling the walls, while what someone chose to keep stays; one-line blocks stay in the transcript because they are mostly commands, which would otherwise crowd out and replace the code being discussed. tree-sitter-highlight does not yet layer Markdown's inline grammar, or a fenced block's own language, correctly over its block grammar, so Markdown is highlighted by its block structure alone.

The open questions of #82 were decided for this slice as follows: panels persist until dismissed, with no time-to-live yet; they are kept in a store of the stage's own, in the space's record, with a schema version, rather than on the state channel, which cannot yet keep state across restarts or across a change of environment, and could adopt #202's persistence and visibility once it has them; visitors may see every panel; there is no "clear agent content" control yet, and a dismissal cannot be undone; fenced blocks are projected when the reply completes, and a refused block is left in the transcript and not reported to the agent; and participants rearrange panels but do not create them.

The maintainer, trying the first slice on a Quest 3 in November 2026, asked for a preview of where a panel carried will snap, for a panel dropped on a wall to go into the slot under the ray, and for panels a participant places to stay where they are put. The ray decides first because it is what the participant is looking along and the preview shows its choice; the panel's own nearness decides only when the ray chooses none, as while a teleport is aimed. Dropped on another panel, the two change places rather than the newcomer being inserted before it: inserting would shift every panel after it, which is exactly the shuffling the layout avoids, while a swap moves only the one panel, to where the participant's own panel came from, so nothing else is disturbed and the participant can undo it by swapping back. Placing by hand marks a panel arranged, so that the agent's new panels take free slots and then the oldest of its own, and only when a participant has arranged a whole wall does their oldest go. Nothing is pinned yet, as pinning is not in this slice; when it is, a pinned panel is to leave a full wall last of all, and a wall of pinned panels to refuse a newcomer, which then goes to the Tomori wall. The maintainer also found that the Tomori wall, front right of home, stood in front of the first workers' stations, and asked for a curved wall below the swarm, one row of six slots facing home, clear of the stations and the swarm in every environment; its slots are smaller than a flat wall's since they stand nearer, 2.3 m rather than about 3, so that six fit within the 40° either side of straight ahead that the stations leave clear, with their text as large to the eye. It stands below the status pill, since both are low in the view and the pill is drawn over what is behind it. Finally, the maintainer asked for a panel pulled off to grow to show all of its text, and to return to a slot's size on a wall: a wall's slots are a fixed size so that its layout is calm, while a panel in the hand is being read.

In November 2026, speaking from the headset, the maintainer was very happy with carrying and placing panels, and asked for three refinements for someone using it for the first time. First, they worried that mashing buttons would dismiss panels by accident, since a quick press of B while merely pointing at one took it away for everyone, and chose that a panel is dismissed only by pressing B while carrying it with the grip: carrying is deliberate, and the participant is looking at the panel they are about to throw away. B on its own then never dismisses one, and still backs out of everything else and leaves the space when held; a press of B while carrying is the panel's alone, as the right stick's push is, so that it neither backs out of something else nor, held, leaves the space with a panel in the hand, and a hold is a change of mind. Second, they asked for a hint beside a panel carried, saying what each control does, so that a first-time participant learns the stick, its click, B and letting go where they need them; it is sized by angle, since a panel is carried from 0.3 m to 5 m, and says what letting go would do, since that changes with where the panel is. A hint that shortens once each control has been used a few times was considered, but needs what a headset has learned kept on it, so it is left for later. Third, they asked for long lines in code panels to wrap rather than run off the panel's edge: the maintainer and the coordinator chose soft wrapping with a hanging indent over wider panels, since the text-sharpness work showed that width is limited by comfortable reading, about 34 to 57 characters a metre at 1.5 to 2.5 m, and 80 characters would span about 54°; the hanging indent and the marker keep a wrapped line reading as one, and code panels keep their slot's size, so the walls stay calm.

Looking at a Rust code panel on the Quest 3 after wrapping arrived, the maintainer found its lines wrapped well, but one, `let entry = totals.entry(customer.clone()).or_insert((0, 0.0));`, ended at `or_insert((0,` with "…", and asked whether that was deliberate and whether a line could wrap onto more than one row. It could, and the headset wrapped whatever it was sent; the "…" was the server's, which still cut every line to 64 characters, a bound chosen when code panels drew each line on one row and kept when they began to wrap. The bound now falls on the lines together, at what the panel could show of them, rather than on each line: a bound is still needed, since the code is untrusted, but one on the whole keeps a code panel no larger than before, while one on each line would either cut lines the panel has room for or let sixteen long lines grow it many times over. A line is then never cut where it is shown, so the only "…" on a code panel says that it has run out of rows.

In November 2026, speaking from the headset, the maintainer asked for the agent to know where people are and to show things near them or where they are looking, so that asking it to "show me the source code for X" puts the panel nearby, or at the thing they are looking at, such as the closest wall; they were not concerned about the agent having detailed positions. The default moved from the wall nearest the asker to the wall they were looking at, falling back to the nearest, since the wall in front of someone is the one they will read, while the nearest may be behind them; it is still captured as the words begin, as attention is, so that what is projected does not depend on where they look while the agent works. Placement is said in the blocks the agent already writes, a header line for a panel and an info-string attribute for a code block, since a code block has nothing after its fence but its info string, and an attribute there keeps each block whole and alone, where a directive before it or a tool applying to the next reply would tie blocks to state the agent cannot see; `move_panel` covers changing one's mind afterwards. Forms that name nobody mean whoever asked, which is what "show me" asks for, and an unknown name or form costs only the placement, never the panel, as attention costs only itself. A panel a participant placed is respected, as code panels' replacement already respects it; floating is offered only when asked for, with `front`, since a panel floating in the way is more intrusive than one on a wall, and every space has a Tomori wall to fall back to.

In November 2026, speaking from the headset, the maintainer found a summary placed on a wall seven metres away, too small to read, when they stood about half a metre from home with the Tomori wall, a low curved row beneath the swarm, 2.8 m away and in view: their head's forward ray passed just above it and met an added wall far behind. They asked that a panel go where it can be read, and that the agent may move panels people placed when asked, after it refused to move one they had placed and asked it to move. The default and `looking` now take only walls within reading distance. A cap of 4 m was chosen over a distance computed from each slot's text, since the comfortable 0.45° x-height of `text.md` would rule out every wall as it stands, while 4 m keeps a flat wall's text about as sharp as the Tomori wall's from home, the sharpest walls are designed to be read at, and one distance is simple to reason about and to tell the agent. A tolerance of 10° about the gaze was added as well, since the head's forward direction is not where the eyes look, and a low wall someone plainly looks towards is often just below that ray; preferring the nearest wall looked at keeps what is chosen readable when the ray and the tolerance name different walls. Floating in front of them is the last resort, rather than a far wall, since a panel nobody can read helps no one. Moving a participant's panel is the agent's when asked, since asking is a participant's own choice, as the maintainer decided; it stays pinned where it goes, so that automatic placement still leaves it, and is announced on the Tomori wall, so that whoever placed it is not left looking for it. A panel carried is refused, since moving it out of someone's hand would be a surprise nobody asked for, and the client says what it carries because only it knows.

In November 2026, speaking from the headset, the maintainer asked for panels the agent can update by id, such as checklists ticked off as a conversation covers them: the agent could project the questions or topics to cover as a GitHub-flavoured checklist, then tick items off and revise the list as the conversation goes, updating the same panel rather than projecting a new one each time, and know which list, and which item, someone points at. An id the agent chooses, rather than the server's identity, lets it write the update as it wrote the panel, in a block, without first asking which identity the panel was given, and keeps the transcript readable; scoping it to the space, and letting the latest projection of an id take the place of one hidden elsewhere, means an id names one panel. An update keeps everything about where the panel is and how it entered its wall, since moving or reframing a list while people read it is the shuffling the layout avoids, and a participant's placing always wins, as for `move_panel`. `update_panel` lets the agent tick an item without resending the list, and `panels` lets it read what the list says now, rather than relying on what it remembers writing. Ticked items are dimmed rather than struck through, since text has no strike style and dimming keeps them legible; boxes replace bullets, as GitHub shows them. The client names the line an item begins on rather than its number, since only the server's numbering, from its own copy of the body, counts, and it never takes a description from a client; a line maps cleanly to a row as the face lays the body out, which the client already does. Ticking with the trigger was designed but left for later, since the trigger attracts the swarm.

Later in November 2026, once input precedence was in (`interaction.md`), the maintainer chose A, rather than the trigger, for ticking an item pointed at, so that the trigger keeps one meaning on Tomori's panels, and approved building it. Participants tick, not visitors, as only participants rearrange panels. Any text panel's task items may be ticked, whoever projected the panel, since that is the simplest rule and a list a participant points at is one they are reading. The agent learns of a tick through `panels` at once and in a note before the next words, rather than in a turn of its own, so that it follows along without replying to every tick; the note keeps only the latest change to each item, since that is what the list now says. A participant's tick is not counted as the agent's update, so that `panels` still says when the agent last revised a panel. The item parser moved into `tomori-protocol` so that a client's optimistic tick and the label's hint read items exactly as the server ticks them.

Later in November 2026, the review of #254 found that a participant's tick could land on another item when racing an agent update, since ticks address items by line, and `update_panel` may replace a body, reordering, adding or removing items, between what a participant saw and their tick reaching the server; the agent's `update_panel` naming items by number had the same race with participants' ticks. The maintainer chose optimistic concurrency with a plain state counter, refuse-and-retry, over transactional matching such as finding the item again by its text: each text panel counts a revision of its content, a tick or an update says the revision it saw, and one that does not match is refused, the participant cued and shown the panel as it is, and the agent told the current revision, to read the panel again and redo its update. It is simple to reason about, never guesses at which item was meant, and costs a participant at most a second look.

In November 2026, speaking from the headset, the maintainer found "…4 more lines" on a code panel of 17 lines of JavaScript with no way to see them: code panels kept their slot's 16 rows, and the long lines that now wrapped took rows of their own, so even modest blocks overflowed, while text panels grew to fit when pulled off and code panels did not. The coordinator chose scrolling with the right stick while pointing, since pointing without carrying left the stick free but for paging result cards, and carrying already gives the stick to the panel; scrolling is each participant's own, a view rather than shared state, so that it never fights someone else reading the same panel. A code panel floating now grows as a text panel does, to the same greatest height, so that pulling one off shows a block of that size whole. Scrolling showed that the server sent only the first 16 lines, so the last of the 17 could never be seen: it now keeps 200 lines and 8,000 characters, generous for what agents show, while a frame holds every code panel whole only while there are few, so a projection that would not fit sends the panels least recently placed as short as before, saying that the rest of each is in the transcript. Code panels projected before the server stopped cutting each line to 64 characters (#220) kept their lines cut, as their text was kept so and the rest of it is not, so such a panel still shows "…" partway along a line; they are left as they are rather than migrated.

In November 2026, speaking from the headset, the maintainer asked for panels to show images, since the agent could only show text and could not show the session browser's layout renders it was describing. Images come from files on the server's machine only, so that nothing untrusted is fetched from the network, and from any readable file, since the agent can read them anyway. A tool, rather than a `panel` block, shows them: a tool's result tells the agent when a file is missing, too large or not an image, which a block in a reply cannot, as a refused block is never reported, and the file is read and prepared while the agent waits, rather than as its reply completes. The server decodes every file and sends one format it made itself, so that a headset never decodes what the agent pointed it at, and the size, pixel and memory bounds hold before anything is decoded. Images travel as frames of their own, after everything else on the connection and in small chunks, rather than in the projection or as a request of their own: the projection stays within one frame however many images it shows, speech and control are never held up behind an image, and one connection, already authenticated, already decides what a client may see, so a fetch needs only the check that its space shows the image. Fetching by hash, a few at a time, means an image shown on several panels, or again after reconnecting, crosses once, and the server never queues more than a client takes. The 2,048-pixel bound is what a Quest 3's eye sees across most of its field of view at arm's length, and the texture budget, with mipmaps made in linear light, keeps a wall of images sharp and steady without taking much of the headset's memory.

In November 2026, speaking from the headset, the maintainer found no way to adjust walls: to get rid of one, to resize it, to change its grid or to change its curvature.
