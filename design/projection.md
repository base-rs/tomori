# Projection

This describes agent projection's first slice (issue #82): text panels and code panels the agent projects onto walls in a space, which participants pull off, leave floating, put back and dismiss, and point at as they speak, and the text and chart panels an environment's server side publishes onto its own walls, which participants handle the same way. Projection belongs to the space and the agent stage, not to any environment, and works in every environment; what an environment publishes stays in that environment. Later slices add the other content formats, manipulation and MCP tools #82 describes.

## Requirement

**Two kinds of wall.** Projection is spatially registered, and environments differ in shape, so there are two kinds of wall:

- **The status wall.** Every space has exactly one, its own, the Tomori wall (`tomori_protocol::TOMORI_WALL`), unless its environment gives the role to one of its own walls (below). It belongs to the space and the central Tomori interface, not to an environment, and what is on it stays as the space changes environment. It is curved: its six slots stand in one row along an arc, each turned to face the point the arc curves about, 2.3 m ahead of the row's middle. By default the Tomori wall stands below the swarm, sized for the swarm whatever avatar a client shows (see `avatars.md`), the middle of its row 2.3 m straight ahead of home at 0.52 m, so that the arc curves about home and every slot faces a participant standing there; its slots are drawn at 0.7 of a flat wall's, about 42 by 25 cm, 3 cm apart, and its label, `TOMORI`, is below the row. There, in the default layout, it hides neither the swarm, whose flock stays above it, nor any worker's station, which stand at least 40° to either side of straight ahead, while the row spans less than 34° either side; its top edge is more than 22° below the eyes from home, below the status pill, which stands about 20° below them (see `interaction.md`); and it is clear in Sky, the grid and the farmyard. **Its home.** The environment says where the status wall is, with `Environment::with_status_wall(StatusWall)`: `StatusWall::at(EnvironmentWall)`, the space's own standing where that wall stands, in the environment's coordinates carried into the space's, laid out by default as it says, its grid, slots, curve and tilt, the default the space's edits stand over; or `StatusWall::own(N)`, one of the environment's own walls, by its place among those `with_walls` declares, from 0, which is then the status wall, its pose, grid, lock and scenery the environment's, the space's own not shown there at all. `with_stage(pose)`, kept for registrations that use it, is `StatusWall::at` at that pose, laid out as the default is. Either must have at least two slots (`STATUS_SLOTS`), and `own` must name a wall the environment declares, or it is left out with a warning; `at` must stand, carried, finite, within 30 m of home and with an orientation, made upright; otherwise the default stands. Without one, the space's own stands beneath the agent's home, wherever the environment puts it: the middle of its row 0.88 m directly below the swarm's middle, facing the way the agent faces, curving about the point 2.3 m before it, with a clear gap between the swarm and the top of its row, and never lower than 0.52 m above the floor where participants arrive (`tomori_protocol::entourage::wall_beneath`, see `environments.md`), which in the default layout is the default above. **It never travels.** It stays at its home whatever the agent does, following, called or asked to go, so that nothing has to find room for it about the agent; its pose is never kept, nor can anyone move it, but an owner an environment's own wall once unlocked, so it stands at each environment's home with the space's edits, and, when the space changes environment, at the new one's. A client still folds the Tomori wall, travels it and unfolds it when its pose changes within the same space and environment, as an older server that travels it with a called agent sends it, drawing its panels folded with it, pointing at none of them and putting none on it by hand meanwhile, and letting go of it if held; a wall that appears elsewhere, as a space is joined or changes environment, stands there at once. **Its name.** `presence` and `panels` call the space's own `status wall (the Tomori wall)`, for a release, and an environment's own wall in the role by both names, as `environment wall 1 (the status wall)`; `place:`, `move_panel` and the wall tools take every name, `status wall`, `Tomori wall`, a wall's own name and either full one, with or without `the`, in any case, `status wall` and `Tomori wall` naming the role, wherever it is. The space's own keeps its label, `TOMORI` (an open question), and an environment's own wall what its scenery draws. `tomori_protocol::Wall::status` says which wall is the status wall; an older client ignores it, and draws the status wall where the projection says, as it drew the Tomori wall. Each environment's tests check that its status wall is read (see `environments.md`).
- **Environment walls.** These belong to the environment the space is in now: those its registration declares, with `Environment::with_walls`, a general hook that names no particular environment, of which the server uses the first sixteen and ignores any more (`DECLARED_WALLS`, see declared walls, below), each a pose or an `EnvironmentWall`, which says its grid and the size of its slots too, their shape, whether it curves and how far it tilts, and what it is called (see declared walls, below); and those its owners add. An environment's own walls are part of the place, and locked until a space's owner unlocks them (see locked walls, below). Tomori's own server gives the farmyard one, two slots wide and one high, on its notice board against the right of the fence, its status wall (see `farmyard.md`); Sky and the grid declare none.

**Notice boards and consoles.** The intended pattern for an environment's own walls is a notice board or a console: a wall that is part of the place, drawn by the environment's scenery as something that stands there, such as a board on posts in a yard or a console on a bridge, which people walk up to and put content on, the agent's panels and their own, and organise as they like. An environment may have several, each where its kind of work is done, locked so that they stay where the place puts them, while what is on them is the space's. The farmyard's notice board is the first; a downstream environment's consoles, beside the data they show, are expected to follow it.

**Status panels, and an environment's own wall in the status role.** The space's status panels are those that came through the status wall's role: Tomori's notices, panels the agent projects without anyone asking, those it places `wall status wall` or `wall Tomori wall`, those a participant puts on the space's own, and those the space kept on the Tomori wall. They belong to the space and go with it, to the next environment's status wall. Whatever a participant or the agent puts on an environment's own wall by hand or by its own name, or its side publishes there, is that wall's, kept for the space in that environment. Moving one between the status wall's slots keeps its kind; pulling one off and putting it back makes it the wall's. `panels` and `presence` mark status panels `status: true`. On an environment's own wall in the role, as the farmyard's notice board, both share its slots:

- **When it is full.** A status panel takes the wall's free slot; with none, the slot of the oldest status panel nobody arranged, which leaves, as a full wall's oldest does; then of the oldest of the wall's own nobody arranged; and, with every slot holding one its environment published or a participant arranged, it floats just in front of the wall's right end, facing as it faces, the environment's, so that a notice is never lost; where another floats there already, a slot's width further to its right, up to six. **The environment's own come first:** one of the wall's own, published or put there, takes the slot of the oldest status panel nobody arranged, which waits; else of the oldest of the wall's own nobody arranged, which leaves; else of the oldest status panel, which waits; else of the oldest of the wall's own, which leaves; but never of one its environment published, as on any wall, so that with every slot holding one, a panel the agent puts there goes to the status role, as by default, and floats in front of the wall's right end. So a side publishing onto a board full of status panels is not refused for them, and notices never displace a live chart. One that names its slot, or comes back to the slot it rested in, takes that slot, the status panel there waiting.
- **Waiting for a slot.** A status panel the status wall has no slot for is kept, not shown, and takes a slot as one frees, as a panel is dismissed, moved or retracted, a side stops or the wall is given more slots, those a participant arranged first, then the newest; `panels` lists those waiting, newest first, as `waiting_for_a_slot`. As the space opens in an environment, after a restart or a change of environment, its status panels take the status wall's free slots the same way. So on first opening in the farmyard, what a space kept on the Tomori wall goes into the board's free slots, those a participant arranged first, and the rest wait; back where the status wall is the space's own, those that waited stand in the slots they had, and the rest take its free slots. Nothing is dismissed; of two in one slot, the older waits.
- **Its edits and lock.** The wall's own edits, its grid, slots, tilt, lock and, unlocked, its pose, are the environment's wall's, kept for the space in that environment, as for any of its walls; the Tomori wall's kept edits do not apply to it, and stand wherever the status wall is the space's own. It stays locked by default, which locks its layout, not its content; changed so that it has fewer slots, its status panels keep their column and row as its own do, and those without a slot float where their slot stood, the environment's.

**A wall's grid, size and shape.** A wall's slots are a grid of columns and rows (`Grid`), numbered from its top left as seen from in front, left to right and then down: from one to eight columns and one to four rows, and at most 24 slots. Its slots are the size of a flat wall's, about 60 by 36 cm, scaled by its `scale`, from 0.5 to 5, so that a panel in one is drawn larger or smaller and is legible from further or nearer, and a wall is as large as its slots and the gutters between them. **A big wall**, of a scale above 2, is for reading from far away, such as a dashboard across a yard: its text is as many times larger, and as legible from as many times further, so that a wall of five times a standard slot's 15 m away reads as a standard one 3 m away. Text is drawn from the glyph atlas's distance fields at any size (see `text.md`), so a big wall's is as sharp as a standard one's at the same angular size, and its panels hold no more than a standard wall's, so a big wall costs the frame and the walls' bound of 780 slots nothing more; an image panel's image, at most 2,048 pixels, is as sharp on a big wall far away as on a standard one near. An older client, which takes a scale of at most 2, draws a bigger wall at 2, smaller than its slots, but legibly. **Its slots may be any shape:** its `aspect`, their width over their height, from 1:4, tall, to 4:1, wide, makes them taller or wider than a standard slot's, about 5:3, at the same `scale`, which is then the size of their text, so that a tall slot holds more rows of a list and a wide one more columns of code, and the wall grows to hold them. A tall slot is as wide as a standard one at its scale and as tall as its aspect makes it; a wide one as tall and as wide as its aspect makes it, to a whole number of text cells, so that a flat wall's board stays a whole number of them. A panel in a slot of its own shape is laid out to fill it at that text size: a text panel's body wraps at the slot's width and shows as many rows as its height holds, a code panel's code wraps at as many columns as its width holds and shows as many rows, never fewer than a standard slot's 34 columns of 12 rows, or 48 of 16 of code; it fills the slot to within a cell, centred. An image panel, where a client draws one, fits its image within the slot, as within a standard one. It is flat, or curved (`WallShape`): its columns along an arc about a vertical line a radius ahead of it, from 1 m to 50 m, spanning at most half a circle, each slot turned to face that line, its rows one above another about its middle. **It may tilt** (`Wall::tilt`) about its horizontal middle line, the line through its pose along its own x, from 45° with its top away from the people in front of it, facing up, as for a wall below them, to 45° with its top towards them, facing down, as for a wall above them; a curved wall tilts its whole arc about that line, the line it curves about tilting with it. Its pose stays upright, turned only about the vertical, as every wall's is, and its tilt is kept beside it, so that an older client, which knows no tilt, draws it upright, and the server's making walls upright keeps its meaning. A wall in a group of walls snapped together (see below) stands upright, since walls each tilted about their own middle lines would no longer meet as one board: a tilt asked for one is refused, saying to take it out of its group first; a tilted wall has none snapped to it; and a tilted wall snapped to another stands upright, as a curved one takes the other's radius. Unless it says otherwise, a flat wall is three columns of two rows at scale 1, with the label `WALL` above them, upright, about 2 m by 0.9 m, translucent, with its empty slots faintly marked; and a curved wall is six in one row at 0.7, each slot a faint board of its own, its label below them, as the Tomori wall is by default. A flat wall's board is measured in a panel's font pixels, 1.9 mm at scale 1: a gutter of 24 pixels between its slots and around them, its label's row above them, and a whole number of rows of text tall, scaled about its middle. A wall is at most 16 m wide and 9 m tall, a cinema's screen.

**The size of its content.** A wall's `content_scale` (`Wall::content_scale`), from 0.5 to 2, and 1 unless it says otherwise, draws the content of the panels in its slots, their text, tables, lists, code and charts' labels, that many times the size its slots give it, and changes neither its slots, their size nor their number: its text is drawn at its `scale` times its content scale (`Wall::text_scale`), which each slot's frame says, so that at a smaller content scale each slot holds as many more columns and rows, never fewer than a standard slot's, and a long panel shows more before it is cut off or scrolls, and at a larger one fewer, never fewer than 12 columns of 2 rows of body, to read from further. So a wall made big to hold more holds more at a smaller content scale, rather than only larger text; resizing it, or its slots, keeps its content scale. **Never too small to read:** its text is never smaller than half a standard slot's as seen from where the wall is read, which its slots' scale already allows (`layout::LEAST_TEXT`): a wall of a standard slot's size or larger, read from as far as a standard one or as many times further as its slots are larger, takes a content scale of at least half; a smaller one as much more, to a hundredth (`Wall::least_content_scale`), so that the Tomori wall's, at 0.7, takes at least 0.72, and a wall of the least scale stays at 1. A content scale asked for below that or beyond 2 is refused, saying so; slots made smaller beneath one they no longer allow keep it at the least they do. The agent reads a wall from as far as its text, so drawn, allows (see where the agent's panels go, below). Each wall of a group keeps its own, as it keeps its own slots. At half a slot holds about four times the cells, so a standard slot's code filled to them is about 12,000 vertices and a tall slot's, 9:16, about 40,000; a wall panel's buffer grows to hold such a face, to four times its usual size (see charts, below). An older client ignores it, drawing each panel as large as its slots give it, showing what it showed before.

**Walls an environment declares.** An environment declares each wall of its own, with `Environment::with_walls`, laid out as an owner could lay out an added wall, so that a console reads as the place has it, whatever a space later changes (a change stands over it, and putting it back, `LockWall`'s `reset`, restores it): an `EnvironmentWall` says its pose, its `grid` and `scale` and also the `aspect` of its slots, from 1:4 to 4:1, a `curve`, the radius of the arc it curves along, from 1 m to 50 m, and a `tilt`, from −45° to 45°, as `SetWall` does (`with_grid`, `with_scale`, `with_aspect`, `with_curve` and `with_tilt`); a value beyond its bounds, or not finite, is as left unsaid, a standard slot's shape, flat and upright, and a tilt of 0 is upright. The whole is then held to the rule a participant's change is (`Stage::fits`): curved no further round than half a circle, and at most 16 m wide and 9 m tall; a declaration that is not, as eight wide columns at twice a standard slot's size, or three wide columns curved at 1 m, is laid out as though its shape, curve and tilt were unsaid, or, should its grid and scale still not fit, as a flat wall's, which the server logs, so that a declared wall is always one the space can then change. It may also say what it is called, `with_name("Details")`, a name of from 1 to 24 ASCII letters, digits, spaces and `-` (`valid_wall_name`), which is the wall's label, in capitals, in place of `WALL`, and the name by which the agent names it, in place of `environment wall 1`: `place: wall Details slot 4`, `move_panel` and `set_wall` take it in any case, with or without `the`, so that someone asking "show me the servers under high load" has the agent put them on the wall for the purpose. A name must not begin as another wall is called, `Tomori`, `environment`, `added` or `wall-`, or begin with `the `, nor hold the word `slot`, which would end a wall's name where a slot's number begins, nor have as its first word one a place begins with, `near`, `nearest`, `look`, `looking`, `front`, `beside` or `wall`, in any case, since the agent's tools try a wall's name before those forms; one that does, or that an earlier wall of the environment already has, in any case, is ignored, and the wall is called `environment wall N` as before. The name is sent as `Wall::name`, of an environment's own wall alone; an older client draws `WALL`, and a client draws it in capitals, cut to 24 cells as drawn once in capitals. What the agent is told names it as a name, `the wall "Details"`, as `The wall "Details" is locked` or `has no slot 9`. The wall is locked, as every such wall is, and the agent puts panels on it as on any wall, by name and slot, since the lock is the wall's, not its content's. A wall's own grid is still at most 8 by 4 and 24 slots, and a declared wall snaps to nothing. An environment declares at most sixteen walls, enough for a console of a wall for each of its stations with room to grow: the server uses the first sixteen and ignores any more, as it ignored any beyond six before, so that a seventeenth is shown to nobody, takes no panel, published or the agent's, and is no status wall. Their names stay unique as above, and sixteen fit the name rules, as `Deck console station 015` does. Their slots, up to 384 at 24 each, count towards the 780 the walls shown in one environment hold together (see bounds, below), so that more walls never make a projection larger than its slots and panels do, and Tomori's own clients state walls and slots enough to draw sixteen standard walls (see limits stated in Hello, below). Where an environment at a later version declares more walls, or larger, than its earlier one, walls owners added beyond the 780 slots then left are hidden from everyone, the last added first, kept with their panels, and shown again, in turn, as owners remove or shrink walls that are shown: an owner notices by the walls missing, and by a new wall, or a wall made larger, being refused, saying the walls hold as many slots as they can, since the hidden walls' slots still count. The agent's `presence` lists only the walls shown, and is not yet bounded in size: at every limit its walls and floating panels are about 378 KB, which #328 is to bound as `panels` is.

**One layout, shared.** Where each slot of a wall stands, given its pose, grid, scale, shape and tilt, is `tomori_protocol::wall` (`Wall::slot_frames`, `Wall::board`, `Wall::size`, `Wall::frame`, its pose tilted, which its slots and board stand in, and `Wall::slot_pose`), which the server and every client share: the server lays panels out in it, tells the agent where slots are and what people look at from it, and floats a displaced panel where its slot stood from it; clients draw slots and aim at them from it. So the two cannot drift apart. A client draws a grid or scale it is sent beyond these bounds as its shape's own, and a curve too tight or around too much flat.

**Adding and removing walls.** An owner adds a wall where they stand, from the hand menu's Space section (see `hand-menu.md`): it stands 1.8 m ahead of their head, level, a little below their eyes, upright and facing them. An owner removes the added wall nearest them from the same section. Each panel on it is left floating where its slot stood, facing as it faced, rather than going with it, so that removing a wall loses nothing. Only walls an owner added are removed; at most sixty are added in each environment of a space, each within 30 m of the space's middle. A collaborator or visitor is refused both, and their menu does not offer them.

**Adjusting walls.** Participants, owners and collaborators alike, adjust walls, as they rearrange panels; a visitor adjusts none, and the server refuses them, as it refuses every action their role does not permit (see `security.md`). `SetWall` changes a wall's grid, the size of its slots, their shape and its own, flat or curved at a radius, its tilt, the size of its content within its slots, and, for a wall an owner added, its pose; what it leaves out stays. A change is the space's, shown to everyone in it at once and kept with it across restarts:

- **The space's own status wall, the Tomori wall,** keeps its grid, shape, tilt and content scale for the space, wherever it is the status wall, as it keeps its panels, over the layout its environment gives it, or the default. Where its environment stands it, it takes any wall's grid, size and shape. Beneath the agent's home, it stays one row of one to six slots, at its own 0.7 and of a standard slot's shape, flat or curved, tilted or not, its content at any scale it allows, since tilting about its middle line keeps it within where it stands, so that it stays below the swarm, within the 40° either side of straight ahead the workers' stations leave clear, and below the pill; an edit made where its environment stood it stands there as one row of as many of its slots as there are, at most six, of its own size, curved and tilted as it was, its panels in slots it no longer has taking free ones or waiting. Nobody moves it.
- **An environment's own walls** are adjusted for the space in that environment, kept with what the space keeps for it and shown again on returning: their grid, slots' size and shape and tilt, but not where they stand or whether they are there, which are the environment's.
- **An added wall's** grid, slots' size, shape, tilt and pose are its own. Moved, it stands anywhere within 30 m of the space's middle, made upright as every wall is, and its panels move with it, in their slots.
- **Panels keep their places.** A panel keeps its column and row as a wall's grid changes, so that nothing moves that need not, even where its slot is numbered anew. A panel in a column or row the wall no longer has goes into the free slot nearest it, by column and row, those a participant put where they are first and then the newest; with no free slot, it floats where its slot stood, facing as it faced. No change to a wall takes a panel away, pinned or not.
- **Bounds.** The walls shown in one environment hold at most 780 slots together, ten times the 78 of the Tomori wall and twelve walls of six they held before frames were negotiated, and at most 120 panels float in it, with one more for each slot the walls hold fewer than 780, so that a projection at every limit still fits in one frame of 32 MiB. These are the server's bounds, `Limits::MOST`, which its `Welcome` states; each client is sent no more of them than it states it draws, and an older client today's, 78 and twelve (see limits stated in Hello, below). Panels a wall gives up therefore float beyond the 120; leaving another floating takes away the one floating longest, as ever, of which they are the newest. A change, or a new wall, that would take the walls past 780 slots, or give them more slots while that many float that they would go past the bound together, is refused, saying so, as is a grid, size or curve a wall cannot have.

**Locked walls.** Every wall is locked or unlocked, for the space:

- **A locked wall changes for nobody.** It is neither moved, resized, reshaped, tilted, bent, given columns or rows, its content scaled, nor removed, by a participant, an owner or the agent, until an owner unlocks it: the server refuses every `SetWall` and `RemoveWall` of it, whatever it asks, saying `That wall is locked: ask an owner to unlock it.`, and the agent's tools refuse it too (see `mcp.md`). Panels still go onto it, are rearranged on it, come off it and are thrown away, and the agent projects onto it, as onto any wall: the lock is the wall's, not its content's. So an environment publishes onto its own walls, locked or not, updates its panels there in place and retracts them, as on any wall, since a lock that froze a console's live charts would break the console, and only a full wall refuses another, saying it is full; participants and the agent move, pull off and throw away an environment's panel on a locked wall as anywhere, which then stays away as a dismissed one does; and one resting comes back to its slot on a locked wall as on any.
- **Who locks.** Only owners lock and unlock walls, with `LockWall`, from the headset's padlock or by asking the agent, since it changes what everyone else may do; collaborators adjust walls that are unlocked as before, and visitors adjust none. The server checks the role.
- **What is locked by default.** An environment's own walls are locked, since they are part of the place; walls an owner adds, and the Tomori wall, are unlocked.
- **Kept with the space.** Whether a wall is locked is kept as the rest of its adjustments are: the Tomori wall's for the space, in every environment; an environment's own wall's, and an added wall's, for the space in that environment, across restarts. Only what differs from the default is written, beside what rests of the environment's panels.
- **An environment's own wall, unlocked,** is moved as an added wall is, carried on the headset or placed by the agent, within 30 m of the space's middle and made upright, its panels moving with it, and where it stands is kept for the space in that environment, over where the environment puts it, as its grid and slots are. It is never removed, unlocked or not, since it is part of the place, and the environment's scenery still draws what it stands on there.
- **Locked again,** it stays where it was moved and as it was changed, so that an owner may move a board and fix it there. **Putting it back** is a choice of its own: `LockWall`'s `reset`, the padlock's companion `↺` on the headset, or `set_wall`'s `reset`, puts an environment's own wall back where and as the environment has it, its grid, slots and curve the environment's, and locks it, the space keeping nothing of its own for it; its panels keep their column and row, or go into the nearest free slot, or float where they were, as for any change to a wall. A wall already as the environment has it changes nothing.
- **The space's own status wall** takes the lock as any wall does, unlocked by default: locked, its grid and shape change for nobody. Its place is never the space's, locked or not: it stands at the home its environment gives it (see two kinds of wall, above). An environment's own wall in the role keeps its own lock, locked by default.
- **Snapping.** A locked wall is no snap target, and is never snapped, attached or detached, as it is never carried: `Wall::is_locked` says which, for the code that snaps walls together. A change to a wall of a group that would move or reshape a locked wall of it, as carrying, bending or resizing a wall the rest stand from does, is refused, saying a wall of its group is locked, and the headset carries no group with a locked wall in it (see snapping walls together, below).

**The agent adjusts walls.** The agent changes, adds and removes walls through its tools when someone in the space asks (see `mcp.md`), as an owner may: `set_wall` changes a wall's columns and rows, the size of its slots, or a width in metres that sets it, their shape, as an aspect such as `9:16`, or their width and height in metres instead, its curve, its tilt, in degrees, the size of its content within its slots, so that "fit more on this wall" draws it smaller, and the place in front of someone of a wall someone added or of an environment's own unlocked, and, only when an owner asks, locks or unlocks it, or puts an environment's own back, locked, so that "tilt this wall down a bit" is the wall the speaker looks at, tilted about 10° further down; `add_wall` adds a wall in front of someone, laid out as it says, a big wall, of a scale above 2, as many times further than 1.8 m as its scale is above 2, so that its text looks as large as a wall of twice a standard slot's does there, and one so tall that it would reach below the floor where people arrive raised until its bottom is 10 cm above it; `remove_wall` removes a wall someone added, leaving its panels floating. It names a wall by its identity or name, as `presence` lists it, or as the wall someone is looking at or is nearest, `looking [NAME]` or `near [NAME]`, so that "make this wall four panels wide and one high" means the wall the speaker was looking at as they began speaking; `front` and `beside` name no wall, and are refused, saying so. Each change it makes is shown to everyone at once, logged with the space, and told: its result asks it to tell everyone what it changed, and a notice titled "Walls", from Tomori, on the Tomori wall says it, replacing the last such notice unless a participant put that one somewhere.

**What the server keeps.** The server keeps every space's walls and panels with the space, in its record of spaces, in a format of its own with a schema version, so that they survive a restart:

- the space's status panels, for the space, each where it last stood on a status wall, or waiting;
- for each environment the space has been in, its added walls, each with its grid, slots' size and shape where changed, how the space has adjusted that environment's own walls, and the panels on its walls or floating in it, keyed by the space and the environment;
- how the space has adjusted the Tomori wall, where changed;
- for each environment, the panels it published, each only as it rests (below): its identity, the environment's id for it, when it was first published, where it is and whether a participant put it there, but nothing it showed, neither its title, body nor chart, so that nothing a run that ended showed can come back as current.

What a wall leaves at its default is not written, so that a space kept before walls could be adjusted reads as it was, in the same schema version.

Walls and floating panels of an environment are hidden, not deleted, while the space is in another, and shown again, where they were, when it returns. At most eight environments are kept for a space; going to a ninth forgets the one left longest ago. At most 1,200 panels are kept for a space in all, more than it can show in one environment, at most 900; beyond that, the hidden panel put where it is longest ago is forgotten, so that what a space keeps stays bounded. Panels on a wall the environment no longer declares are kept, but not shown. The server makes every wall and floating panel a client places upright, turning one sent tilted only about the vertical.

**Where the agent's panels go.** Panels are shared, so a panel goes where everyone in the space sees it, on a wall or floating in the space's scene coordinates, and the agent chooses a spot good for whoever asked:

- **By default, where whoever asked was looking, near enough to read.** When a participant asks something, each panel the agent projects in answer goes onto the wall they were looking at as their utterance began, from where their head was and the way it faced in the shared space, as their presence last said, if it is near enough for them to read. Only walls within **4 m** of their eyes, measured to the wall's nearest slot or board, or as many times further as a big wall's text, at its content scale, is larger than a standard slot's, so that a wall five times as large is taken within 20 m, and at a content scale of half within 10 m, are taken: within it, a flat wall's text, at its 1.9 mm font pixel, is about as sharp as the curved Tomori wall's, at seven tenths of that, from home, about 2.6 m away, as far as walls are designed to be read from; beyond it, as on a wall 7 m away, a slot's text is too small to read without pulling the panel off. Comfortable reading, an x-height of about 0.45° (see `text.md`), would put a slot's text within about 1.4 m, nearer than any wall stands from home, so the reading distance follows the walls' design instead, and is one distance for every wall of a standard slot's size or smaller rather than computed from each slot's text; a big wall's text is as legible as many times further away as it is larger, so its distance grows with it. Of those walls:
  - **The nearest wall looked at.** A wall is looked at when the ray from their eyes meets one of its slots, its board or a panel on it, or when one of its slots' middles is within **10°** of that ray, since a head faces a little above what the eyes look at and passes just over a low wall, such as the Tomori wall below the swarm, that they plainly look towards. Of the walls looked at, the nearest is chosen: into the empty slot the ray meets, when it meets one of that wall's, or else into its most prominent free slot.
  - **Else floating beside them.** Looking at no wall near enough to read, as between two of a curved wall's slots, at a floating panel, past a person, at a wall too far away or away from every wall, it floats beside them, as `beside` puts it (below), rather than onto a wall they were not looking at, which may be far from them or behind them.

  The nearest wall in their view, and the nearest wall, are no longer the default: `looking` still falls back to them, and then beside them, and `near` names the nearest wall. Panels the agent projects with nobody to put them for, such as in a reply after a restart before anyone has spoken, and Tomori's own notices, such as a worker finishing, a pairing decided or a wall changed, go onto the status wall, the space's wall for what it and Tomori say of themselves.
- **Beside someone.** A panel floating beside someone stands **1.2 m** from their eyes, measured level, a comfortable reading distance for a floating panel's text, its middle **15 cm below their eyes**, upright and facing them, **30° to their right** of the way they face about the vertical, or, looking straight up or down, of the way the top of their head faces, so that it is read by turning the head a little and never covers what is straight ahead: at that distance a floating panel of 48 cells, about 85 cm wide, the widest a text panel floats for prose, begins about 10° to the side. Where that spot is taken, it stands **30° to their left**, then 70° to their right, 70° to their left, 110° to their right and 110° to their left, 40° apart, as far as such a panel takes, so that several panels in one reply, or over a conversation, fan out about them, right, left, then further out, rather than stacking. A spot is taken where it would stand over a panel already floating within 3 m of them, as seen from their eyes, across and up and down, whoever left it there, an environment's panel a participant floated among them, which it never moves, taking each panel as 85 by 60 cm; and it is not clear where an edge or diagonal of it, or the line from their eyes to its middle, passes through the environment's scenery, its solids (see `environments.md`), a wall shown, its slots as thick as 5 cm more on every side, or the agent, the swarm's reach and 10 cm more about where it stands or goes, so that scenery, a wall or the agent to their right sends it to their left. The agent comes to someone straight from wherever it was, on no fixed side of them, so a panel beside them goes to whichever side the agent is not on. With every spot clear but taken, it takes the first clear spot, before what floats there; with none clear, as when walled in, it floats in front of them, as `front` puts it. It is placed as the panel is put, so that each of a reply's panels, placed in turn, finds the spots its predecessors took, and a panel moved beside the same person is not in its own way. Panels floating beside someone **stay put** where they were left, as every floating panel does, rather than following the person; the agent moves them again with `move_panel` when someone asks, and a participant carries them as any panel. They float within the 120 that float in an environment, and the room walls give up, and leaving more floating takes away the one floating longest, as for any panel.
- **At an anchor.** `place: anchor NAME` floats a panel in the cluster of the space's cluster anchor of that name, as `presence` lists them (see `environments.md`), in the next free slot as it arrives, laid out by `tomori_protocol::cluster` beside the workers and the agent standing there, upright and facing whoever the cluster gathers about, several of one reply each taking a slot of their own. It stays a member while it floats where it was put: the server floats it again as the anchor moves or its cluster is laid out again, and a participant carrying it elsewhere, or the agent moving it, takes it out. One at an anchor not here goes where it would without a placement, as for a wall not here; `move_panel`, `update_panel` and `show_image` refuse it, naming `presence`. As the anchor goes, its panels stay floating where they stand, out of its cluster, within the 120 that float, and the notice of its going counts them (see `environments.md`). The stage keeps a panel's anchor, by name, with it, so that across a restart one whose anchor its environment's side has not placed again floats where it last stood and joins the cluster as the anchor appears.
- **Where the agent says.** A panel block's header may say where its panel goes with `place:`, and a code block's info string with a `place=` attribute, as below, in one of these forms, its form in any case and separated from what it names by spaces or a colon:
  - `near NAME`: onto the wall nearest that participant, as they last reported;
  - `looking NAME`: where that participant is looking, as by default, near enough to read;
  - `beside NAME`: floating beside them, as above;
  - `front NAME`: floating straight in front of them, 1.2 m ahead, level with where they face, 15 cm below their eyes and facing them, as a participant's panel left floating does, within the 120 that float in an environment;
  - `wall WALL`: onto the wall with that identity or name, as `presence` names it, such as `status wall`, `environment wall 1` or `added wall 2`, in any case, with or without `the`, `status wall` and `Tomori wall` naming the status wall wherever it stands, its panel then a status panel;
  - `wall WALL slot N`: into that wall's slot `N`, numbered from 0 as `presence` and `create_layout` number them, `slot` in any case and separated from the wall and the number by spaces or colons, as in `wall added wall 2 slot 3` or `wall:wall-4:slot:0`, so that the agent fills a layout it built precisely; a slot the wall does not have is refused by the agent's tools, saying how its slots are numbered, and, in a block, ignored as a wall not here is;
  - `anchor NAME`: floating in the cluster of that cluster anchor, as above;
  - `near`, `nearest`, `looking`, `front` or `beside` alone: the same, for whoever asked, as below.

  A name is a participant's display name, in any case, which names them while nobody else in the space has it, or their handle, their name and tag, as `Ada#23`, its name in any case, which always names one identity (see `security.md`); a name several in the space share names none of them, and the agent's tools refuse it, saying the handles that name each, as `More than one person in this space is called "Samuel": say which, as Samuel#23 or Samuel#41.`. Several devices of one identity, sharing its name and tag, are one person, the first of whose devices joined. A name that only looks like a handle, such as someone called `Ada#3`, is theirs where no handle matches. A form the server does not know, a name of nobody in the space who has reported where they are, a name several share, a visitor's, and a wall not here are each ignored, and the panel goes where it would by default. Each block is placed alone: a block without a `place` goes where the default puts it, whatever its reply's other blocks say.
- **Whoever asked.** The default and a form that names nobody are for whoever the agent's turn answers; a form with a name is for that person where they are now, as their presence last said.
  - **In reply to someone's words**, nothing having reached the agent since, it is where their head was, and the way it faced, as their words began, so that a panel goes onto the wall they were looking at as they asked, though they look elsewhere as they listen. Once they have walked more than **3 m** from there (`ORIGIN_MOVED`), a few steps, beyond which a panel beside where they stood would be about 4 m from their eyes, too far to read, or their words began **60 s** ago (`ORIGIN_FRESH`), longer than a long question and a reply that calls a tool or two take, after which the agent answers after long work, it is where they are now, as it is when they did not report as they began; where they no longer report, as once they have left, it is where they were.
  - **In reply to nobody's words**, as after a sub-agent's report, a timer, an anchor's note or a message typed into the agent's session outside Tomori, it is where the participant the agent talks with, its partner as following keeps it, while they were last in conversation with it within 10 minutes (see `environments.md`), or else whoever spoke last, is now, since where they stood minutes before says nothing of where they can read it; with neither here, nobody asked.

  A reply's panels find whoever asked as it completes, and the agent's tools, `move_panel`, `update_panel`, `show_image`, `set_wall`, `add_wall`, `remove_wall`, `create_layout`, `remove_layout`, `move_agent` and `place_worker`, as they are called.
- **The layout decides the rest.** A panel put on a wall takes the slot chosen if it is free, or else the wall's most prominent free slot, and a full wall gives up a panel for it as the layout below says, so that what a participant arranged stays while the agent's own panels make room; a panel placed where nothing is free never floats instead. Since there is always the status wall, there is always a wall.
- **Moving panels.** The agent may move any panel in the space with its `move_panel` tool, by its identity or title, to a place in the same forms (see `mcp.md`), when someone asks or when it serves what they asked for, such as gathering the details someone asked to see where they can read them, and says what it moved. A form that names nobody is about whoever asked for what the agent is doing now, as above.
  - **A panel someone placed stays theirs.** A panel a participant put where it is, which is pinned, moves too, and stays pinned where it goes, and arranged on a wall, so that what the agent projects next, a full wall's room for it and the next reply's code panels treat it as they treat any panel a participant placed. A notice on the status wall, titled "Moved", says what moved and where, such as "Tomori moved 'Merging now…' to the status wall.", its title on one line and cut at 48 characters, so that whoever placed it, and everyone else, sees why it is no longer where they left it; one such notice replaces the last unless a participant has moved it, as Tomori's other notices do. Moving the agent's own panels gives no notice, since they were never anyone's.
  - **A panel carried stays in the hand.** A panel a participant is carrying is refused, saying so, until they let it go. A client says which panel it carries in its presence (`ClientMessage::Presence`'s `carrying`), which the server keeps with the rest of what it reports and relays to nobody; a report gone stale, 5 seconds old, says nothing, and an identity over 1024 bytes names no panel. An older client never says, so its participant's panel can be moved out of their hand, and they then carry nothing.
  - **Only what was asked moves.** The agent's moves never displace another panel a participant placed, but as the layout below makes room on a full wall; nothing else moves automatically.
  - **Placed from a head once, never chasing it.** Whatever the server places from where someone's head is, a panel beside or in front of them, where they look, a wall added in front of them, or the agent going to them, is placed from their presence once, as it is placed, and stays there as they move: a presence only says where they are, and moves nothing. Re-placed as their head moved, a panel would trail it by a round trip and seem to shake; see `rendering.md`.

**Layout.** The server lays walls out, and a client draws each panel in the slot it is given, in that slot's own plane on a curved wall. A wall's slots are numbered from its top left, as seen from in front of it, left to right and then down; a curved wall's, left to right.

- **The agent's panels** take their wall's most prominent free slot.
- **A participant chooses the slot.** A panel put on a wall by hand goes into the slot the participant put it in, and is then **arranged**. When that slot holds another panel, the two **change places**: the other goes into the slot the one put there came from, on the same wall; or, when it came from elsewhere, into the wall's most prominent free slot; or, with none free, to where it came from, on another wall or floating where it floated. A panel put back in its own slot stays, arranged.
- **A full wall** gives up a panel for a newcomer: the one that has been on it longest of those not arranged, or, when every one is arranged, the one there longest. It leaves, dismissed, and the newcomer takes its slot. So the agent's new panels never move what a participant arranged while there are others to make room, and arranged panels keep their slots. A panel its environment published is never the one given up (see panels an environment publishes, below): a wall whose every slot holds one takes no newcomer, so the agent's panel sent there goes onto the Tomori wall instead, as when nobody asked, and a panel moved onto it by its name, by a participant or by the agent's `move_panel`, is refused, saying that the wall is full of its environment's own panels and to put it on another wall or leave it floating. Nor does one leave its environment by changing places: a panel moved from the Tomori wall into the slot of an environment's panel, on a wall with no free slot for that panel to move along to, is refused, since it would send it onto the Tomori wall.
- **Nothing else ever moves:** a panel pulled off leaves its slot empty until the next one fills it, so that nobody's reading is disturbed. The newest panel on each wall is framed in blue, so that it is found at once.
- An older client, which names no slot, has its panel put on the wall as the agent's are, in the most prominent free slot, though arranged, and kept in its slot when put back on the wall it is on.

**Floating panels.** Squeezing the right grip while pointing at a panel pulls it off its wall; it follows the ray at the distance it was picked up from, facing the participant, as a GitHub card does (see `github.md`). **Where it will go: docking is magnetic, by nearness.** A panel carried goes onto a wall only when the panel itself is near the wall, never because the ray points at it: it docks in the slot its middle is within reach of, in front of the slot's plane by at most the **capture distance**, or behind it by at most 5 cm, as a panel pushed a little into a wall is, and within 0.25 m of its edges, in each slot's own plane, so that a curved or tilted wall's slots are docked in as they stand, whether the ray points there, at another slot or at another wall. A panel further behind a wall, at its back, docks in none of its slots, since a wall's slots face one way and a panel put in one from behind would turn about to face away from whoever put it there. Of the slots in reach it docks in the nearest to its middle across the slots' planes, how far it is beyond each slot's edges, and, of slots as near across, the nearest in depth, as of two walls one behind the other. The capture distance grows with how far the participant is from the slot, **15% of that distance, from 0.3 m to 1 m** (`projection::capture`): 0.3 m for a wall within 2 m, half a metre for one 3.3 m away, 0.75 m at 5 m, and a metre at 6.7 m or more. So putting a panel on a far wall is pushing it there with the right stick, along the ray, until it is within reach, which the push's 8 m makes walls as far as about 9 m, the old reach of the ray; a wall further away takes panels the agent places, and a participant walks or teleports nearer. Docking **holds**: a slot docked in keeps the panel until its middle is a quarter further out than it docks at, in front and beyond the edges, and another slot takes it from the one it docks in only once it is 5 cm nearer across, however deep the panel is held, so that a hand's tremor at the edge of reach, or between two slots, does not flicker it. While it docks, the slot is outlined, framed brightly and faintly filled, at the slot's size and in its plane, over any panel there, and the panel is drawn **pulled into the slot**, as it will be shown there, at the slot's size, laid out as the slot lays it out, in its middle, facing as it faces, 2 cm in front of it, gliding there over about a tenth of a second, from slot to slot as it moves along a wall, and back to the ray at its own size as it leaves, so that the participant sees it snap towards where it will go before letting go, a wide table never spills over the slots beside it, and letting go changes nothing it shows; the ray ends on it there. Out of reach nothing is outlined, the panel stays on the ray, and the hint says it would be left floating. Since docking is decided where the panel is carried, by the projector the headset's controllers, the desktop window's keyboard and mouse and any later way of carrying share, they dock alike: the desktop window's G and Page Up docks as the grip and the stick do, and hands, which carry no panel yet, are to dock by the same rule when they do. Where the agent puts its panels is unchanged (see where the agent's panels go, above). Let go docking, it goes into that slot, as the layout says, and is shown there at once, at the slot's size, until the server agrees; anywhere else it floats where it was left, upright and facing the participant, within 30 m of the space's middle, at its own size. A floating panel belongs to the environment it was left in, since where it floats means something only in that environment's geometry; one pulled off the Tomori wall stays with that environment, and is hidden when the space changes environment, unless it is put back on the Tomori wall first. At most 120 float in each environment of a space, of which each client is shown those left most recently, as many as it draws; leaving another floating takes away the one floating longest. A panel let go is shown where it was let go until the server agrees, or for 3 seconds. While the hand menu, the right wrist's transcript or a card carried takes the ray, a panel carried is let go where it is. Travel does not: a panel carried stays in the hand through a teleport, a turn and a backstep (see `locomotion.md`). When a card and a panel are both on the ray, the grip picks up the nearer, as the ray points at the nearer.

**Moving a panel carried.** While a panel is carried, the stick of the hand that carries it moves it, and nothing else, as each stick follows its own hand (see `interaction.md`):

- **Forward and back** push it further along the ray or pull it closer, smoothly, for as long as the stick is pushed, at up to 1.2 of its distance a second, by the same proportion of its distance near or far, so that it feels alike at arm's length and across the room, from 0.3 m to 8 m, about as far as the ray once reached to put a panel on a wall, so that a wall that far still takes one pushed to it (above).
- **Left and right** turn it 10° about the vertical, clockwise for right as seen from above, once a flick, as a snap turn turns the participant; it stays upright. Its turn is always a whole number of 10° snaps from facing the participant square, so square is always exactly reachable, by flicking back as many times, or at once by **clicking the right stick**, which, while a panel is carried, turns it square rather than playing the latest reply again.
- Each push is one gesture, owned by the axis it first passes its threshold in, forward or back at 0.3 from the middle and sideways at 0.6, until the stick returns within 0.2 of the middle: a push along the ray that rolls sideways turns nothing, and a flick that turned moves it nowhere.
- A push belongs to the panel if one is carried as the stick leaves the middle, and is then nobody else's until it returns there, so that the right wrist's transcript and the result cards never take a push that moved the panel, nor the panel one that moved them. The transcript does not show while a panel is carried, since turning the wrist to see it would let the panel go.
- The other stick still travels, carrying the panel along (see `locomotion.md`), or, in an environment that allows no travel, goes where its own hand's ray says (see `interaction.md`). A panel in each hand is moved by each hand's stick.
- Let go, the panel floats as far along the ray and as turned as it was carried, and the server is sent that pose, which it makes upright; docking in a slot, the slot decides its pose. Pushing it is also how it reaches a far wall, since it docks only within reach of one (above).

A 3D object carried later is to be moved the same way, by the same gestures, gaining its further axes as further snapped turns, without changing what a panel does.

**Both hands.** Either hand's grip takes hold, with the other, of a panel or a wall the other hand holds, so that one hand points and the other stretches:

- **Taking hold with the second hand.** A squeeze of one grip while its ray meets a panel the other hand carries, or a wall it carries or holds by its board, slot or bar, or a panel in one of that wall's slots, however far away, or while that hand is within **12 cm** of its face and edges (`projection::TOUCH`), wherever its ray points, takes hold of it too, felt on that hand as taking hold is, rather than being nobody's; a panel or a handle elsewhere nearer on the ray is taken as ever. It is the grip's `Carried` rung (`Projector::joins`), so that nothing of the environment's takes it.
- **Moving and turning.** Each hand keeps the point it took along its own ray, its hand's point where it touched, and what both hold follows the middle of the two, turning about the vertical as the line between the hands turns, as a wheel does, and staying upright, as a panel or wall floats; the sticks move it no further meanwhile, and B still throws a panel away.
- **Stretching and shrinking.** Spreading the hands apart grows it, and closing them shrinks it, about the middle of the two points, as many times as the hands are further apart than as the second closed, taking them as at least 10 cm apart, in steps of 5%, each taken only once the hands are a quarter of a step past halfway to it, so that hands held at the edge between two do not flicker and a size taken hold of between steps is kept until they move it: by the hands' own spread, not the points their rays hold, which move apart many times faster than the hands across a room as the wrists turn, and shake as much more, so that a panel across the room grows as one at arm's length does, and a hand held still holds its size still. A floating **panel**, or volume (see `diagrams.md`), is sized uniformly, its text and a volume's depth with it, from half its own size, so that its text is never smaller than half a standard slot's (`layout::LEAST_TEXT`), to four times (`FLOAT_SIZES`), and it says its size above it, such as `120%`, for 1.5 seconds after each change, and `· smallest` or `· largest` at either end. A **wall's** slots are sized as its corner sizes them, `scale` in the same 5% steps, within the same bounds and no larger than a wall may be, keeping its grid and shape, its corner's readout, such as `90 × 54 cm · standard`, in place of its hint; carried, its middle moves from the hands' middle as much further as it grows, and held where it stands, as the Tomori wall or an environment's own where the server does not lock walls, it grows about its own middle. The Tomori wall's slots are its own size, and a wall of a group stands from its others as it is, so neither is sized by hands: a wall of a group is carried between them, and the Tomori wall is not held with both. A locked wall is never held, so nothing of it is.
- **Marked.** What both hands hold shows small bright angles at its corners, just outside them, for as long as both hold it; reaching the least or the most it may be ticks on the hand that took hold second.
- **Letting go.** Letting go with one hand leaves it carried by the other, at its new size, from where it is, settling onto that hand's ray within about a tenth of a second rather than jumping, and turned as it was; letting go with both puts it down as one hand does, docking in a slot it is carried near or floating, or, for a wall, sending its change and snapping as a wall carried does.
- **Kept by the server.** A panel left floating at a size of its own is sent with it, `PlacePanel`'s `size`, with the version it had as the second hand took hold of it, its `revision`, so that one changed since, as by the agent rewriting it or another participant moving it, is refused, as a tick is, and drawn as the server has it at once; the server keeps it with the panel, `Panel::size`, which a move with one hand keeps, and a wall loses, since its slot sizes it. A wall's slots sized by hands are sent as its corner's are, `SetWall`'s `scale`, with the version it had as the second hand took hold of it, beside its pose where it was carried; one carried with both and not sized is sent as one carried is, with no version. Nothing is sent while both hold it, so that what they do is shown at once and sent once, as they let go.
- **Not stretched.** A card in the other hand, the farmyard's stones and sticks and other objects of the environment's are taken as before: a second grip on a card is nobody's, and the environment decides for its own, the latest grip winning for stones and sticks (see `physics.md`). The desktop window has one mouse, and holds nothing with two.

**Dismissing.** A panel is dismissed, for everyone, only by throwing it away while carrying it: pressing **B** while it is carried with the grip, and letting B go within 0.4 seconds while it is still carried, dismisses it as B is let go. **B on its own never dismisses a panel**, whatever the ray points at. A press of B while a panel is carried is the panel's, until B is let go, as a push of the right stick is: it backs out of nothing else, and a hold that began on a panel carried, however long, neither dismisses it nor leaves the space, so that letting go of B after a hold changes nothing. A panel let go of the grip before B is put down where it was, not dismissed. A panel thrown away is no longer carried, nor put down as the grip is let go, and is drawn nowhere from that moment, until the server's projection agrees, or for 3 seconds. Otherwise B backs out of what is open as ever, the list, a selected queue entry, waiting for a name, the card saying no speech was heard and a problem shown, in that order, and holding it for a second leaves the space (see `interaction.md`). Pointing at a panel, with nothing else to back out of, a participant's press of B says, on the status pill, "Grip it and press B to throw away." A dismissal cannot be undone; the panel's text stays in the transcript. A visitor carries no panel, so their **B** dismisses nothing, and says to hold B to leave, as it always does. In the desktop window, holding **G**, the right grip, carries the panel pointed at, and **Backspace**, B, throws it away as B does (see `desktop.md`).

**The hint while carrying.** While a participant carries a panel, a small hint stands just below it, or above it where below would be under the floor, 1.5 m below the eyes, or more than 40° below the eye line. It faces the participant, moves with the panel, and shows only while a panel is carried and the panels take the ray and grip, from the frame after it is pulled off, once where it would go is known. It says, for someone carrying a panel for the first time, what each control does to it, in five short rows of the control, bold, and what it does, the throw in a warm colour of its own, as it is for everyone:

```text
Stick ↕  further / closer
Stick ↔  turn
Click    square up
B        throw away, for all
Let go   leave it floating
```

The last row says what letting go of the grip would do now: "put it in the slot" where a slot is outlined and empty or the panel's own, "swap with that one" where another panel is in it, and "leave it floating" otherwise. The desktop window names its keys instead: `PgUp PgDn`, `[ ]`, `R`, `Backspace` and `Let go G`. Its font pixel takes 0.0015 radians from the eyes, whatever its distance, so that its x-height is about 0.48°, above the 0.45° text reads comfortably at (see `text.md`), alike at arm's length and across the room, and it is at most about 25° wide. It is always shown in full: a hint that grows shorter once a participant has used each control a few times on a headset is an idea for later, which would need what each headset has learned kept on it.

**Drawn by distance.** Walls and panels, the outline of a slot, the label on the ray and the hint are drawn as `rendering.md` requires: whatever is nearer the eye is in front, so that the hand menu, the pill, cards or a controller in front of a panel cover it, and a panel covers whatever is behind it, in both eyes. A panel's face, the label and the hint hide what is behind them; a wall's board and the outline of a slot are see-through. A panel carried over a slot, about as far away as the panel in it, is drawn over it and over the outline, with its hint whole below it.

**Shared.** Everything here is the space's, the same for everyone in it, participants and visitors alike. Any participant pulls off, puts back and dismisses any panel, and the server sends the result to everyone. Visitors see the walls and panels, as they see the transcript that holds the same text, but change nothing: their grip pulls nothing off.

**Text panels.** A panel has a title on one line, a body of Markdown-lite text, and a little metadata: up to four keys and values, and how long ago it was projected. Its face shows the title, bold, the metadata and age on the next row, a rule, and the body on twelve rows, 34 cells wide, styled as the right wrist's transcript styles a message, with the same renderer and the same glyph atlas (see `wrist-transcript.md` and `text.md`), ending with "…" where it is longer. Its font pixel is 1.9 mm, so a panel is about 60 by 36 cm, legible from about 3 m.

**Checklists.** A task item, as GitHub-flavoured Markdown writes one, `- [ ]` or `- [x]`, with `*` or `+` for `-`, `[X]` for `[x]`, or after a number, as `1. [ ]`, shows a box in place of its bullet, after its number where it has one: `☐`, empty and bright, for an item not done, and `☑`, ticked and green, for one done, whose text is dimmed, so that what is done recedes and what is left stands out. Both boxes are bold, one cell each, drawn from Noto Sans Symbols 2, so they are as legible as the text beside them at a panel's font pixel; a ticked item is dimmed rather than struck through, since text has no such style, and the dim colour is the one metadata uses, legible as it is. A nested item stands under the text of the item it is in, however wide that item's number, a later paragraph of an item under its own text, and a wrapped item hangs under its text, past its box. The right wrist's transcript shows task items the same way, as it shares the renderer.

**Tables.** A GitHub-flavoured Markdown table, with its header, its delimiter row and its rows, is drawn as a grid, never as its pipes and never cut. Each column is as wide as its widest cell where the table fits; otherwise each keeps at least 10 cells, or its widest cell where that is narrower, then grows to fit its longest word, then shares the room left in proportion to what it still lacks, and a cell's text wraps at words within its column onto as many rows as it needs, a row of the table being as tall as its tallest cell, and one row tall where every cell is empty, so that an empty row still shows, where it took none before. A word is broken only where it alone is wider than its column, and nothing in a table ends with "…". A cell keeps its strong text and inline code, and is aligned as its column's delimiter says, `:---` or `---` left, `:---:` centred and `---:` right, its header too. The header is bold on a faint blue bar, with a rule below it; columns are two cells apart, a faint thin line at the left edge of the second, a cell's width from the text either side; and every other row of the body is faintly striped, so that a row that wraps reads as one. Widths are counted in cells as drawn, a CJK character taking two and an emoji, drawn `?`, one, so that columns stay aligned. A `<br>` in a cell, or anywhere, breaks the line. Where the columns do not all fit at their least, as a table of many columns in a slot or on the wrist may not, it is drawn as a card for each row instead: a line of `Header: value` for each of its cells that is not empty, the header dim and bold and a wrapped value hanging two cells under it, with a faint rule between cards. On a wall a table takes the slot's 34 cells; floating, a body with a table grows to as many as 60 (below). Scrolled into a table's body, its header and the rule below it stay at the top of the face, over the rows they cover, so that every column stays named, where the face has room for two rows below them, until what follows the table pushes them up, a row at a time, as its last row passes beneath them. The right wrist's transcript draws tables the same way, at its own width.

**Floating panels fit their text.** A panel floating or carried is sized to show all of its text: as wide as its widest row of body, its title and its metadata, from 24 to 48 cells, a comfortable reading width, or to 60 for a body with a table, about a metre, 38° from 1.5 m, so that a table shows more of its columns side by side and a row of it is still read by moving the eyes rather than the head, its body wrapped there by the same rule, and as many body rows as that takes, from 3 to 30, ending with "…" beyond that. Put on a wall, it is a slot's size again, its text cut with "…" as before. Its size is pointed at and aimed with as it is drawn. How a panel is sized is worked out from the rows its body makes, the one place its content becomes rows, so that a content type added later sizes the same way. A code panel floating or carried fits its code in the same way, keeping its slot's width (below).

**Scrolling.** A panel with more rows than its face shows scrolls, for each participant alone: how far down they have scrolled it is theirs, kept on their own device, never sent, and never moves what anyone else sees, so that two people reading the same panel never fight over it.

- **Either stick, pointing.** A push of either stick that leaves the middle while its own hand's ray, shown in full, points at such a panel, that hand carrying nothing and, for the right stick, the right wrist's transcript not showing, scrolls it, and belongs to it, as every push belongs to what took it, until the stick returns within 0.2 of the middle: back scrolls down and forward up, as forward moves the transcript's focus up, smoothly, from none at 0.2 from the middle to 15 rows a second, about a slot's rows of code, pushed all the way, as the square of how far past 0.2, so that a little push reads slowly. The push goes on scrolling the panel it began on if the ray wanders off it, and pages no cards and moves no focus. A push that began elsewhere never scrolls, even once the ray comes to a panel. While a panel is carried the stick is the panel's, pushing, pulling and turning it (above), and scrolls nothing, nor does a push begun scrolling move the panel if the grip then pulls it off; pointing without carrying is otherwise free, since the stick only pages result cards there, which a push begun on a panel that scrolls no longer does. Pointing at a panel with nothing to scroll, the push travels where the environment allows it, and is otherwise never an environment's client side's, since the panel is Tomori's and comes first (see `interaction.md`). One stick scrolls at a time: the other's push meanwhile is nobody's.
- **The mouse's wheel.** In the desktop window, the wheel scrolls the panel the mouse points at, three rows a notch, down for a turn towards the participant, a touchpad's 40 pixels counting as a notch, unless a panel is carried or the transcript shows (see `desktop.md`).
- **Where it stops.** A panel scrolls from its top until its last row is its face's last, and no further, however far it is pushed; scrolling back starts at once from there. Each scroll is kept while the panel is projected, as its size changes: a code panel pulled off and grown to show all of its code shows it from the top, and put back on a wall is scrolled as it was. A panel no longer projected is forgotten.
- **What it shows.** A code panel scrolls by rows of code, so that a line taller than its face is read whole: scrolled partway through a wrapped line, its first row shown is that line's continuation, marked `↪`. Its row below the code says how many lines start above the rows shown, as `↑ 3 above`, and how many below are not shown whole, counting those it was not sent, as `↓ 4 more`; once the last line it holds is shown, it says how many lines of its block it was not sent, as `180 more lines in the transcript`, or nothing where there are none. A text panel scrolls by rows of its body, its last row shown ending with "…" while there is more below, after its text where there is room for it, never over its last character nor on a table's row, and keeping a table's header at its top (see Tables). Either, while it has more than it shows, has a slim bar at the right edge of its rows, in its padding: a faint track as tall as the rows shown and a brighter thumb as tall, and as far down, as the rows shown are of them all.
- **Saying so.** The label on the ray, while it points at a panel that scrolls, ends with a row of how to scroll it, `Stick ↕  scroll`, or `Wheel  scroll` in the desktop window, so that someone pointing at one for the first time learns it where they need it, as the hint while carrying teaches carrying; the hint while carrying says nothing of scrolling, since the stick then moves the panel.

**Pointing.** In a space, the ray points at the nearest panel it meets where nothing else it meets, a card, a worker or what the environment shows, is nearer; a panel carried is pointed at all along. It highlights the panel and ticks lightly, as for any new target (see `feedback.md`). A small label on the ray, just in front of where it meets the panel, names the panel, its metadata, how long ago it was projected and where it is. A panel pointed at as **X** is pressed is what the words are about: the client sends only its identity, `{"kind": "panel", "panel": "panel-3"}` (`Resource::panel`), with the utterance's beginning, and the server describes it from its own store, never from what a client says, before the words, as `Ada: [Pointing at panel "Build status" (on the status wall, projected 2 minutes ago; source: "CI")] explain this one`. The title, or the start of the body, and each value are in double quotes, since they are the agent's text and may come from what it read. Everything in the description is cleaned onto one line, with double quotes and square brackets made single quotes and parentheses, so that nothing in it can end the block (see `spaces.md`). A panel the server does not show in the space's environment, or an oversized identity, is dropped and the words kept. The recording indicator and status line name the panel by its title, or the start of its body.

**Pointing at an item.** The description names a panel's id, where it has one, and when the agent last updated it, as `panel "Agenda" (id agenda, on the status wall, projected 5 minutes ago, updated just now)`. Where the ray meets a row of a list item of a text panel's body as X is pressed, a row of the item's text, wrapped or nested, the client sends with the identity the line of the body, from 0, that the item begins on, as its own face lays the body out, `{"kind": "panel", "panel": "panel-3", "line": 4}` (`Resource::panel_at`), and the server describes the item from its own copy of the body, never from the client: its number of how many, whether it is ticked, for a task item, and its text, in double quotes, cleaned as the title is, as `Ada: [Pointing at panel "Agenda" (id agenda, on the status wall, projected 5 minutes ago; item 3 of 5, not ticked: "Decide the pairing window")] this one's done`, so that "this one" ticks the right item. Items are numbered from 1 in the order they are written, nested ones included, not counting lines in a fenced code block or a rule such as `- - -`, the same numbers `update_panel` takes (see `mcp.md`). A line that begins no item, one past the body, or a code panel's, adds nothing, and the panel is described as before. An older server ignores `line`. Pointing anywhere else on a panel, or at a code panel, sends its identity alone, as before. A row of an environment's list panel is described as its row instead, its number of how many rows, whether it is highlighted and the id to inspect it by (see `panel-actions.md`).

**Reading a chart by pointing.** Pointing at a chart panel's plot, with the right ray, the left ray keeping its label there, or, in the desktop window, the mouse, shows a readout there, for that participant alone, nothing of it sent: a thin line down the plot at the time pointed at, a dot on each series at its value there, and a small label beside the line saying the time, as the axis says it, and each series' name and value with its unit, in its colour. Scanning the ray from left to right follows it smoothly, the time and values changing as it goes, and the readout disappears as the ray leaves the plot, onto the chart's title, legend or axis labels, or off the panel; the label on the ray does not show while it does, as the readout says more. Where the ray points is a time along the axis, a point or a share of the way between two, to the nearest font pixel across the plot, so that a hand nearly still keeps the readout as it is: each series is read as it is drawn there. A line's or an area's value is read on the straight line between its two points; past the last point of a run its line draws, before a gap or a break, nothing is read, but for a point alone, drawn as a dot, read within half a point of it; a line of more points than it is drawn with, thinned to be drawn, is read on the line drawn, its dot on it. A step's is the value held from its point; a bar's the bar drawn nearest, which on a chart of more than 128 points is one for each stretch of points, as tall as the one of them farthest from its base. A gap reads `no data`, and is never drawn or said as zero. Values are kept to four figures, grouped by thousands, as `203,800 a minute`. The time is read from the two axis labels about it that say a time of one kind, relative, as `-15 min`, `now` or `2 h ago`, or a clock time, as `14:05`, and written as they are, to the second where the points are closer than the labels' unit, as `-6 min 25 s` between `-10 min` and `-5 min` or `14:03:25` between `14:00` and `14:05`; labels that say no time give the label at the point nearest, or `point 121 of 181`. The label stands right of the line, or left of it where there is no room, at the plot's top or its bottom, whichever covers fewer of the series' values under it, and inside the panel always, its lines cut to fit: at most five lines, its time and each series, of at most 40 characters. It is drawn each frame as an overlay of its own in the face's plane, over the chart's face, from where the face's plot was drawn and the chart's values, so that scanning neither lays the chart out nor draws or meshes its face again (see `rendering.md`). A chart pointed at as **X** is pressed says, with its identity, where across its time axis the ray met its plot, in ten-thousandths from its oldest point to its newest, `{"kind": "panel", "panel": "panel-3", "across": 6000}` (`Resource::chart_at`), and the server reads the time and each series' value there from its own copy of the chart, as the readout does, but for a thinned line, which it reads from its points, and adds them to its description, in double quotes, as the environment's text: `Ada: [Pointing at chart panel "Root admissions" (on a wall, published 3 minutes ago; the observe environment's; shows: "admitted: 2600 a minute now"; at the time pointed at: "-6 min: admitted 203,800 a minute")] what was it here?`, at most 240 characters, cleaned onto one line as the rest of it is, quotes and brackets in a series' name with it. An older server ignores `across`; an older client sends none, and the chart is described as before.

**The linked time cursor.** While a readout shows on a chart pointed at, every other chart that shares its time shows a readout linked to it, at the same time: its own line down its plot there, a dot on each series and its label saying the time and each value, as its own readout would, with its line and frame fainter, so that the chart pointed at stands out. Charts are linked only by what their time axes say, `Chart::time` (`tomori_protocol::chart::TimeAxis`), never by their labels, which are only shown, in whatever form and zone the environment writes them: a live chart, `TimeAxis::Live { step_ms }`, whose last point is now and each point that many milliseconds after the one before, links to the live charts on the same wall as it, by how long before now, so that ten minutes ago on a 15-minute chart is ten minutes ago on an hour's chart beside it; and a chart of a fixed window, `TimeAxis::Fixed { from_ms, to_ms }`, its first and last points at absolute instants in milliseconds since the Unix epoch in UTC, links to every other chart of a fixed window in the space, wherever it stands, by the instant, where their windows overlap, never by the time of day, so that 02:30 one day never links to 02:30 the next, nor a window in one zone to another's. A live chart never links to a fixed window, nor one on another wall, floating or carried; a chart whose axis says no time, or a time it does not reach by more than half a point, shows none. Live charts link on the assumption that each one's last point is now: charts their environment updates at different rates may be up to one update apart there, a few seconds on a live console, which matters little on a chart of minutes. The server keeps a time axis only where it is sound, a step from 1 ms to a day and a window that ends after it begins, within the years 0 to 9999, however few points the chart has, and drops it otherwise, keeping the chart; it is part of the chart's structure, which its version covers. The time is found on the chart pointed at, and on each other chart, from its time axis alone (`Chart::time_of`, a `ChartTime`, and `Chart::at_time`), so that it needs nothing of the server beyond the chart. It is each participant's own, as the readout is, and nothing of it is sent. At most 8 charts (`projection::LINKED`) are linked, those first in the projection's order. Each linked readout is an overlay drawn over its chart's face as it was last drawn, as the readout is, never laying out, drawing or meshing a face again, and is kept and handed over again while it shows the same, as while the ray is still. The agent's description of the chart pointed at stays on it and adds, after its reading of at most 240 characters, what at most 3 of the charts that share its time on the server's own copy of the projection read then, each its title and reading, in quotes, at most 120 characters, as `; linked charts at that time: "Provisioning: -10 min: ready 12"`, so that the linked readings add at most 400 characters with their separators.

**How the agent creates panels.** The agent projects a panel by writing a fenced code block whose language is `panel` in its reply:

````text
```panel
title: Build status
source: CI
---
**3 crates** failed.
```
````

- **The format.** An optional header of `key: value` lines, each key a letter and up to 23 more letters, digits, `-` or `_`, ended by a line of `---`; then the body. Without such a header the whole block is the body, so prose with a rule in it stays prose. `title` is the panel's title; `id` is the agent's own name for it, by which it updates it in place (below); `place` says where it goes, as above; of `id` and `place`, the first a block gives decides, and neither is ever metadata; every other key is its metadata, lower case, the first of each kept, at most four.
- **Bounds.** Everything in a block is untrusted. A block larger than 8 KiB is not projected, nor one with neither a title nor a body, nor more than four from one reply. A title is cut to 80 characters, a key to 24 and a value to 80, each on one line as text others choose is cleaned (see `security.md`), without format characters, and a body to 30 lines and 1,200 characters, each line cleaned the same way; each ends with "…" where it is cut. The body is drawn as text, never as HTML.
- **Where it is found.** The server finds a reply's panel blocks when the reply completes, not while it streams, from its Markdown: a fence of three or more backticks or tildes, indented at most three spaces, whose info string starts with `panel`, closed by a fence of the same character at least as long, or else running to the end of the reply, as CommonMark reads it. A block of another language is skipped whole, so that a panel block quoted inside one is not projected. Each reply is projected once, as it completes, or, for one that completed while the backend was reconnecting, as the session is read again or the message restored; the history a space reads when it is bound again after a restart is not projected again.
- **Speech and the transcript.** A panel block is a code block, so speech skips it, as it skips every code block (see `speech.md`), while the transcript keeps the reply as the agent wrote it, the block shown as code.
- **The agent's instructions.** Tomori's own layer of the agent's instructions (see `spaces.md`) says that it may project a text panel when something is better read than heard, such as a list, steps, a summary or figures, in a fenced block of language `panel`, with its header, a line of dashes and a short Markdown body; that Tomori shows it without reading it aloud and keeps it in the transcript, so it says in a sentence what it shows; that it uses at most one or two in a reply, and only when they help; and that a participant may point at one as they speak. It says that a Markdown table in a panel is drawn as a grid, its long cells wrapped, but to keep it compact, with few columns and short cells, since a panel too narrow for its columns shows each row as lines of `Header: value` instead, and that a list of label: detail lines often reads better in a headset. It says to give a panel it will revise an `id` header line, that a later block with the same id, or `update_panel`, updates that panel where it is, rather than projecting another, and to project an agenda, questions to cover or a to-do list as a checklist and tick its items off as the conversation covers them; and that when someone points at an item of a list, Tomori names the panel's id and which item it is.

**Panels the agent updates.** A text panel may have an id, the agent's own name for it, by which the agent updates it in place rather than projecting it again, so that, for example, an agenda stays where everyone is reading it while its items are ticked off as a conversation covers them:

- **Ids.** A header line `id: agenda` gives one: from 1 to 32 ASCII letters, digits, `-` and `_`, read in lower case, scoped to the space. A value that is not one names nothing, and the panel is projected without an id. Only text panels take ids; code panels, which a reply's next code panels replace, do not. The server keeps the id with the panel, and sends it as `Panel::slug`.
- **Updating in place.** A later panel block with the id of a text panel the space shows in its current environment, on the Tomori wall, on the environment's walls or floating in it, replaces that panel's title, body and metadata, bounded and cleaned as any block's, and keeps everything else: its identity, its wall and slot or where it floats, whether a participant put it there, its place in its wall's order of which leaves first, and its age, since it was projected. So it is not a new panel: it takes no slot, makes no room, is not framed as its wall's newest, and moves nothing else. The server keeps when it last updated it. A block the same as what the panel shows changes nothing. Two blocks with the same id in one reply project it, then update it.
- **Moving it.** An update whose block says `place:` moves the panel there, as `move_panel` does, unless a participant put it where it is, which always stays, or is carrying it, which stays in their hand; without one, it stays where it is, wherever whoever asked stood or looked.
- **Dismissed, or elsewhere.** A block with the id of no panel shown projects a new panel, placed as any new one is, so that a list dismissed comes back where new panels go. A panel with that id kept hidden with another environment the space has been in is forgotten as the new one is projected, so that an id names at most one panel in a space.
- **Clients.** The change reaches every client as any change of the projection does, the panel with the same identity and its new text, which a client draws in place of the old, laying its body out again, with no flicker, no new frame and nothing moved; a client keeps what it holds for a panel by its identity, such as one let go but not yet confirmed. An older client, which knows no ids, draws the new text the same way.
- **Through the tools.** `panels` reads back each panel's id, body, items and version, and `update_panel` changes a text panel's title or body, or ticks and unticks its items by number or text, without resending it, keeping it where it is in the same way (see `mcp.md`).
- **Versions.** Each panel has a version, the projection revision at which it last changed (see versions and compare-and-swap, below), sent with it as `Panel::version`: the revision it was projected at, and then the revision of each change to its title, body or items, by a block with its id, by `update_panel` or by a participant's tick, and of each move. A change that leaves it as it was changes none. A participant's tick, and the agent's `update_panel`, may say the version they saw; **one that says a version the panel has changed since is refused, and changes nothing**, so that a tick or an item named by its number never lands on another item of a body changed since it was read. Tomori does not look for the item elsewhere in the body, by its text or otherwise: the participant, or the agent, reads the panel as it is now and does it again.

**Code panels.** Every other fenced code block in a reply, of any language but `panel`, is projected as a code panel, onto the same wall as the reply's text panels, with them, in the order they are written:

- **Where it goes.** As a text panel goes: by default where whoever asked was looking, or where a `place=` attribute in its info string says, as a word of its own, `place=FORM:NAME`, or, to hold spaces, `place="FORM NAME"`, such as `rust place=near:Ada` or `python place="looking Ada Lovelace"`. The attribute is taken out of the info string, whether the server knows its form or not, so that it is neither the panel's title nor its language.
- **What it shows.** Its title is the block's info string, such as `rust` or `python title=area.py`, cleaned and cut as a text panel's title is, or `code` when it has none; its language is the info string's first word, in lower case. Its body is the block's first 200 lines, from the first that is not blank and without the blank lines it ends with, numbered from 1, each with its tabs expanded to stops of four, format characters removed, control characters and other spaces made plain spaces, and trailing spaces dropped, which a participant scrolls through (above). Its lines are kept whole, however long, and cut with "…", the lines after left out, only where they reach 8,000 characters together. It says how many lines the block has, so that one longer than it holds says how many more lines are only in the transcript. Its face shows the title, bold, a row of its language, how many lines and how long ago it was projected, a rule, and the lines in monospace, each after its number, dim, in a calm palette on the panel's dark face, at a font pixel of 1.36 mm, so that the same 60 by 36 cm holds 48 columns and 16 rows of code, legible from about 2 m, and a row below them saying what is above and below them.
- **Long lines wrap.** A line longer than the panel's 45 columns of code wraps, softly, rather than running off its edge or being cut: it breaks after a space, or after punctuation that ends or opens a part of it, such as a comma, an operator or an opening bracket, but never before a closing one, at the last such place in the latter half of the row and after its indentation, and otherwise, as for a token longer than a row, at the row's end, never through a wide character. The spaces at a break are dropped. Each later row of the line hangs under it, at its own indentation and one indent step more, the least indentation of the block's lines, of two to four spaces, or four where none is indented, keeping at least 16 columns for code; it has no number, but a dim `↪` in the number's place, so that it reads as the same line. A line wraps onto as many rows as it needs, every one after the first marked so, and is never cut short with "…" while there are rows for it; a token longer than a row breaks at the row's end rather than being cut. Every character keeps its kind, so highlighting carries across a break. A line that fits is drawn as before. A code panel on a wall keeps its slot's size, wrapped or not: below the first line shown, a line that does not fit whole in what is left of its 16 rows is left for the row below them, which counts it among those below, as `↓ N more`, counting every line not shown in full; and a first line shown that alone is taller than what is left shows what fits, ending with a dim "…", the rest of it reached by scrolling.
- **Floating, it fits its code.** A code panel floating or carried keeps its 48 columns and is as tall as its rows of code, wrapped as above, from 3 to 40 rows, so that at most it is about 75 cm tall, as tall as a text panel floating at most, about 28° from 1.5 m, comfortably within view at the distances panels are carried; it has a row below its code only where it does not show all of its block, then scrolling as on a wall. The 17 lines of the block the maintainer could not see, which wrap onto about 25 rows, show whole. Put back on a wall, it is a slot's size again, scrolled as it was. Its size is pointed at, aimed with and hinted beside as it is drawn.
- **Highlighting, on the server.** The server highlights the code; a headset never parses it. `tomori-highlight` classifies Rust, Ruby, JavaScript, TypeScript and TSX, Python, JSON, YAML, Markdown, Bash and TOML with tree-sitter, each with its official grammar crate and that grammar's own highlights query, Markdown by its block structure alone; every other language syntect's default syntaxes know, such as C, Go, Java, HTML or SQL, with syntect; and any other, or none, is plain. Both map what they find onto one small set of token kinds, `TokenKind`: keyword, string, comment, number, function, type, variable, operator, punctuation, constant, attribute and plain. Only the first 200 lines are highlighted, up to 8,080 characters of them together, more than are held, so that a line cut where it is held is still highlighted as it continues, within 16 KiB, which they always fit in but for characters of more than two bytes, and 50 ms from when its grammar or syntax is ready, which happens once, after which the rest is plain; the server prepares the grammars on a thread of their own as it starts serving, rather than as the first code panel arrives.
- **Highlighting holds nothing up but its own space.** The server never highlights while it holds the hub's lock, which every space and connection shares, nor on a scheduler thread. It reads a completed reply's blocks under the lock, which is quick, and highlights their code afterwards on a thread of its own, then projects the reply's panels, all together, under the lock again. Only the space's own next agent event waits for it, so that each reply's panels are projected in the order the replies completed, and a later reply's code panels are never overtaken by an earlier one's slow highlighting. One event highlights at most four blocks, each bounded as above, so at most about 200 ms: when one event completes several replies, as reading the session again after a gap may, only the code panels of the last of them with any are highlighted, since those replace the others' as they are projected together, and the others' are projected plain. A code panel whose highlighting cannot start, because the system will not give it a thread, or fails, is projected plain; the panels of a reply that completes as its space stops, while they are highlighted, as when the server shuts down, are not projected.
- **What a client is sent.** The text and its spans, never colours: each span `[start, length, kind]`, in characters of the body, inside one line, in order and none overlapping, at most 2,048. A client draws code whose spans are not valid as plain text, and maps each kind onto the one palette every client shares, drawing coloured text with the glyph atlas, as it draws every panel.
- **Fitting in one frame.** A projection is sent in one frame of at most the connection's frame limit, 1 MiB for an older client, which its ninety code panels could not all fill with 200 lines each, and 32 MiB for Tomori's own, which ninety fill whole and nine hundred, as many as a space shows, could not. The server sends every code panel whole where the projection, as JSON, fits in that frame less 4 KiB; where it would not, it sends as many as need be **short**, the least recently put where they are first, so that those most likely being read are whole: a short panel holds its first 16 lines, cut where they reach 720 characters together, with at most 256 spans over them, as much as a slot shows, as code panels held before they scrolled, and keeps its block's length, so that it says how many lines are only in the transcript. A projection of every code panel short at every limit fits in a frame: about 826 KB of today's ninety, and of the server's nine hundred within 32 MiB. Where every code panel short is still too long, as only a frame of 1 MiB is whose ninety panels are an environment's 32 charts, each the largest a chart can be, among the largest text panels, it sends as many chart panels as need be without their charts, the least recently put where they are first: each is then the text panel of its summary, as a client that cannot draw charts shows it, the same panel at its version and data seq, sent with its chart again once there is room. A frame of 32 MiB holds every chart there can be. It checks cheaply first, from a bound on each panel's JSON that reads its text without writing it, within a kilobyte of what the panel takes, and measures the JSON only where that bound passes the frame's and the projection is short enough, within 4 MiB by its bounds, that measuring it takes a few milliseconds: one far longer, as a space full of the largest code panels is, over 40 MB, is first fitted by the bounds alone, to its frame or to 4 MiB, whichever is longer, so that fitting it takes 18 ms where measuring it took 70, and a frame of 1 MiB still holds as much as it can. Room is left in the frame for the message around the projection, 4 KiB, and for the versions and data seqs its walls and panels are given once it is fitted, 32 bytes each. What it keeps of each panel is unchanged, so that a panel sent short is sent whole again once there is room.
- **Clutter.** A reply's code panels replace the code panels of the replies before it, on every wall, but those a participant has pulled off and left floating, put back on a wall or moved onto another, which are pinned where they are; a reply without code panels replaces none. Text panels are never replaced, and persist until dismissed. A block of a single line, such as a command, is not projected, and stays in the transcript: it reads as well there, and agents write many, so a panel for each would push useful panels off the walls and replace the reply before's code panels with a command. At most four code panels are projected from one reply, as at most four text panels are; later blocks stay only in the transcript. A pinned code panel says so on its face and its label.
- **Pointing.** A code panel is pointed at as a text panel is, its label naming its title, its language and how many lines, and when and where it was projected, and attended to as `Resource::panel`, which the server describes from its own store with its language and the lines it holds, as `Ada: [Pointing at code panel "rust" (on the status wall, projected just now; language: "rust"; lines 1–200 of 240)] what does this do?`. How far a participant has scrolled is their own, so it is not said.
- **Speech and the transcript.** Unchanged: speech skips every code block, and the transcript keeps the reply as written.
- **The agent's instructions.** Tomori's layer adds that any other fenced code block of more than one line, with its language after the fence, appears as a code panel on the same wall, highlighted and titled with its language, which the code panels of its next reply with any replace unless a participant moved it; so that it shows code only when seeing it helps, in a few short blocks, and says in a sentence what each is. It also says that a panel goes onto the wall whoever asked for it was looking at, if near enough to read, or else floats beside them, so that they can read it wherever they stand, and how to place it elsewhere, with a `place` header line in one of the forms above, `beside NAME` among them, or a `place=` attribute after a code block's language; and that people's names may repeat, so that it names one of two who share a name by the handle `presence` gives, such as `Ada#23`, but speaks of them by name alone.

**Image panels.** The agent shows an image, such as a render, a diagram, a screenshot or a preview, with its `show_image` tool (see `mcp.md`), as an image panel, which is laid out, carried, swapped, pinned, thrown away and pointed at as every panel is, and kept until dismissed, as a text panel is: code panels never replace it.

- **The source.** A file on the server's machine, by a path absolute or relative to the space's directory, which is the agent's working directory: any readable regular file, its symbolic links followed, since the agent can already read whatever the server can with its own tools, so confining it to some directories would protect nothing. What the path names is checked before it is opened, so that a device, even one a link points to, is never opened, and the file is opened without waiting and read only so far, so that a pipe or a device put in its place never holds the thread reading it, and is refused as too large or not an image; and refused if missing, a folder, unreadable, over 20 MB, or not a PNG, JPEG, WebP or GIF by its content, whatever its name. Nothing is fetched from the network. A refusal is the tool's result, saying why, so the agent learns of it; the path is at most 4,096 bytes.
- **Made safe on the server.** The server decodes the file with the `image` crate's pure Rust decoders, a GIF's first frame only, within 16,384 pixels on a side, 50 megapixels and 512 MB of memory, so that a small file claiming an enormous image is refused before it is decoded; prepares one image at a time, on a thread of its own, never holding the hub's lock or a scheduler thread; turns it as its EXIF orientation says; lays it over white where it is transparent, since most diagrams and screenshots with transparency are drawn for a light page; scales it down, keeping its shape, to at most 2,048 pixels on its longer side; and encodes it again as a PNG of 8-bit RGB without metadata, halving it until it is at most 8 MB. That PNG is the only format a client decodes. Its title is the agent's, or else the file's name, and its caption the agent's, each cleaned onto one line and cut, the caption at 240 characters.
- **Kept by content.** The server keeps each image as `HASH.png`, named by its SHA-256, in the `images` folder of its state directory, readable only by its user, and the latest 64 MB in memory; an image read again is checked against its name. A panel keeps the image's hash, size and length with the space, never the original path, so that it survives a restart. An image no kept panel shows, in any space and environment, is removed once it is a minute old, as the kept spaces are written, so that one just made for a panel not yet projected is not removed under it.
- **Sent apart, on request, by hash.** A panel names its image, `Panel::image`, by hash, size and length; a client fetches it with `FetchImage` and keeps it by its hash, so that each image crosses once however many panels show it, and a client that reconnects keeps those it has. The server sends an image only to a client whose `Hello` sets `images`, and only one a panel shown in the client's own space has, visitors included: what a client may fetch is what its space shows, and a request for any other, or for one no longer kept, is dropped without an error, since it happens innocently, as when a panel is dismissed while its image is on its way. It sends the image after everything else waiting on the connection, control, agent speech and participant audio, in `Image` frames of at most 32 KiB, one image's in order before the next's, so that an image never holds up anything else for longer than a chunk takes to send; at most 32 images wait for one connection. A client asks for at most two at a time, asks again after 20 seconds, at most three times until it reconnects, checks each image against its hash, dropping one that is not what it says, and keeps the latest 64 MB, never forgetting one shown.
- **Older clients.** A client whose `Hello` does not set `images` is sent each image panel as a text panel of its caption, followed by "*An image, W × H pixels: this headset needs an update to show it.*", and is never sent an `Image` frame, which it would refuse.
- **Drawn.** Its face shows its title, its size, metadata and age, a rule, the image and its caption, below it, on at most two rows in a slot and three floating. In a wall's slot it is a slot's size, the image as large as fits in the rows above its caption, keeping its shape, centred, with space either side or above and below. Floating or carried, it grows to the image's own shape, as large as fits within 48 columns and 30 rows, as wide as its title and metadata, and no narrower than 24 columns. Until its image arrives, the face shows where it will be, a little lighter than the face. The image is drawn over the face, in its plane, as opaque, before any panel in front of it, so that what is nearer still covers it (see `rendering.md`).
- **Sharp, steady and bounded on the headset.** Each image is decoded on a thread of the renderer's own, never on a frame, with every mipmap level made in linear light, each pixel the average of all it covers in the level above, so that it neither darkens as it shrinks nor shimmers far away, nor loses its right or bottom edge where a level's width or height is odd, and uploaded, at most one a frame, as an sRGB texture, sampled trilinearly and anisotropically; into a target that is not sRGB, as stored. One texture serves every panel showing the image. Textures are kept within 128 MB: one no panel has shown for about ten seconds, 900 frames, is let go, as a dismissed panel's is; when a new one does not fit, those not shown this frame go first, the least recently shown first, and then the new one is uploaded without its largest levels, down to 256 pixels on its longer side, and otherwise waits, decoded, for room, its panel showing where it will be. The image is sent at its own size, up to 2,048 pixels, whatever the slot, and is never cut down otherwise, so that floating, carried or grown it stays sharp; but in a wall's slot it is drawn far smaller than that: in a 1×1 wall's slot at scale 1.6, 1.5 m away, a 960 × 1440 image is about 132 × 197 of a Quest 3's eye's pixels, at about 13 pixels per degree, and floating 0.7 m away about 466 × 700, a tall image narrower than a wide one. Its detail is then averaged, not lost by aliasing or drawn as blocks, and what is finer than the eye's pixels, such as a render's small text, cannot be read however sharp the image is; `show_image`'s description therefore asks the agent to crop to what matters and keep text large (see `mcp.md`).
- **Pointing.** An image panel is pointed at as a text panel is, its label naming its size, and attended to as `Resource::panel`, which the server describes from its own store with its size and caption, as `Ada: [Pointing at image panel "Session browser" (on the status wall, projected just now; 1600 × 900 pixels; caption: "The layout, rendered")] make the folders narrower`.

**Volumes.** A volume is a panel with depth, showing a diagram of nodes, edges and groups the agent describes, in a `diagram` block or with `show_diagram`, and the server lays out: `Panel::diagram`, its body the diagram's outline, which a client that draws no volumes shows, and is sent alone where `Hello::diagrams` is unset or past `Limits::volumes` and `Limits::nodes`. It is laid out, carried, docked, thrown away, kept and placed as every panel is, and pointing at its nodes and edges names them; its world edges, leaders to things in the space, are sent only where `Hello::world_edges` is set, and `place: near references` stands it between whoever asked and what it points at; `design/diagrams.md` says how.

**Panels an environment publishes.** An environment's server side may publish panels onto its own walls, as `environments.md` describes, by ids it chooses, each a text panel, a list panel, whose body the server writes from rows the environment names and highlights by id, which it may lay out in columns, as a table under a header drawn as a text panel's table is (see `panel-actions.md`), or a chart panel (below), for everyone in the space:

- **Where.** Onto one of the walls the environment declares, by its place in their order, in the slot it prefers where that is free, or else in that wall's most prominent free slot, or else in the place of the agent's panel there longest; never onto another environment's wall, the Tomori wall or a wall an owner added, and never past a wall's slots. A wall full of panels participants arranged and the environment's own refuses another, saying so, and none of the environment's own on it is ever given up for another panel. A panel published is arranged, so that the agent's newcomers make room by giving up the agent's own panels first.
- **Updated in place.** Publishing an id the environment published before replaces what that panel shows, its title, its body and its chart, and keeps its identity, where it is, wherever a participant or the agent moved it, whether a participant put it there, its place in its wall's order and its age, as the agent's updates by id do, and changes its version, as the agent's update does, but for a chart's live data, which changes only its data seq, so that a live chart refuses nobody's change (see versions and compare-and-swap, below); the same again changes nothing. Only its environment changes what it shows: `update_panel` and a participant's tick are refused, saying that it is its environment's. An environment's ids and the agent's are apart: a `panel` block or `update_panel` with an id an environment's panel has names the agent's own panel of that id, or makes one, and never touches the environment's, nor the environment's publishing the agent's.
- **Shared and kept as the agent's are.** Participants pull it off, carry it, put it in another slot of the environment's walls or an added wall, leave it floating, point at it and throw it away as any panel; the agent moves it with `move_panel`. It never becomes a status panel, which would take it out of its environment: placing it on the space's own status wall, by hand, or as a status panel by the agent, is refused, saying so; on an environment's own wall in the status role it stays the wall's. It goes onto an added wall, snapped into a group or not, as any panel; filling a layout's slots places only the agent's own panels, and taking a layout down leaves an environment's panel on it floating where it was, as it leaves every panel, never removing it.
- **Dismissed, it stays away.** A panel a participant throws away stays away, its updates ignored, until the environment retracts it and publishes it again, the space comes back to the environment or the server restarts, but not when its side fails: a live panel updated every few seconds would otherwise come straight back, and throwing a panel away is a decision for everyone (above). A full wall never gives one up for a newcomer, the agent's or anyone's (see a full wall, above). One that leaves as too many float stays away the same way: more than 12 floating, the one floating longest goes, whoever's it is, so that the agent's panels floated beside someone can push away an environment's panel a participant left floating, which then stays away until the environment retracts it and publishes it again, the space comes back to the environment or the server restarts (see open questions). The environment is not told; it may retract a panel and publish it again when it has reason to show it, since its walls are its own.
- **Told to its environment.** Every change of the wall one of its panels is on, and its dismissal, is told to the environment's side, as an intent the server makes, `Intent::moved()`, with the environment's id for the panel and where it is now, one of the environment's own walls by its order and slot, another wall, floating or dismissed, whatever made it: a participant moving it, or dropping another into its slot so that it swaps, a wall removed or shrunk, the agent's `move_panel`, the floating bound or a full wall giving it up, or its being put back, as the side publishes it again, somewhere other than the wall it asks for, as where it rested; a participant's as theirs, within their rate of intents, the rest as nobody's. Moves between one wall's slots, a floating panel moved on, a retraction and a run ending are not told (see `panel-actions.md`).
- **Only what is current.** What an environment published is shown only while its side's run in the space publishes it. When the run ends, as the space leaves the environment, the server restarts or its side fails or finishes, each of its panels rests at once, and any update of it still waiting is dropped: it is shown no more, holds no slot, so that the agent's panels and participants' may take it, and counts towards no limit. Published again by the next run, it is the same panel, back in its slot where that is still there and free, or floating where it floated, or else placed as a new one is. A side that has failed or finished changes no panels from then on, whatever thread it left running calls, until it runs again for a new binding, as the space comes back to the environment or the server restarts. One that 3 runs in turn which publish others end without publishing again is forgotten, so that a panel a side shows only now and then, as an alert, keeps its place a while, while a run that publishes nothing counts for nothing, and at most 64 rest in an environment, twice as many as it shows, those missed by the most runs forgotten first, so that panels a side stopped publishing never pile up.
- **Bounds.** At most 32 shown in a space (`tomori_environment::PANEL_LIMIT`), enough for a console of several terminals, each a few charts and a list, each cleaned and bounded as the agent's panels are, a title of 80 characters on one line and a body of 30 lines and 1,200 characters, drawn as text and never as HTML, its chart as below. Changes are limited, so that an environment publishing as fast as it can never floods the space's clients: each change sends the panel again, as its live data alone to a client that applies data ops, whole in a patch to one that applies patches without them, and in the projection whole to one that applies none (see revisions and patches, and versions and compare-and-swap, below), coalesced for a connection that has not yet taken the last. **Told four times a second.** What an environment's side changed is told to the space's connections at most once every 250 ms (`PROJECTION_INTERVAL`, in the server's `hub.rs`): a change after a quiet interval is told at once, and one within the interval of the last waits for its end, when each connection not yet sent the space's revision is sent it as it then is, with every change since, the newest winning; a participant's or the agent's change is told at once, as ever, and carries whatever waited. What waits is due on the server's snapshot timer, with spaces' deferred states and the agents' next moves (`environments.md`), which is woken as a change is deferred, so that it is sent as its interval ends though nothing else happens in the space. So each kind of client is sent, of an environment's changes, at most four messages a second: one that applies data ops, a patch of the points its charts gained, a few hundred bytes each, and any text or list panel that changed, whole, within the 128 KiB a second below; one that applies patches without data ops, each changed panel whole, within that 128 KiB a second, or, where more than half of what it is shown changed since it was last sent, as only a projection of a few panels does once its first 32 are up, the projection whole; and one that applies no patches the projection whole each time, within today's limits and so at most 1 MiB, at most 4 MiB a second, as it was sent at the 4 changes a second a space was held to before. A projection waits in a connection's outbox behind the speech and audio already queued, and holds up only what is queued after it begins, for as long as one frame takes to send (see `server.md`): at most four frames a second, for a client that applies patches a few kilobytes each. Only a change the stage makes counts: a panel added, updated, or taken away; the same again, an update of a panel dismissed, one refused and a retraction of nothing cost nothing. A panel changes at most about once a second, 0.9 s apart at the least, and a space's panels at most 16 times a second, after a burst of 32, and by 128 KiB a second, after a burst of 512 KiB, a larger panel waiting until the bytes are there; and a side may publish or retract at most 80 times a second, after a burst of 160, whatever it changes. A space's rates are defined from the panels a side may show, and grow with them: as many changes at once as it has panels, so that it puts up, or updates, every one together, and half as many each second after; 16 KiB at once for each panel, more than a chart of every series at every point takes, about 13 KiB, and a quarter of that each second, 8 KiB for each change, so that 32 live charts of three series over 181 points, under 4 KiB each, are within what may change each second; and five calls at once and two and a half a second for each panel, so that a side publishing every one each second, on a timer, whether or not each changed, is never refused for how often it calls. An update that comes too soon is not lost: it waits, one for each panel, at most 32, the newest replacing the one before in its place, and is made as soon as it may be, at the side's next change or the hub's next sweep, about every second, so that the latest a side published always arrives. What can be known before it is made is checked at once, as for one made at once, and refused saying why: a wall the environment does not declare, a slot it does not have, and more than 32 panels, counting those waiting, as `An environment publishes at most 32 panels in a space.`; what changes meanwhile, such as its wall filling with panels participants arranged, drops it once it may be made, said in the server's log once for each panel until it is taken or retracted, at most 64 panels remembered, the earliest forgotten first. Updates waiting that are due are made first, the oldest first, and a fresh one after them, at once while the space has changes left, and otherwise waits behind them, so that none is starved: a side updating its 32 panels every second, twice what a space may change, has each one's latest made within 2 seconds. A retraction takes away any update of its panel waiting, and the panel may be published again at once. It needs a slot for each on the walls it declares, which hold 36 as they are, three by two, unless it gives them larger grids; with the Tomori wall's six they count towards the 78 slots a headset is sent (see limits stated in Hello, below). A projection at every limit with 32 charts of every series at every point, each name as long as it may be, fits in one frame: about 4 MB as the server holds it among the largest text panels, and, as an older client is sent it, today's ninety panels, 980 KB among the largest text panels and 1,009 KB among code panels sent short, every version at its longest, within 1 MiB. So many of the largest chart there can be, its lines broken after every point too, about 22 KiB each, would take 1,094 KB of it, so three of them are sent as their summaries, and the frame is 1,041 KB (see fitting in one frame, above).
- **What the agent is told.** Pointing at one tells the agent whose it is and, where the environment gave it a reference, the id it may inspect it by, and a chart what it shows now, as `Ada: [Pointing at chart panel "Scaling pressure" (on a wall, published 3 minutes ago, updated just now; the observe environment's, id observe:chart/pressure; shows: "mean: 0.73 now, greatest: 1.7 now, target: 1")] why is it above the target?`. `panels` lists it with its kind, its environment's id for it and that id (see `mcp.md`), and `inspect` asks the environment about it by that id, as about what it describes.
- **An older server.** A record written with panels an environment published, read by a server from before them, which ignores what it does not know, simply has no such panels: what rests is written apart from the panels, without their text, so no environment's panel comes back as an agent's that could be edited. An accepted limit: going back to an older server forgets where they rested.

**Chart panels.** A chart panel shows a chart, a small native spec built for live data, `tomori_protocol::chart::Chart`, rather than an image or Vega-Lite, which may come later on #82: evenly spaced points along one time axis, at most 256, such as 15 minutes at 5 seconds, 181; up to 8 labels along it, under the points they name, such as `-15 min` and `now`; one value axis, its lowest and highest values each fixed or found, from 0, or its lowest value below 0, to a round number above its highest value, or its reference lines' with a third to spare, and its unit; up to 4 series, each a name, a colour of a small palette (teal, amber, blue, violet, green, red, grey, white) or RGB, a style, a line, steps, a line with its area filled faintly, or bars, a width, from 0.5 to 4 font pixels, whether it is dashed, and its value at each point, a gap where it has none, with the points after which its line breaks though both are measured, as where what it counts changes; up to 4 reference lines, such as a target of 1.0, each a value, a label and a colour; up to 4 shaded regions, each between two series or a series and a reference line, optionally only where one is above the other, such as the pressure above its target or the replicas wanted above those ready; a legend; a badge, such as `SHORT 3`; and a status line, such as `7 of 8 reporting` or `stale 2 min`, each in a tone, neutral, good, a warning or bad.

- **Gaps are never zero.** A missing value breaks its line, and is never drawn as zero, nor a shaded region across it; a point joined to neither neighbour is a dot, so that a lone sample shows. A series with no values says `no data` in the legend, one without a latest value `--`, and a chart with no value at all says `No data`, with its status line, in the middle of its plot, never an empty plot that could be read as quiet.
- **Cleaned on the server.** The server keeps every chart within these bounds: from 2 to 256 points; every series exactly as many values, the newest last, those it lacks gaps before its first and those beyond dropped from the oldest; every value finite, or a gap, held within 10¹⁵ of zero and kept to 4 significant figures, so that a chart of every series at every point is under 12 KiB of JSON; its breaks, labels and shades only where they name what it has; every name, label, unit and note on one line, cut to 32 characters, or 80 for the status line. It refuses a chart of too few or too many points, an axis whose lowest value is not below its highest, and a reference line that is not finite.
- **Drawn.** Its face shows its title, bold, its status line, in its tone, and its unit, a rule, its legend, each series' key, a short line in its colour, its name and latest value, then its reference lines', as many whole as fit, and its chart in the rows below: a plot a little darker than the face, a faint line across at each end of its range and down at each axis label, its range's ends and each reference line's value beside it, in the reference line's colour, where they do not overlap, its axis labels below it, the first starting at its point and the last ending at its, any that would overlap the one before left out, its shaded regions, faint, its bars and areas, its reference lines, and its lines, the first series in front, and its badge, framed in its tone, at the plot's top right. Its age is not shown, unlike a text panel's: a live chart says how fresh it is in its status line, and the time since it was first published says nothing of what it shows. A line is clipped to the plot: where it leaves past a fixed end of the value axis, it ends at the plot's edge with a dot there, at most 16 a series, so that a value beyond the range never reads as lying at its end; a shaded region and an area are clipped to the plot as well, and a bar held to it. A series' area is shaded to zero, or the plot's edge nearer it, split where the line crosses it. In a wall's slot it is a slot's size, its chart wider in a wide slot and taller in a tall one, laid out in the slot's cells as a text panel is, whatever size, shape, tilt or bend the wall has, so that a 4:1 slot shows every axis label and the whole legend and a wall five times a standard slot's size draws it five times as large; floating or carried, it is 48 columns by 14 rows below its heading. It is drawn in the panel's own mesh, so that it is tested against depth, drawn alike in both eyes and follows a curved wall's slot as a text panel does (see `rendering.md`). It is read from the same reading distance as a text panel, its labels in the same font at the same size.
- **Drawn within a wall panel's buffer.** A wall's or floating panel's face is uploaded into a buffer of `tomori-render`'s `CARD_CAPACITY`, 12,000 vertices, grown for a face of more, as one of many small cells at a content scale below 1 is, to at most `CARD_MOST`, four times as many, about 1.7 MB, beyond which it would be cut, its text first, since text is drawn last, over its shapes; a chart is held within the first. So a client draws a chart as `tomori_scene::chart::bounded` holds it, whatever a server sent, every count and string within the bounds above and every value within 10¹⁵ of zero, and draws it in a bounded number of pieces: each series' line, its dashes and its bars in at most 128 vertices, its points closer than a fifth of a font pixel to the line through their neighbours merged away and, where that is not enough, each stretch of its points kept as its highest and lowest, so that no spike is thinned away, a step series' by columns, upright from where it was to the stretch's highest and lowest and level to the next, so that it keeps its square corners; each segment one piece, overlapping its neighbours by half the line's width where it turns by a right angle or less, and meeting them at a dot where it turns more sharply, at most 64 a series; every shaded region and area in pieces, at most 512 quads shared alike among its regions: a region is cut into pieces, each where its edges are measured and have a gap throughout, in one order, within one of equal columns across the plot, half as many as its share of quads, never narrower than a font pixel, so that nothing is shaded at any place, among fine samples of both edges and their points, where the edges meet or cross, nor across a gap or break in either; each piece is one quad reaching the widest gap within it, both edges there, so that a spike's shading reaches the line's peak, level on top there and sloping to its ends, or narrower about the peak, or, as many as the quads left allow, the widest first, two quads meeting at the peak and sloping to the gap at its ends, and each shades the area between the edges within it, worked out from fine samples of both; with more pieces than quads, neighbours within one stretch of gap are joined first, the narrowest first, and only then the narrowest across where the gap closes, which no chart within the spec's bounds above a level needs, since 256 points cross a level at most 255 times; between a step series and another edge, whose gap is level from point to point, a piece is one level quad as tall as the gap is on average; how shading is drawn depends only on the plot's width and how many regions it has, never on the values, so that a live chart's shading never changes style from one update to the next; a dashed series always dashed, all along, however noisy, its dashes laid across the plot, 4 font pixels long and 3 apart, or longer in proportion where the plot is wider than 64 of them, each the part of the line whose place across the plot is within it, so that the pattern depends only on the plot's size, its gaps are the same everywhere, there are never more dashes than periods across the plot, the same points draw the same dashes from one update to the next and a point added at its end changes only its end, its points not merged but, where it has more than 128, thinned to each period's highest and lowest, its legend's key two dashes; and each dashed reference line in at most 64 dashes. A chart at every bound, with every label at its longest, in a standard slot, the widest and tallest slots and floating, is at most about 9,900 vertices with its text; the design's pressure chart about 2,500. A face cut all the same is said in the client's log, once each time a panel's face first is. A chart is laid out once for each change of it or its size, not every frame: the client keeps the drawings of the latest 32. The bounds above are the spec's, unchanged; only the drawing is bounded.
- **Older clients.** A chart panel's body is a summary of what it shows now, in plain text, which the server makes: its status line, `No data.` where it has none, each series' latest value or that it has none, each reference line and its badge, a line each. A client that knows no charts ignores `Panel::chart`, as it ignores every field it does not know, and draws the panel as a text panel of its title and summary, so nothing is needed of it and nothing is bumped.

**Highlighting stays on the server.** No client builds tree-sitter, its grammars, syntect or `tomori-highlight`: `tomori-server` highlights with its default `highlight` feature, and the clients' tests build it without, as `bake/tests/dependencies.rs` checks for every client crate, every feature and every target, the headset's included. In November 2026 the grammars and syntect's syntaxes added 11.4 MB to the `tomori-server` release binary, from 52.7 to 64.1 MB, and about 15 seconds of compilation to its first build, most of it in parallel with the rest, and nothing to any client or the APK.

**The protocol.** Projection has messages of its own rather than the environment state channel, which belongs to one environment's binding and is forgotten when the space changes environment, and cannot yet keep state across restarts (#202):

- `ServerMessage::Projection` carries the space's projection as seen in its current environment, complete: the space's own status wall first, where it is shown, then the environment's walls and the added ones, and the panels on them or floating, at the space's projection revision, `Projection::revision`. It is sent on joining, after the space's state, and, to a client that applies no patches, whenever it changes, coalesced so that a burst of changes sends one, only to a client whose `Hello` sets `projection`, so an older client, which would refuse a message it does not know, is never sent it. A client that applies patches is sent it whole only as below.
- `SpaceState::projection` says the server projects, and takes `AddWall` and `RemoveWall` from owners and `PlacePanel`, onto a wall, in the `slot` it names, or floating at a pose, at the `size` it says, with its `revision`, where both hands stretched it, and `DismissPanel` from participants. A `slot` past a wall's last is refused, and a size against a version since changed; an older server ignores a size, and an older client a panel's, drawing it at its own size. A client sends them only to such a server, so an older server is never sent them. The server checks each against its own store and refuses one it cannot do, saying why.
- `Wall` says its `grid`, as `[columns, rows]`, and `scale` only where they are not its shape's own, so that a wall nobody adjusted is sent as before; an older client, which knows neither, draws its shape's grid and scale, and no panel in a slot past them. It says its slots' `aspect` only where they are not a standard slot's shape; an older client, which knows none, draws standard slots at its `scale`, so that each panel is drawn at its text's size, as a standard panel smaller than its slot, and the wall smaller than newer clients draw it. `SetWall` says an `aspect` to change it, `0` for a standard slot's, which an older server ignores, leaving the slots their shape. `SpaceState::walls` says the server takes `SetWall` from participants and leaves a removed wall's panels floating; a client offers adjusting walls only to such a server, and an older server, which says nothing of it, is never sent `SetWall`. A slot past the last of a wall's grid is refused. These are additions an older client or server ignores, so the protocol version does not change.
- The new types, `Projection`, `Wall`, `WallKind`, `WallShape`, `Panel`, `Place` and `Meta`, are non-exhaustive, with constructors, and `WallKind` and `Place` read a newer server's values as `Unknown`, which a client does not draw. `WallShape` reads a newer shape as `Unknown`, and a wall without a shape, as an older server sends, as a flat grid; a client draws either flat, as it does an arc too tight for its slots.
- A code panel is a `Panel` with `code`, a `Code` of its language, how many lines its block has and its `Span`s, each of a `TokenKind`, which reads a newer server's kinds as `Plain`; `Panel::pinned` says a participant has put it where it is. An older client, which knows neither, draws a code panel as a text panel of its code. `CODE_LINES`, `CODE_CHARS` and `CODE_SPANS`, 200 lines, 8,000 characters and 2,048 spans, bound it, and `valid_spans` checks its spans. An older client, which took at most 16 lines and 256 spans, draws a code panel's first 16 lines as before, but plain where it has more than 256 spans.

- `ClientMessage::CheckPanelItem { panel, line, checked, revision }` ticks or unticks a checklist item, from participants only, sent only to a server whose `SpaceState::checks` says it takes it, which an older client ignores and an older server never sends; its `revision`, the panel's version that the client saw, is absent from an older client. `Panel::version`, sent as `revision`, the name such a client sends back, is the panel's version, absent, and read as 0, from an older server, as are `Wall::version`, sent as `revision` too, and `Panel::data_seq`. `ServerMessage::Error::panel` names the panel of a refused tick, and `Error::wall` the wall of a `SetWall` refused for its version, each absent for other errors and ignored by older clients. `tomori_protocol::checklist` reads a body's items and ticks one, the same rule for the server and every client.
- `ClientMessage::ChoosePanelRow { panel, row, revision, key }` chooses a choosable row of an environment's list panel, by the row's id, saying the revision of its key seen, for that environment, from participants only, sent only to a server whose `SpaceState::choices` says it takes it; a list panel's `Panel::rows`, each a `ListRow` of its id, whether it is `choosable`, whether it is `highlighted`, its `tone`, its `value`, for a table, its `cells`, and, for a list with keys, its `key`, the revision of the key the server keeps for it, names its rows and tells a client that draws rows how to draw them, a list panel's `Panel::columns`, `ListColumn`s of a `title`, an `align` and a `value`, lays it out as a table, and `Resource::Panel`'s `row` the row pointed at, each absent from and ignored by older peers (see `panel-actions.md`).
- `Panel::slug` is the agent's id for a text panel, left out where it has none, and `Resource::Panel`'s `line`, made with `Resource::panel_at`, the line of the body the item pointed at begins on, left out elsewhere; an older client ignores `slug`, and an older server `line`, as each ignores a field it does not know.
- A wall of a group says its `group`, the identity of its group's first wall, and its `joined` edges, each a `Join` of an `Edge` and a wall, left out for a wall alone, so that an older client, which knows neither, draws each wall alone, its board whole. `SpaceState::groups` says the server snaps walls; a client offers snapping and carries a group only for such a server, and then sends `ClientMessage::AttachWall { wall, attach, alone, pose }`, which attaches `wall` across `attach.edge` of `attach.to`, its group with it unless `alone`, or, without `attach`, takes it out of its group, standing at `pose`. An older server, which says nothing of groups, is never sent `AttachWall`, and the walls it keeps are read where they stood. `Edge` and `Align` read a newer peer's values as `Unknown`, which the server refuses in a message and forgets in what it keeps.
- An image panel is a `Panel` with `image`, an `Image` of its SHA-256 as 64 lower-case hexadecimal digits, its width, height and length, which `Image::is_valid` bounds by `IMAGE_SIDE` and `IMAGE_BYTES`, and which a client fetches only if it is valid. `Hello::images` says a client shows image panels; it then sends `ClientMessage::FetchImage` with an image's hash, and reads frames of a third kind, `FrameKind::Image`, each an `ImageChunk`: the image's hash, where the chunk starts, the image's length, each a little-endian `u32`, and at most `IMAGE_CHUNK_BYTES` of it, which a client refuses if it reaches past its image or its image is larger than `IMAGE_BYTES`. None of these changes the protocol version: an older client neither says `images` nor is sent an image frame, and an older server sends no image panel to fetch from.

**Revisions and patches.** A change to what a space projects is sent as what changed, not as the whole projection again, to every client that applies patches:

- **Revisions.** The server counts a projection revision for each space, raised with every change to what it projects: a wall added, removed or changed, by its grid, size, shape, curve, tilt, lock, group or pose; a panel projected, dismissed, moved, pinned, updated or ticked; and the floating panels, which are panels too. A change that leaves the projection as it was, as when the server sends it again so that a client undoes a tick it refused, is a revision too. Each message of the projection to a client is at a later revision than the one before it; two messages at one revision show the same. Revisions are counted for each run of the server, not kept: a client connects again after a restart, and is sent the projection whole. Each space's count begins at the time, in milliseconds since the Unix epoch, as the run first holds it, so that no wall's or panel's version a run gives is one an earlier run gave, as long as no run raised a space's revision more than a thousand times a second on average, far more than its changes are limited to, and the machine's clock is not set back between runs, which could let a version from before a restart name another state after it; within a run, revisions only rise, whatever the clock does; a version the agent read before a restart is then refused after it, rather than taken for another state that happens to share it. Anchors and homes are the space's state, not the projection, and a panel's scroll is each client's own, so neither is a revision. Each wall's and panel's version is the revision at which it last changed (see versions and compare-and-swap, below).
- **Patches.** `ServerMessage::ProjectionPatch` carries a `ProjectionPatch { space, environment, from, to, ops }`: what changed from revision `from`, the one the client was last sent, to `to`, the space's now. Its ops, applied in order, make the projection at `from` the projection at `to`, exactly as it would be sent whole, but for each panel's `age` (below): `wall`, a wall new or changed, whole; `remove_wall`, by its identity; `panel`, a panel new or whose content changed, as by the agent's update or a tick, whole; `place`, a panel that only moved, pinned or not, with where it now is and its version, and nothing else of it; `chart_append` and `chart_replace`, a live panel's data, to a client that applies data ops (see versions and compare-and-swap, below); and `remove_panel`. Walls and panels are replaced and removed whole, by their identity, rather than field by field, so that applying a patch is simple and a panel's body changing is that panel sent again. An op names where a wall or panel now stands in its list, after another or first, only where it moved, taking as few ops as keep the rest in order, so that a panel moved to the end of the list, as one placed goes, is one op. A burst of changes before the server sends, as several changes in one hub pass, is one patch of several ops, since a connection waits on one projection at a time, as it did when each was whole. A patch may have no ops, as when the server sends the projection again with nothing changed, so that a client undoes what it showed at once; it is still from one revision to a later one.
- **Which is sent.** The server keeps, for each connection whose client applies patches, the projection it last sent it, as it was sent, and sends the next as a patch from it, where it is of the same space in the same environment and the patch sends at most half as many walls and panels whole as the projection shows; otherwise, the projection whole: on joining a space, or joining it again, after the space changes environment, when most of it changed, and on the client's asking. A client whose `Hello` does not set `projection_patches` is sent the projection whole whenever it changes, exactly as before patches, and keeps no copy on the server. One whose `Hello` sets `projection_patches` but not `live_panels`, as a client from before versions, is sent a panel whose version changed whole, even where it only moved, and a live panel whose data changed whole, `ProjectionPatch::between`, so that what it holds is exactly what is sent whole; one that sets both is sent `ProjectionPatch::between_live`, a panel that only moved as a `place` op with its version and a live panel's data apart. Tomori's own headset and desktop window apply patches (see `clients.md`); the spectator, and a client built on `tomori-client` whose session does not say it applies them, do not.
- **Resynchronising.** A client applies a patch only to its projection of the same space and environment at `from`. One at another revision, as after missing a patch, one of another space, or a patch it cannot apply, naming a wall or panel it does not have or an op it does not know, it does not apply, keeping what it shows, and sends `ClientMessage::ResyncProjection { revision }`, the revision it holds, once until it is sent the projection whole; the server forgets the projection it last sent that client and sends it whole, at the space's revision now, patching from there again. A patch refused, even partway through its ops, changes nothing of what the client holds, so that it never shows a projection half patched. The server ignores a request from a client already waiting for the projection whole, as on joining or having asked, so that asking again and again is sent it once, and one from a client that applies no patches, which is sent it whole at every change anyway. A client that connects again is sent it whole on joining, as ever. A checksum of the whole projection is not sent: patches are whole records applied by one shared rule, `ProjectionPatch::apply`, so a client's copy can differ from the server's only by a patch missed or refused, which its revision shows.
- **Age.** A panel's `age` is how long ago it was projected when it was sent. A patch sends it for the panels it sends whole; a client counts each other panel's age on from when it was last sent, so that what it shows of a panel's age is as it was before patches, and a panel's age alone never makes a patch.
- **Speech goes no later.** A patch goes on the connection where a projection always went, before agent speech queued after it, and of a few hundred bytes for a tick, an update or a wall's edit, it holds speech up far less than the projection whole did. The projection whole, sent on joining, after an environment change, when most of it changed and on the client's asking, may be one frame of up to the connection's frame limit, 32 MiB for Tomori's own clients, so it waits behind the agent speech and participant audio already queued, and goes out in pieces between which the connection reads its input (see `server.md`); speech queued once it has begun waits for as long as the frame takes to send. It is not split into frames interleaved with speech, at the maintainer's decision (see the rationale).
- **Measured.** In a typical space of the Tomori wall, an added wall and eight short text panels, the projection whole is 4.4 KB, and a tick, an agent's update and a wall's tilt patch it in 445, 476 and 272 bytes, versions and all; in a space at every limit, of sixty-seven walls and every slot and floating panel holding the largest text panels, it is 3.5 MB whole and the same changes patch it in about the same, thousands of times less. Versions, begun from the time, add about 25 bytes to each wall and panel sent and to a `place` op. `crates/server/src/stage/patch_tests.rs` measures them, at revisions of the size a run gives.
- **What a change costs the hub.** A projection is built, and a patch found, under the hub's lock, which every space shares, so what a stream of changes costs it is bounded. The space's projection is built once for each change told, at its revision in that second, whole, and once more within each set of limits its connections state, kept for at most eight sets; every connection of the same limits shares that build, holds it rather than a copy of it, and has only its own patch found from it. With an environment's changes told at most four times a second (see panels an environment publishes, above), a space costs the lock at most four such builds a second for each kind of connection in it, however many connections it has and however often its panels change. In a space at every limit, 534 panels, on an Apple M-series desktop, a build whole takes about 4 ms among the largest text panels, 4 MB, and 19 ms among the largest code panels, over 40 MB, with 2 ms and 10 ms more within today's limits, and each connection's patch 0.3 ms and 0.9 ms: at most 16 ms and 76 ms of the lock a second for Tomori's own clients, and 25 ms and 116 ms for older ones, with 1 to 4 ms more for each connection. Before, every change woke every connection and each built the projection for itself, twice where it took less than the most, which at 16 changes a second was about 72 ms a second among text panels and 1.1 s a second among code panels, 70 ms each, for every connection. `crates/server/src/hub_tests/patches.rs` counts them: for six connections of three kinds, 32 panels put up at once are one send each from three builds, and 80 changes in five seconds are 21 sends each from 63 builds, where they were 80 sends each from 800.

**Versions and compare-and-swap.** One version concept serves both what a client holds and the changes made against it:

- **A version is the revision of the last change.** Each wall's and panel's version, `Wall::version` and `Panel::version`, is the space's projection revision at which it last changed, in anything a client is sent of it but a panel's age and a live panel's data: for a panel, its title, body, metadata, id, kind, code, image, its chart's structure, where it is, how large it floats and whether it is pinned there; for a wall, everything it says. Every projection, whole or patched, carries each wall's and panel's version, a wall or panel in a patch is sent with its own, and a `place` op says the panel's, which a client keeps. The server finds versions by comparing each projection it builds, as it sends one, checks a change against one or tells the agent of one, with the last it built (`crates/server`'s `versions.rs`), so that every change counts, whichever part of the server made it, as patches are found; a change found with no revision raised since the last was built raises one first, so that one version never names two states of a wall or panel. A wall or panel not shown in the last, as one new, or shown again on coming back to an environment, is at the revision now. Neither the version nor its count is kept: a restart begins every version again, past any an earlier run gave (see revisions, above).
- **What edits a wall or panel says the version it saw.** A change that reads a wall or panel and changes part of what it read is compare-and-swap: it says the version it read, and the server applies it only while the wall or panel is still at that version, and otherwise refuses it, changing nothing. These are a participant's tick, `CheckPanelItem`'s `revision`; the agent's `update_panel`, its `version`; a participant's change to a wall by its handles, its edges, corners, steppers, knob and hinge, `SetWall`'s `revision`, the wall's version as the handle was taken hold of; a size given with both hands, `PlacePanel`'s `revision` and `SetWall`'s, the version as the second hand took hold; and the agent's `set_wall`, its `version`, read from `presence`. Refused, a participant's client is sent an error naming the panel or the wall, which it cues as not done, two falling notes and two taps on the right hand, and the projection again, which undoes what it showed at once, a wall's change drawn as the server has it rather than as sent; the agent is told the version now, to read the wall or panel again, with `panels` or `presence`, and redo the change from what it shows. Of two participants changing a wall or a panel from one version, the first to reach the server wins and the second is refused, rather than undoing the first's change without seeing it.
- **What replaces a whole state, or is the participant's own, is last-writer-wins.** Placing a panel, carried and let go, into a slot or floating, swapping it with another, and the agent's `move_panel`; throwing a panel away; carrying an added wall, or an environment's own unlocked, and letting it go, whose `SetWall` says its pose alone and no version, since where a participant leaves a wall they carried is theirs, whatever was changed of it meanwhile; snapping a wall or taking it out of its group, `AttachWall`; locking and unlocking, `LockWall`, which says the state wanted, and putting an environment's own wall back; adding and removing walls and layouts. Each says no version, and the latest to reach the server stands. Each changes the versions of what it moves, so that a tick or an update read before a move is refused and read again, as before any other change.
- **Saying a version is optional.** A change that says none goes by what it says alone, as an older client's tick, by its line, and the agent's call without one, did before versions; a version of 0, from an older server, is said by no client.
- **Live panels take a fast path.** A panel written only by its environment's server side, a chart panel today, and live panels later, has live data apart from its version: a chart's series' values and breaks, its badge, its status line and the summary its body is. Its data seq, `Panel::data_seq`, is the projection revision at which its live data last changed, which changes without changing its version, so that live data never refuses anyone's edit, and takes no compare-and-swap of its own, since only its environment writes it. To a client whose `Hello` sets `live_panels` a change of it is a data op in the patch, in place of the panel whole: `chart_append { panel, seq, from, values, breaks, status, badge, body }`, the points each series gained, in order, the same number of each and fewer than half its points, as many of its oldest dropped, each series' breaks whole only where they are not its own moved along with its points, and its badge, status line and summary as they now are, where every series only moved along and gained points, as a live chart polled on a fixed step does; or else `chart_replace { panel, seq, values, breaks, status, badge, body }`, its data whole. A client applies a data op only where it is newer than what it holds: one at or before the seq it holds it drops, the rest of the patch applying, and an append from another seq than it holds, or that does not fit the chart's series, it cannot apply, and asks for the projection whole, as for any patch it cannot apply. Several changes before a client is sent the next are one op, from the seq it holds to the latest, as they are one patch, and live data is still bounded by the limits on an environment's panels, about once a second for each and 16 changes and 128 KiB a second in a space after a burst (see panels an environment publishes, above). The projection whole, as on joining and resynchronising, carries each live panel's data whole at its data seq. A client that applies patches without data ops is sent the panel whole at each change of its data, exactly as before, and one that applies none, the projection whole.
- **Caches key by version.** What a client lays out and draws of a panel, as a cache of its face, is keyed by its identity and version, for everything but its live data, and its data seq, for that, so that a tick, an update or a move lays it out again and a live chart's next point redraws only its chart; neither changes for its age, its scroll or a move of another panel. `tomori-scene`'s cache of each panel's faces (`projection/faces.rs`, see `rendering.md`) keys them so, from a server that versions panels: a new version lays a panel out again, and a chart's new data seq at the same version draws its face again from its new points, its summary's rows followed, without laying it out; it also follows what the client changes of a panel itself, which no version follows, its body, as a tick shown at once and a code panel sent short or whole as its frame allows change it, and its age as it shows; from an older server, which versions nothing, it compares the panel whole, as before.

**Limits stated in Hello.** A client states in its `Hello`, with `limits`, a `tomori_protocol::Limits`, what it reads and draws at most: the longest frame, `frame_bytes`; and, in one projection, the most walls, `walls`, slots on them together, `slots`, panels floating while they have every slot, `floating`, and panels in all, `panels`. The server states its own in its `Welcome`: `Limits::MOST`, frames of 32 MiB, 77 walls (the Tomori wall, sixteen of the environment's own and sixty owners add), 780 slots, 120 floating and 900 panels, ten times the slots and panels it held before. Every field left out, as by an older peer, which states none, reads as today's: 1 MiB, 13 walls, 78 slots, 12 floating and 90 panels, `Limits::TODAY`. A connection is bound by the lesser of each, within `Limits::MOST`, `Limits::negotiate`, so that no declaration raises a bound past the server's, and frames never below 1 MiB; an older client is bound by today's, exactly as before. The server sends each client the projection within its limits, whatever the space holds: the status wall first, whichever wall holds the role, so that no client loses the space's status panels, such as pairing decisions and notices, however many walls come before it, then the walls in order, the Tomori wall, the environment's own, then those owners added, while they are fewer than its walls and their slots together within its slots, each kept in its order, a join to a wall left out left out too; the panels on those walls; the panels floating that were left most recently, as many as its floating panels and one more for each slot its walls have fewer than its slots; and of those, the panels put where they are most recently, as many as its panels, in their order; and as many code panels short as fit its frame. What a client is not sent stays in the space, shown to those that take more, so that an older client in a space a newer one fills sees today's bounds of it, the first walls, as it always did. Patches are found for each client from what it was last sent, within its limits, so a wall or panel coming within them is sent as new. Tomori's headset states today's panels with frames of 32 MiB, on 23 walls of 138 slots, the Tomori wall, sixteen of an environment's own and six added, six slots each, and its desktop window twice today's panels on 29 walls of 174 slots, with twelve added (see `clients.md`), so that an environment declaring sixteen standard walls is drawn whole, and the server's bounds are there for a client that can draw more. An older client, stating today's 13 walls and 78 slots, is sent the Tomori wall and as many of the environment's walls as fit them, the first twelve of sixteen standard walls, as it is sent the first of any walls beyond its bounds.

**Planned.** These follow revisions, patches and negotiated frames, in turn, each an addition an older peer ignores. Splitting the projection whole over several frames interleaved with speech, once planned here, was dropped at the maintainer's decision (see the rationale): frames are negotiated up to 32 MiB instead.

- **Code bodies by hash.** A code panel's lines and spans, and later a chart's series, sent by their content's hash, as images are, fetched once and kept across reconnecting, small ones staying inline.
- **Interest management.** Sending each client only what is within a distance of it, or what it looks at, as multiplayer games do, a far wall's panels as their titles until it comes near: distance-based loading, in chunks of the space as Minecraft loads its world, which the maintainer expects may come later but is not needed up front.
- **Level of detail by distance.** Drawing a far panel's title alone, and culling what is out of view, on the client, which needs no protocol change.

An environment's live chart panels ride on patches' data ops: each update of a chart that moves its points along is, to a client that applies data ops, a `chart_append` of the points it gained, 208 bytes for a pressure chart of 181 points polled once more, its patch and all, and to a client that applies patches without them its panel sent again whole, 1,334 bytes, at most about 22 KiB for the largest chart there can be; and a space's panels change at most 16 times and by 128 KiB a second after a burst (see panels an environment publishes, above), so that its live charts cost a client that applies patches at most about 128 KiB a second, in at most four patches, and a client that applies data ops a few hundred bytes a poll for each; a client that applies no patches is sent the projection whole at most four times a second (see panels an environment publishes, above). `stage/published/tests.rs` measures an update both ways.

**Not in this slice.** The other content formats, such as charts the agent shows (#82's `show_chart`), images from the network, an image panel in a `panel` block, animated images and zooming into an image, items as #82's general model, with history or merging of concurrent changes, scaling, rotating, pinning other than of code panels, grouping and attaching to anchors, scrolling shared between participants, selecting lines or spans of one (#147), a time-to-live, a control to clear the agent's content, refusals reported to the agent, MCP tools beyond `move_panel`, `panels` and `update_panel` (with #100), panels in the spectator and the console, panels a participant creates, and ticking an item with the trigger, which keeps attracting the swarm.

**Editing walls on the headset.** A participant who may adjust walls, in a space whose server takes wall changes, edits a wall with the right grip, as they carry a panel:

- **Taking hold.** Squeezing the grip while the ray meets a wall's board, one of its slots without a panel, or its bar, nearer than anything else the grip would take, takes hold of the wall, unless it is locked: a panel the ray meets takes the squeeze first, as it is in front of its slot, and a card nearer than either takes it, as ever. A wall taken hold of shows its handles, and a hint of its controls above it, until B, a squeeze at nothing, or taking hold of another wall lets go of it; it is let go too when it is no longer shown, or the participant may no longer adjust it, as on leaving the space.
- **The bar.** Every wall shown has a grab bar, and a group of walls snapped together one, below its bottom left wall (see below), after the bar below a window in the Quest's own interface, so that a wall whose every slot holds a panel, curved or not, is as easy to take hold of as an empty one: a thin bar, about half a handle thick, drawn subtly and highlighted while the ray meets it or its wall is carried, centred below the wall's bottom edge, level with its bottom corners, in the wall's plane and facing as the wall faces there, or, for a curved wall, below its label, as for the Tomori wall, whose top the status pill stands just above from home. It is a third of the wall long, but never shorter than three handles nor longer than five, and is met as a handle is, where the ray crosses the wall's plane within 0.8 of a handle's size of its middle line and of its ends; the grip on it takes hold of the wall exactly as on its board, carrying a wall someone added, and the ray ends on it. It stands where it does whether the wall is held or not, on one row with the handles beside it and the wall's bottom corners, each at least a handle's spacing beyond its ends, so that where the ray meets it and where it meets a handle never overlap, and it is never confused with a handle: held, a wall is carried again by its bar. While the ray meets the bar of a wall not held, a label below it says what the grip does, `Grip  carry and edit this wall` for a wall someone added and `Grip  edit this wall` otherwise, or `G` in the desktop window. A panel the ray meets nearer than a bar takes the grip, and a bar nearer than a panel is pointed at in its place, as a handle is. Bars show only to a participant who may adjust walls, the clutter being worth nothing to anyone else, and, as a wall's board and handles are, a bar is Tomori's own interface where an environment's client side may take the ray (#233).
- **Carrying an added wall.** A wall someone added, or, where the server locks walls, an environment's own wall that is unlocked, is carried while the grip is held, as a panel is: the point it was taken hold of stays on the ray, at the distance it was taken from, and it keeps facing the way it faced, and its tilt, so that taking hold of it moves nothing; the right stick pushes it further or pulls it closer, from 0.3 m to 8 m, as a panel, and turns it 10° a flick about the vertical, and its click turns it square to the participant. Let go, its new pose is sent, unless it is where it was. The Tomori wall and an environment's walls stand where Tomori or the environment puts them, so they are taken hold of but not carried.
- **Handles.** Small square handles stand at fixed places about the wall, in its own frame, each in its plane and facing as it faces there, whatever the participant's place or gaze, so that they are always in the same place relative to the wall, as plain as its own edges, and are laid out once for the wall and its group, each handle placed once, with no search. A handle is 7.5 cm across on a wall of standard slots, about 1.7° from 2.5 m, as near as such a wall is read from, and as much larger as the wall's slots are, since a larger wall is read from further away, from 4.5 cm to 30 cm; it never grows or moves with the participant's distance, so that walking and turning move nothing. They stand on a frame 0.9 of a handle beyond what the wall draws, a curved wall's label included: its **corners** diagonally beyond its corners; the **knob** above the middle of its top edge; the handle of **its columns** beside the middle of its right edge, with the steppers of its columns beyond it, fewer then more; the **hinge** beside the middle of its left edge, on the line it tilts about, so that it stays under the ray as the wall tilts; the **bar** centred below its bottom edge, level with its bottom corners, and on its row the **text handle** to its left and the handle of **its rows** between the steppers of its rows, fewer to the left, to its right, beside it where they fit within its bottom corners and otherwise beyond them, so that nothing stands below the bar, and the status pill, which stands about 20° below the eyes, covers none of them as the wall is looked at; and the **padlock** a row beyond its top left corner, with the control that puts the wall back beside it, inwards. **No two overlap:** each handle's middle stands at least 1.8 handles from every other's, across or up the wall, leaving a gap of most of a handle between them, and the bar's middle line and ends as far from every handle as both reach, whatever the wall's grid, size, shape of slot, curve or tilt: a small wall's frame is as large as its handles need, at least two spacings to either side of its middle and one above and below, and the bar's ends at least a spacing from its bottom corners. Since each face lies in the wall's plane, they stand apart as seen from wherever the wall is seen, near or far, straight on or from the side, foreshortened together with it. A curved wall's slots are each flat, their middles on its arc and each turned to face the line it curves about, so its handles follow its slots, across the gaps between them, and continue beyond its sides along the slot at that end, rather than along an arc as long as its slots, which on a wall of few wide slots curved tightly, such as one slot five times a standard one, ends far in front of where the slots end. A tilted wall's handles stand in its tilted frame; a wall above the eyes has its bar below it, nearest the hand. **Nothing stands in the floor:** where its bar and its row would stand within 15 cm of the space's floor, as below a wall standing low, they stand a row above its top instead, the padlock a row above them, and any handle still within 15 cm of the floor, such as a low wall's bottom corners, is left out, its top corners sizing its slots as well, but for the handle being dragged. Seen from behind, a wall's handles and bar stand where they do and work as they do from in front, seen from behind as the wall itself is: nothing is mirrored or hidden, so that from either side they are the wall's own. Each handle's symbol is drawn as shapes, centred on its handle by what it draws, but the knob's dot, a glyph centred in its cell (see `rendering.md`). **The nearest is taken:** the ray meets a handle where it crosses its face within 0.8 of a handle's size of its middle, across or up, never as far as halfway to a neighbour's, and of those it meets, the nearest along it; it highlights it, ticks lightly and ends on it, and the grip holds it while held. The ray meets a handle before a panel or board behind it, and before a bar: from far to the side of a wall curved tightly, a handle beyond its end may come in line with its bar as seen, and is then taken. The handles are:
  - **corners**, which size and shape the wall's slots: dragged in its plane, each slot's width follows how far across from the wall's middle the ray is against where it took hold, and its height how far up or down, apart, each as the square of that, so that dragging down makes tall slots and across wide ones, and twice as far makes them four times as large: from a standard slot to the largest, five times as large, is one drag to a little over twice as far from the middle, and back is one drag to under half as far. The slots' text is as large as the narrower of the two allows against a standard slot, in steps of 5%, from half to five times its size, and their shape, from 1:4 to 4:1, snaps to 1:1, 4:3, 3:4, 16:9, 9:16 or a standard slot's within about 3.5% of it, and is otherwise kept to a hundredth;
  - **its right edge**, `↔`, which gives it as many columns as put its right edge nearest where the hand has moved it, from where it took hold, and **its bottom edge**, `↕`, as many rows, a whole slot at a time, the wall growing about its middle, so that a handle standing beyond its edge changes nothing as it is taken hold of;
  - **steppers**, `-` and `+`, beyond the handle of its columns for a column fewer or more, and on either side of the handle of its rows, on the bar's row, for a row, which change it at once, a squeeze each;
  - **a knob**, `●`, above the middle of its top edge, which bends it, measured from where it was taken hold of, **across**: the hand moved to the right, as the participant sees it, level and square to the line from their eyes to the knob as they took hold, the wall curves around the participant, and moved back to the left, towards where it was taken, and beyond, it straightens, like a slider, from flat to as tightly as the wall may curve, never around more than half a circle nor tighter than 1 m, over **25 cm**, whatever the wall's size: one movement of the hand 25 cm to the right bends a flat wall fully, and as far back flattens it, never beyond either end, where it stops with a tick. Along its range the knob is eased: its curvature, one over its radius, is the most it may be times `t (1 + t) / 2`, `t` how far along the range it is, so that it moves half as fast as evenly near flat, for fine control there, and half as fast again near the end, monotonically, and a knob taken hold of on a curved wall starts where that wall's curvature is along it, so that moving back to where it was taken is the wall as it was. Within 1 cm of where it was taken, across, the wall is as it was, so that a hand's drift and shake change nothing, and beyond it the curvature follows the movement past that centimetre, without a jump; bending less than 6% of the most it may, within about 4 cm of the hand's movement from flat, it is flat, unless it was bent less than twice that to begin with; and moving the hand up or down, or towards the wall, bends nothing. The radius is a whole number of centimetres. In the desktop window, whose ray begins at the eyes, dragging the knob right bends the wall, and left straightens it;
  - **a hinge**, a level line with the wall's face tilted across it, drawn as shapes, beside the middle of its left edge, on the line the wall tilts about, so that it stays under the ray as a flat wall tilts, which tilts it, measured from where it was taken hold of, **up and down**: the hand moved down, the wall's top comes towards the participant, so that it faces down, and moved up, it tilts back, from 45° one way to 45° the other over 25 cm, as the knob's travel, whatever the wall's size, so that 12.5 cm down takes it from upright to 45°, stopping at each end with a tick, eased as the knob is about upright, its tilt 45° times `t (1 + t) / 2` either way, half as fast as evenly near upright. Within 1 cm of where it was taken, up or down, it is as it was; within 3° of upright, about 2.5 cm of the hand's movement, it is upright, with a tick, so that upright is always exactly reachable; and moving the hand across or towards the wall tilts nothing. The tilt is a whole number of degrees. In the desktop window, dragging it down tilts the wall down;
  - **a text handle**, `aA`, a small and a large letter drawn as shapes, on the bar's row, to its left, as on the Tomori wall, which sizes the wall's content within its slots, measured from where it was taken hold of, **across**, as the knob is: the hand moved to the right, the content is drawn larger, and to the left, smaller, like a slider, by as many times over **25 cm** as from half to twice, alike either way, so that 12.5 cm to the left takes it from as large as its slots give it to half, in steps of a tenth, never below the least its slots allow nor beyond twice, stopping with a tick at either end and at as large as its slots give it, while its slots, their size and their number stay as they are and its panels are drawn at once as they would be, more and longer lines in each. Within 1 cm of where it was taken, across, it is as it was, and moving up or down changes nothing. In the desktop window, dragging it right draws the content larger.
  
  A locked wall shows none of these, the hinge included, but its padlock (see the padlock, below); a wall in a group, which stands upright, shows no hinge.
  
  The Tomori wall has only its right edge, the column steppers, the knob, the hinge and the text handle, since it stays one row of at most six at its own size and shape. A change the server would refuse is not shown. While a handle is held, the wall is drawn as it would be, its panels with it, and in place of the hint, what it makes of the wall: a corner its slots' size and shape, such as `60 × 107 cm · 9:16`, the knob its radius, such as `◠ radius 2.4 m`, or `flat`, the hinge its tilt, such as `tilted 12° down`, `tilted 8° up` or `upright`, the text handle its content's size and how many columns and rows of a panel's body each slot then shows, such as `Text 80% · 42 columns × 15 rows`, and an edge its grid, such as `4 × 2 slots`; let go, the change is sent, saying the wall's version as its handle was taken hold of, and shown so until the server's projection agrees or for 3 seconds. Where another participant, or the agent, changed the wall meanwhile, the server refuses it, naming the wall, rather than undo their change: it is cued as a refused tick is, and the projection sent again shows the wall as it now is, to change again from there (see versions and compare-and-swap, below). A stepper's change says the version of the wall shown as it is squeezed, so that a second squeeze before the first comes back is refused, and squeezed again.
- **The padlock.** Where the server locks walls (`SpaceState::wall_locks`), a wall taken hold of shows its padlock, a handle as the others are, a row beyond its top left corner, drawn of shapes: open and bright, since a locked wall is never taken hold of. A locked wall shows no bar and no handle and is taken hold of by neither its board nor a slot, though it stands in the way of what is behind it, as a wall placed by the environment or an owner is not something to adjust. Only an owner, who may unlock it, sees its padlock, closed and warm, at whichever of its places is nearest their eyes (`padlock_points`): a row beyond its top left corner, where it would stand on the wall taken hold of, or a row beyond its top or bottom edge, from beyond one corner to beyond the other, its four corners among them and no two more than 2 m apart along a wall wider than that (`PADLOCK_SPACING`), never along an edge another wall of its group is joined across, beyond its own slots beside one joined to its side, over another wall's slots or by its group's bar, nor within 15 cm of the floor. It moves to another place only once that is 30 cm nearer the eyes than where it stands (`PADLOCK_STAY`), so that it stays put as they move about and never flickers between two, or at once to the nearest where it would not be seen where it stands, beyond 3 m or hidden, so that it shows wherever the place nearest them would; unlocked there, its padlock, open, stays there on the wall taken hold of, as far along the same edge as the wall changes, with `↺` beside it, inwards, rather than jumping to beyond the top left corner; each owner's client chooses for them alone, as only they see it. It shows only standing within 3 m of where it stands (`PADLOCK_NEAR`) in clear view of it, no floor or deck the environment declares (see `locomotion.md`) crossing the line from the eyes to the padlock or to the middle of its wall, so that walls far away show nothing, an owner walks up to the wall to unlock it, and a wall beneath a deck shows nothing to an owner standing on the deck, however near; the ray at it highlights it, ticks, ends there and says `Grip  unlock this wall`. Where the ray meets a locked wall, nearer than anything else it would take, and no handle, padlock or bar, a faint label at its end says `Locked`, or, to an owner too far to see its padlock, `Locked: come closer to unlock`, so that it is clear why the grip takes nothing there; an owner near it, who sees its padlock, is told nothing more. Each padlock's places are laid out once and kept until its wall, where it stands or the other walls of its group change, so that showing it costs a frame nothing but measuring the eyes against a few points and drawing it; what its ray says stands beyond it, above it, or below it where it stands below its wall, unless that would reach within 15 cm of the floor, when it stands above it. The ray takes nothing of a wall, its padlock, a handle, its bar, its board or a panel on it, beyond a declared floor or deck it crosses first, as it takes no teleport pad there, nor says `Locked` there; and where a declared floor or deck hides a locked wall's padlock or the wall from an owner, the ray meeting the wall says only `Locked`, since coming closer would not show the padlock. A wall's handles, bar, padlock and hint are drawn with the depth test, as every surface is (see `rendering.md`), so that what the environment draws opaque in front of them hides them; none is drawn on top. Being in clear view does not rest on depth alone, since a floor of glass or grating writes none, nor on distance in a straight line, which passes through a floor. An owner's squeeze on it unlocks the wall, for everyone, and takes hold of it, shown unlocked at once with every handle; on a wall held, the padlock locks it again, as it is, and lets go of it, its closed padlock alone left. A wall locked while held, by an owner here or elsewhere, is let go of. Anyone else sees no padlock on a locked wall, and the open padlock of a wall they hold does nothing. Beside the padlock of an environment's own wall unlocked, inwards, an owner has `↺`, whose squeeze puts the wall back where and as the environment has it, locked, and lets go of it, since it then stands where the server says. The hint of an unlocked wall adds, for an owner, `Grip lock  lock it as it is` and, for an environment's own, `Grip ↺  put back, locked`. The padlock is squeezed with the grip, as every handle and stepper is, rather than pressed with A or the trigger, so that the grip means take hold, and changes a wall, everywhere on a wall.
- **Removing.** For an owner, a press of B while carrying an added wall, let go within 0.4 seconds while it is still carried, removes it, for everyone, as throwing a panel away does; when it holds panels, the first press only asks, the hint saying, in the warm colour, that B again removes it and how many panels float, and a second press within 4 seconds removes it, its panels floating where they were. A press of B is the wall's while it is carried, as it is a panel's, and never leaves the space nor backs out of anything else. A collaborator's B removes nothing, and their hint does not offer it.
- **B lets go.** With a wall taken hold of and nothing carried, B lets go of it, and backs out of nothing else.
- **The hint.** A wall taken hold of shows, above it and its handles, clear of the status pill, at the angle the hint beside a panel carried is drawn, what each control does: `Grip ↔ ↕` more or fewer slots, `Grip corner` size and shape, but for the Tomori wall, `Grip - +` one fewer or more, `Grip knob ↔ bend`, `Grip hinge ↕ tilt`, `Grip aA ↔ text size`, the arrows saying which way the hand moves, `Grip bar` carry it, for an added wall, and `B` done; while it is carried, `Stick ↕` further or closer, `Stick ↔` turn, `B` remove, for all, for an owner, and `Let go` leave it here. The desktop window names its keys instead, as it does for a panel carried: `G`, `PgUp PgDn`, `[ ]`, `Backspace` and `Let go G`.
- **Visitors**, and participants where the server takes no wall changes, take hold of no wall, and see no bars.
- **Older servers and clients.** A wall says it is locked with `Wall::locked`, sent only where it is, and a server that locks walls says so with `SpaceState::wall_locks` and takes `LockWall` from owners. A client shows the padlock, and carries an environment's own wall, only where the server says it locks walls, so that against an older server nothing changes; an older client, which knows no lock, shows a locked wall's handles, and the server refuses what they ask, saying why, and sends the wall again as it is.

**Snapping walls together.** Walls stay simple rectangles and arcs, with no merged cells or internal seams within one wall; larger arrangements are composed by snapping several walls someone added together, so that a 2×2 wall and a large 1×1 wall beside it read as one piece. Where walls stand together is `tomori_protocol::group`, which the server and every client share, as they share `tomori_protocol::wall`:

- **Which walls snap.** Only walls someone added, in the environment the space is in: the Tomori wall and an environment's own walls stand where Tomori or the environment puts them, so they neither snap nor are snapped to, and a wall locked in place (`Wall::locked`) is no target either, nor snaps to anything, refused where `tomori_protocol::group::snaps_to` and `snap_within` decide, which the server and every client share. Both flat, or both curved, whatever their radii; a flat wall and a curved one never snap.
- **Snapping while carrying is magnetic.** While a participant who may adjust walls carries a wall, in a space whose server snaps them, a wall carried, or a wall of its group carried with it, snaps to another when it is within reach of where it would stand against that wall's facing edge, the two meeting along it, or within reach of it: one of its edges within 30 cm of there across the edge, or as far as 6° seen from the participant where that is more, so that a far wall is as easy as a near one; within 25 cm in front of or behind the other's surface, less than a firm pull takes a wall out of its group; turned from facing as the other's surface faces there by up to 30° about the vertical, three of the stick's 10° turns, and tilted by up to 15°. It need not be lined up first: where it would stand is outlined lined up with the other, each of its slots framed brightly and faintly filled at the pose it will take, as a slot is for a panel carried, so that the participant sees it straighten, with a tick as one is shown or another takes its place; the hint says `Let go  snap it there` and, above it, `Hold trigger  don't snap`, or `Hold click` in the desktop window. Let go, it is sent to the server, and drawn there at once, exactly where it was outlined, until the server agrees or for 3 seconds.
- **Which snap is shown.** Of every wall carried against every other wall, and of each other wall's edges, the one that ranks first: the nearest, by how far the wall carried would move across, along and out of the surface, ranked a little further for each degree it would turn, 0.2 m a radian, and 5 cm further across a top or bottom than a side, so that walls go side by side unless one is clearly above or below the other. A snap shown is held until it is 25% beyond each limit, and ranks 20% nearer against another, so that the outline neither flickers at the edge of reach nor between two neighbours. A wall pulled out of its group snaps back where it was only once pushed back within 10 cm of the surface, measured against where the walls it left stand, so that the pull is not undone at once; elsewhere it snaps as any wall does.
- **Placing a wall near another without snapping.** Holding the trigger while carrying a wall, the left mouse button in the desktop window, shows no snap and attracts no swarm, so that let go it stays exactly where it is, beside another or not; letting go of the trigger while still carrying it snaps as before.
- **Where a wall snaps.** Against the other's edge, `left`, `right`, `top` or `bottom`, the gap between their slots one gutter at the larger of their two scales, a flat wall's gutter or a curved wall's gap, so that spacing is even across the join; aligned along it at its `start`, its top beside a side or its left above or below, its `middle`, or its `end`, whichever is nearest where it was let go, its top first where two are as near, as two walls as tall are; lined up with it, in its plane facing as it faces; and on an arc, the arc continued, about the same line, at the other's radius, which a curved wall snapped to it takes, so that the group reads as one curve, unless that would take it round more than half a circle, when it does not snap.
- **Groups.** Walls snapped together are a group, kept by the server with the space: each wall but the group's first is kept attached to another, across that wall's edge and aligned along it, `Attach { to, edge, align }`, its pose following from the first wall's, and kept as it stands too, so that a server that knows no groups reads every wall where it stood. So a group stays joined as its walls change: a wall given more columns, rows or larger slots pushes the walls attached beyond it along, and bending one wall bends every wall of its group alike, along one arc, while each keeps its own grid and slots. A curved wall of a group takes exactly the radius of the wall it is attached to, whatever its slots' size, so that every wall of a curved group stands on one arc about one line. Carrying any of its walls carries the group, every wall keeping where it stood from it, and the server moves the group as the wall carried moves. A wall snapped to another brings its own group with it, the two becoming one group; snapped to a wall of its own group, it comes alone.
- **Taking a wall out.** Pulled firmly towards the participant while carried, more than 30 cm nearer than it was taken hold of, with the right stick or the desktop window's Page Down, a wall comes out of its group alone, with a tick, the rest returning to where they stand, and the hint, while it is still in its group, says `Stick ↓ far  take it out alone`, or `PgDn far`; let go, it leaves its group where it is, or snaps where it is outlined. Taken out, or removed, a wall's neighbours stand where they are and stay together, attached to another wall of the group they still touch as they touched it, or else as groups of their own.
- **What the server checks.** The server, not the client, decides: both walls someone added, in this environment of this space, not the same wall, an edge and an alignment it knows, every wall of the group within 30 m of the space's middle, all flat or all curved, each curved wall then bent to its group's radius without going round more than half a circle, and no two walls' slots reaching into each other, where either has changed, and no locked wall attached, detached or moved, nor any wall of a group a locked wall of which would move; otherwise it refuses, saying why, and nothing moves. A group's walls count towards the sixty added in an environment and the 780 slots shown, as walls do. Participants snap and take out walls as they move them; a visitor does neither.
- **Drawing a group.** The server sends with each wall of a group of more than one its group, named by its first wall, and which of its edges are joined to which of the group's walls, as `tomori_protocol::group::joined` finds them, standing edge to edge a gap apart. A client draws a flat wall of a group edge by edge, as quads: its translucent board reaches, across a joined edge, only to the seam halfway across the gap between their slots, so that two boards meet without overlapping, and elsewhere as far as the margins of the group's largest wall, so that the group's outer edge is even; its border is drawn only along what no board of its group meets, so that the union reads as one board; and its group's label is shown once, on its top left wall, or, curved, as a curved wall's label is below its slots, its bottom left. An edge the server says is joined is drawn joined only while the wall across it still stands there, as the client draws it.
- **A group's controls cover none of its slots.** A group shows one grab bar, its bottom left wall's, whose slots reach lowest, by a centimetre, then leftmost, as `bears_bar` in `tomori-scene`'s `projection/boards.rs` chooses it, centred below the slots of the whole group, a third of them long within a bar's bounds, standing below that wall as a wall's bar does, so that it stands below every wall of the group, or, where that would be within 15 cm of the floor, above the group's highest slots; its other walls show none. A locked wall bears none: the bar goes to the group's bottom left wall unlocked, by the same rule among its unlocked walls, and stands below the group's lowest slots where a locked wall's reach lower than its own, so that it still covers none; a group of none but locked walls shows no bar. It carries the whole group, as any of its walls does, and a wall of the group is still taken hold of alone by its board or an empty slot, to be edited or pulled out. A wall of a group taken hold of keeps its handles off what another wall of the group shows: those along an edge another wall of its group is joined across stand along the opposite edge, the knob a row below its bottom corners, the handle of its columns and their steppers along its left, which a wall of a group, having no hinge, leaves free, and the handle of its rows, their steppers and the text handle a row above the padlock's; the padlock a row below its bottom left corner, level with the knob, where its top is joined, and a row beyond its top right corner where its left edge is; where the opposite edge is joined too, they are left out, as is a corner beside a joined edge; and any still standing over another wall's slots, as large as it is drawn, or where the ray meeting it could meet the group's bar, is left out, but for the handle being dragged and the padlock. The hint stands above the group's slots. Handles and the bar are sized by their wall's scale, not by the participant's distance, so that what they keep clear of is the same however far away the group is seen; a wall that has lost a handle to its neighbours is edited from another wall of its group, as bending one bends them all, or pulled out first. A wall in a group has no hinge, since a group's walls stand upright.
- **The agent snaps walls.** `set_wall` attaches a wall someone added to another, `attach: { to, edge, align }`, alone, after any other change it asks for, or takes it out of its group with `detach`; `presence` names each wall's group and the walls joined to it, across which edge (see `mcp.md`).

**Layouts the agent composes.** When someone asks the agent to visualise or lay out information, it builds a layout of walls in one step with `create_layout` (see `mcp.md`), and fills it:

- **What it asks for.** From one to six walls, as many as an environment took added before frames were negotiated, so that one layout stays a group taken in at once, each with the agent's own id for it, a slug as a panel's id is, and laid out as `add_wall` lays a wall out, its columns and rows, its slots' size as a scale, a width in metres or their width and height, their shape and its curve; every wall after the first attached to an earlier one by the agent's id, across its `left`, `right`, `top` or `bottom` edge, aligned at its `start`, `middle` or `end`, by default its start, and built as one snapped group, as a participant's snapping builds one. A wall attached without a size of its own fills the edge it is attached to, as far along it as the other's slots reach, within the bounds of a slot's size, so that a big 1×1 beside a 2×2 is as tall as it; and each wall is curved as the first is, unless it says otherwise, a layout's walls being all flat or all curved at one radius.
- **Where it stands.** `place`, `looking`, by default, `front` or `near`, with a name or for whoever asked, as a panel's are, and `beside` as `front`, since a layout is too large to stand beside someone: the layout's middle stands in front of that person, upright and facing them, level with where they face a little below their eyes, or, `looking`, as high as they look, from 0.8 m to 0.6 m above their eyes; a flat layout as far away as it takes to be seen whole within about 60°, at least 1.5 m, and a curved one at its radius, so that its arc curves about them, either no further than 3.5 m. It must be **readable**: every slot within 4 m of their eyes for a standard slot's text, the reading distance panels go by, and as much nearer or further as its text is smaller or larger, so that a slot at half the size is read within 2 m. Where it is not, or where it would overlap a wall already there, it comes nearer, to 2.4, 2, 1.6 and then 1.2 m, and is refused where it is readable nowhere. `wall WALL` places no layout.
- **All of it or nothing.** The whole layout is checked first, built apart from the space and then where it stands, and refused whole, saying why, as for an attachment to no earlier wall, two walls of one id, walls of different shapes, an edge it does not know, beyond the walls an environment takes or the slots it shows, overlapping a wall already there wherever it would stand, or readable nowhere; nothing is built until all of it is.
- **What it says.** Its result names each wall, the agent's id, its identity and its name, such as `added wall 2`, its grid, its slots, numbered from 0, their size and its own, and asks the agent to fill each slot it should hold, with a panel block's `place: wall NAME slot N`, a code block's `place="wall NAME slot N"` or `show_image`'s `place`, and to tell everyone what it built; a notice titled "Walls" on the Tomori wall says it, as for its other changes to walls, and it is logged and kept.
- **Taking it down.** `remove_layout` removes every wall of the group of a wall it names, as `remove_wall` names one, each wall's panels floating where they were, and says how many.
- **Its instructions.** Tomori's layer says that when someone asks it to visualise or lay out information it builds a layout with `create_layout`, with as few walls as the information needs, fills each slot it lists with `place: wall NAME slot N`, and says what it built; `tomori-tools` says what `create_layout` and `remove_layout` do.

**Ticking an item with A.** A participant ticks or unticks a checklist item themselves, for everyone, by pointing at it and pressing **A**, or, in the desktop window, pointing the mouse at it and pressing **Enter**, A's key:

- **Which items.** A task item, one with a box, `- [ ]` or `- [x]`, on any text panel, whoever projected it, pointed at on any row of its text as attention finds it (above), on a panel pointed at rather than carried. An item without a box, the panel's title, a line of no item, a code panel and an image panel tick nothing. It is A's rung after the right wrist's transcript and the list, as `interaction.md` orders it; the trigger still attracts the swarm.
- **Who.** Participants, owners and collaborators, as they rearrange panels; a visitor only watches, so their A is refused, saying so, and the server refuses their message too.
- **The message.** The client sends `CheckPanelItem { panel, line, checked }`, the line of the body the item begins on, as attention names it, and whether it is to be ticked, the opposite of what it shows, only to a server whose `SpaceState::checks` says it takes it. It shows the change at once, ticking its own copy by the same rule, until the server's projection says how it is. Saying `checked`, rather than toggling, means two people ticking an item at once both leave it ticked, though the second, from a revision the first changed, is refused and cued. It also says the panel's version the server last sent, where it sends one, and never counts its own tick in it: a guess at the version its tick makes could equal that of another change the tick raced, such as the agent's rewrite, and so let a second tick land on another item. A second tick before the projection comes back is therefore refused, for the participant to tick again once it shows.
- **On the server.** The server checks the panel is a text panel shown in the space's current environment, that it is at the revision the tick says, where it says one, and that the line begins an item with a box, as `update_panel` reads items, ticks it there in place, keeping the panel's identity, wall and slot or where it floats, and when the agent last updated it, since a participant's tick is not the agent's update, and sends the projection to everyone. An item already so changes nothing; one refused says why, naming the panel in its error, and either sends the projection again, so that the client that showed it at once shows it as it is.
- **A tick racing a change.** A tick whose version the panel has changed since, as when the agent rewrote the body, reordering, adding or removing items, another participant ticked an item, or someone moved it, is refused, saying `That panel changed as you ticked it; look again.`, rather than ticking whatever item is on that line now. The participant's client takes the error naming the panel as a cue, not a problem to show, and the projection sent again undoes its tick at once and shows the panel as it is, so that they look again and tick the item where it is now. A refused tick reaches no note to the agent. **An older client**, which says no version, ticks by its line alone, as before: its tick racing a change of the body may still land on whichever item begins on that line by then, a residual race that ends once it says the version it saw. A client from before versions, which says the `revision` it was sent, is checked against the panel's version, which it was sent as that, and so keeps the protection it had.
- **Telling the agent.** The agent is not given a turn for it, so that it does not reply to every tick: `panels` shows it at once, and the space keeps a note of it, the latest for each item, at most eight, told in a block of its own before the next words spoken in the space, whoever speaks them, after the speaker's name and before the note of which message they answer and what they point at (see `spaces.md`), as `Bob: [Ada ticked item 2 of 5 of panel "Agenda" (id agenda): "Panel ids"; Ada unticked item 1 of 5 of panel "Agenda" (id agenda): "Pairing window"] [Pointing at …] next one`. Names, titles and texts are cleaned as the context block's descriptions are, and the block keeps the latest notes within the context block's 1,000 characters. Notes are kept only in memory. The agent's instructions say that participants tick items so, and to take one as done without remarking on it unless it matters.
- **Saying so.** The label on the ray, while it meets such an item, ends with how to tick it, `A  tick`, or `A  untick` for an item ticked, `Enter` in the desktop window, after how to scroll a panel that scrolls, for a participant in a space whose server takes ticking.
- **Feedback.** A that ticks or unticks an item clicks and taps as choosing does (see `feedback.md`); one the client refuses, as a visitor's, does not. One the server refuses, as one racing a change, is cued as not done, with two falling notes and two taps on the right hand, when its error arrives.

**Environment-specific content, later.** Generic content, text and charts, may stand on any wall. Content an environment publishes stands only on that environment's walls, an owner's added walls or floating in it, and is hidden with it (above); content types an environment defines will too. Whether the agent's own charts, once it shows them, may stand on the Tomori wall, and whether moving an environment's content there should degrade it to a text summary rather than be refused, are open questions on #82.

## Rationale

The maintainer asked, in November 2026, for a simple first slice of #82 that shows how projection looks and works, without overfitting for later content types: projection walls with text panels, created from fenced blocks in the agent's replies, which participants rearrange and point at. The maintainer then refined it: projection is spatially registered, and environments differ in shape, so the space has one Tomori wall that travels with Tomori across environments, while each environment's walls and the panels left floating in it are kept for that environment, hidden and restored with it.

Fenced blocks are what agents already write, and keep a reply useful in the transcript as well as in the space; projecting when the reply completes means a block is never projected half written. The nearest wall to whoever asked puts the answer in front of them, as #82's section on presentation suggests, and capturing where they stood as their words begin follows how attention is captured (see `spaces.md`). A calm layout, where nothing moves but the panel that leaves, follows the maintainer's brief that panels must not shuffle while someone reads them; dropping the panel there longest, rather than showing "+N more", keeps a wall from growing a second page nobody can reach, and its text stays in the transcript. Visitors see panels because they see the transcript, which holds the same text; they change nothing, as they change nothing else.

Code panels were agreed with the maintainer in November 2026, after the text panels, as the first part of #147's shared code toolkit: agents already write code in fenced blocks, and code is far better read than heard. Highlighting on the server keeps headsets running only Tomori's own fixed renderers on bounded declarative data, as the trust boundary in #82 requires, and keeps the grammars out of the APK; tree-sitter for the languages Tomori is used with most gives the most faithful classification, and syntect covers the long tail without a grammar each. Kinds rather than colours let every client share one palette and change it without the server, and let both highlighters agree. Replacing the last reply's code panels, unless someone pinned one, keeps a conversation about code from filling the walls, while what someone chose to keep stays; one-line blocks stay in the transcript because they are mostly commands, which would otherwise crowd out and replace the code being discussed. tree-sitter-highlight does not yet layer Markdown's inline grammar, or a fenced block's own language, correctly over its block grammar, so Markdown is highlighted by its block structure alone.

The open questions of #82 were decided for this slice as follows: panels persist until dismissed, with no time-to-live yet; they are kept in a store of the stage's own, in the space's record, with a schema version, rather than on the state channel, which cannot yet keep state across restarts or across a change of environment, and could adopt #202's persistence and visibility once it has them; visitors may see every panel; there is no "clear agent content" control yet, and a dismissal cannot be undone; fenced blocks are projected when the reply completes, and a refused block is left in the transcript and not reported to the agent; and participants rearrange panels but do not create them.

The maintainer, trying the first slice on a Quest 3 in November 2026, asked for a preview of where a panel carried will snap, for a panel dropped on a wall to go into the slot under the ray, and for panels a participant places to stay where they are put. Placing by hand marks a panel arranged, so that the agent's new panels take free slots and then the oldest of its own, and only when a participant has arranged a whole wall does their oldest go.
