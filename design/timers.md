# Timers and the Event Queue

This describes how a space's agent sets timers that wake it on a schedule, attached to whether anyone is present, and the space's event queue, through which Tomori tells the agent what its own sources noticed, timers first.

## Requirement

**What a timer is.** A space's agent may set timers in its space with Tomori's `set_timer` tool (see `mcp.md`), so that Tomori wakes it, rather than the agent sleeping in a shell or a sub-agent to wait. Each timer has a name, a note in the agent's own words saying what it is for, a schedule, a condition and a time zone, and optionally an end and a count of firings. When a timer fires, Tomori tells the agent through the space's event queue, below, never mid-turn. A timer belongs to its space, not to the session the space is bound to, and is kept with the space, below.

**Schedules.** `set_timer` takes exactly one schedule, each in a field of its own, so that the tool's schema says which forms there are and each is checked by its type:

- `every_minutes`, from 1 to 10,080, a week: where the number divides a day, as 5, 15, 30, 60, 90, 120 or 1440 do, at every multiple of it after midnight by the clock of its zone, as times of day are, so that every hour falls on the hour, every two hours on the even hours and every day at midnight, before and after daylight saving changes; otherwise every so many minutes of elapsed time from when it was set.
- `every_seconds`, from 60 to 604,800, a week: every so many seconds of elapsed time from the whole second nearest when it was set, never on the clock, as for the intervals of a workout.
- `daily_at`, `HH:MM` on the 24-hour clock, with optional `days`, a list of day names or their first three letters, `weekdays`, `weekends` or `daily`, by default every day: at that time of day on those days.
- `once_at`: once, at the next `HH:MM`, or `HH:MM:SS` to the second, today if it is still ahead and otherwise tomorrow, or at a `YYYY-MM-DD HH:MM`, or `HH:MM:SS`, after now and within 366 days. `until` takes the same forms.
- `in_minutes`, from 1 to 527,040, 366 days: once, that many minutes from now.
- `in_seconds`, from 1 to 31,622,400, 366 days: once, that many seconds from the whole second nearest now, so that it fires within half a second of when it was asked for, such as 30 for a 30-second exercise.
- `cron`: a five-field cron expression, minute, hour, day of the month, month and day of the week, of numbers, `*`, lists, ranges and steps, such as `*/30 9-17 * * 1-5`; Sunday is 0 or 7. As in Vixie cron, where the day of the month or the day of the week begins with `*`, as `*` or `*/2` does, a day must match both, so that `0 9 */2 * *` fires on odd days and `0 9 */2 * 1` on odd days that are Mondays; where both are otherwise restricted, a day matching either fires, so that `0 0 13 * 5` fires on every 13th and every Friday. Names of months and days, `L`, `W`, `#` and `?` are refused. A cron schedule that has no occurrence within four years and a week, such as the 31st of February, is refused.

**Time zones and daylight saving.** A timer's times of day are in its `zone`, an IANA name such as `Europe/London`, by default the zone of the machine the server runs on, or UTC where it has no IANA name; the zone is kept with the timer as it is set, so that moving the server does not move its timers. A time of day that does not exist, as clocks go forward, fires as far past the gap's start as it is, so that 02:30 fires at 03:30 where 02:00 becomes 03:00; a time of day that happens twice, as clocks go back, fires once, at the first. An `every_minutes` that divides a day follows the same rule for each of its times, so that, as clocks go forward, an hourly timer's hour that does not exist fires once, at the next hour, and as they go back, the repeated hour fires once, at its first; one that does not divide a day counts elapsed time, which daylight saving does not touch. This holds in zones whose clocks move by half an hour, as Lord Howe Island's, or whose offset is not a whole hour, as Chatham's.

**Present.** A participant is present in a space while they are in it as an owner or a collaborator, connected, and their client reports them `engaged` or `distracted` (see `spaces.md`): wearing their headset, or with the desktop window visible. A visitor, a participant whose client reports no engagement, such as the spectator or an older client, and a participant who is `away` or has gone, are not present. A space counts as attended while someone is present, and for 2 minutes after the last participant stopped being present, the grace, so that lifting a headset for a moment or reconnecting neither skips a firing nor counts as arriving again. Someone **arrives** when the space was not attended and a participant becomes present.

**Conditions.** A timer's condition says whether it fires while nobody is present:

- `present`, the default: an occurrence fires only if the space is attended as it falls due; otherwise it is skipped, not saved for later, and the next firing says how many were skipped.
- `present_or_arrival`: as `present`, but an occurrence that falls due while the space is not attended waits, and fires once, as the first participant arrives, if someone arrives before the timer's next occurrence, or within 24 hours for its last; otherwise it lapses, counted as skipped. It never fires twice for one occurrence, so leaving and coming back the same morning fires nothing more. This is "every morning at 9 when someone is present, or after 9 when they join for the first time".
- `always`: every occurrence fires, whether or not anyone is present, still never mid-turn. It is bounded more tightly: its occurrences are at least 15 minutes apart, and a space delivers at most 24 firings in any 24 hours while it is not attended.

**Missed occurrences.** The server looks at every timer of a space whose agent can be told, open, bound to its session and connected to it, by the clock about every second, and as the soonest of their next occurrences falls due, so that a timer fires within milliseconds of its second, and one due sooner than the server would look again, as one set a second ahead, within half a second of it (`server.rs`'s `look_at_timers`). Occurrences that fell due since a timer was last looked at, as while the server was stopped, the space was closed or not yet bound again after a restart, or Tomori was reconnecting to the agent, coalesce into one: the latest of them is evaluated against the condition, and fires at most once, saying how many before it were missed, counting at most 999. A closed space's timers are not looked at, so that what fell due meanwhile is caught up once it is bound again. After a restart, a space's timers are not looked at for the grace, 2 minutes from when they are first looked at, while nobody is present yet, so that a catch-up as the space is bound again, before headsets reconnect, counts the participants who come back. Occurrences held back by the limits, below, are counted, and the next firing says so.

**Limits.** A space keeps at most 8 timers. A name is 1 to 40 characters on one line, unique in its space in any case, as Unicode lowercases it, the same comparison wherever a name is matched, set, cancelled or pointed at, and setting a timer of a name the space has replaces that timer; a timer set without a name takes `timer N`, the next number whose name no timer has, so that it never replaces one. A note is 1 to 200 characters, on one line. A timer's occurrences are at least a minute, 60 seconds, apart, which a cron schedule's minute field is checked for, counting from the last of an hour to the first of the next; `always` timers' at least 15 minutes. A single occurrence, as `in_seconds` sets, may be as soon as a second from now. A space delivers at most 96 firings in any 24 hours, of all its timers, and at most 24 of them while it is not attended; a firing remembered as later than now, as after the clock is set back, counts towards neither. `count` is at least 1, and `until` is after now and within 366 days; a timer with no occurrence before its end is refused. A timer that has fired its count, or has no occurrence ahead and none waiting for an arrival, is removed.

**The event queue.** Each space has an event queue of notes from Tomori's own sources, of which timers are the first, with workers' failures and stalls (`events.md`) and participants coming near an anchor (`environments.md`): a note has its source, a key and its text. A note replaces a note of the same source and key not yet told, counting it, so that an hourly timer that fired twice while the agent worked is told once, saying `and 1 earlier, coalesced`; at most 16 notes wait, the oldest left out beyond that and counted. What waits is told to the agent only while it can be given input: the space is open, the agent's session idle, nothing is waiting for delivery or a decision, and Tomori is not reconnecting. Participants' words queued then take every note waiting with them, before the notes of what participants did, such as ticking an item (see `projection.md`), so that the agent reads both in one turn. Otherwise a prompt of the notes alone is given only once 10 seconds have passed since the agent's turn ended, so that it does not cut in on what was just said, 30 seconds since the last such prompt, so that a burst of events arrives as one, unless a one-off timer's firing waits among them, which is told without that gap, since each costs one turn someone asked for and a 30-second timer told 30 seconds late is no use, and while nobody in the space is speaking to the agent, since their words will take the notes; words count as being spoken for at most 2 minutes after they began, longer than anyone holds X, and a client that disconnects takes its open words with it, so that a client that drops or never ends its words cannot hold the notes back. A block of notes is at most 2,000 characters, each note at most 600, the latest kept. So a timer fires at its second, but is told then only while the agent is idle and quiet: one that falls due during a turn, or within 10 seconds of its end, is told once the turn has ended and 10 seconds more have passed, or with the next words, and a repeating one 30 seconds after the last prompt of notes alone, which for a timer every minute can be half its interval; a one-off set at the end of a turn and more than 10 seconds ahead, as a 30-second timer, is told within a second of its time. Its dial shows it fired at its second meanwhile, a one-off's going, and its note says when it fired, so that the agent can tell how late it is.

**How a note reads.** Each note is in square brackets, on one line, its own square brackets made parentheses, so that it can neither end early nor begin another; a prompt of notes alone has no speaker's name, so it cannot be taken for a participant's words, whose square brackets are already made parentheses (`interaction.md`). A timer's note gives its name and its schedule and condition in short words, when it fired, as `HH:MM`, or `HH:MM:SS` for a timer every so many seconds or a one-off not on a minute, or a day and time where it was not today, in its zone, whether it fired as someone arrived and when it was due, when it last fired, what was missed, skipped and held back, who is present, whether it was its last firing, and the agent's own note, quoted, double quotes in names and notes made single quotes: `[Timer "status" (every hour, when present): fired 10:00; last fired 09:00; present: Samuel; note: "Give a status update"]`. The note is data, the agent's own reminder: the tool's description and `tomori-tools` tell the agent that it is not a request from anyone, and to decide then what, if anything, is worth doing or saying.

**Whose turn it is.** A prompt of notes alone is no participant's request: it supersedes the words the agent was given last, so that no tool that needs `requested_by` acts on those words in a turn a timer began (see `security.md`), and the agent is asked to have whoever wants it say so again. It is Tomori's own delivery, so the space remembers its key as one it gave, and its delivery is not taken for input that did not come through Tomori. A following agent goes to nobody for it (see `environments.md`). It goes only to the session the space is bound to; a space bound to another session, as when its session is replaced, keeps its timers and tells the session bound now. Notes waiting in the queue are for this run only; a timer's occurrences not yet looked at are kept, and caught up after a restart.

**The tools.** `set_timer`, `timers` and `cancel_timer` are space tools, acting only on the space bound to the calling session (see `mcp.md`). The agent sets a timer when someone asks, or to check back on work it started, and cancels one when someone asks or it is no longer needed; participants cancel a timer by asking the agent, as they have it remove a worker. Neither needs `requested_by`: a timer tells the agent only its own note, within the limits above, and a turn it begins can act on nobody's word.

**Kept with the space.** A space's timers are kept with it in the state directory, in `KeptSpace::timers`, in a format of their own with its version, now 1, a timer every so many seconds of the schedule kind `every_seconds`, which an older server leaves out: each timer, with its next occurrence not yet looked at and an occurrence waiting for an arrival, the times of the space's latest firings, for the daily limits, and the number the next unnamed timer is called by. They are written as a timer is set, cancelled or fires. The record is read leniently, so that it never stops the spaces from loading: a version this server does not read leaves every timer out, and a timer it cannot read, such as one of a schedule kind a newer server knows, a cron it refuses or a field edited by hand, is left out alone, each with a warning naming the space and the timer, the space and its other timers loading; at most 8 are read. A timer kept in a zone this server does not know goes by UTC, with a warning, and `timers` says so of it. Deleting a space deletes its timers, and a space forgotten deletes them with it.

**Drawn in the space.** The space's state carries its timers, `SpaceState::timers`, in the order they were set, each a `tomori_protocol::Timer`: its name, schedule and condition in short words, when it next fires, in seconds since the Unix epoch, for a countdown, and its state, `waiting`, `paused` while a timer that fires only while someone is present has nobody, `awaiting` while an occurrence waits for an arrival, or `fired` for a minute after it fires, or half the time between its occurrences where that is shorter, so that one every minute counts down between its firings, each for a distinct look; an older client ignores them. A participant pointing at a timer as they speak names it as `tomori_protocol::Resource::Timer`, by its name, and the server tells the agent of it as it describes it then, `[Pointing at timer "status" (every hour, when present; next in 12 min)]`, as it describes a worker pointed at (`interaction.md`).

**Its dial.** Every client that draws workers, the desktop, the headset and the spectator, draws each of the space's timers, at most 8, the first 8 a server sends and a name once, as a small dial standing at the agent's home, beside its workers, in the order they were set (`tomori-scene`'s `dials`):

- **Where it stands.** The dials stand where the home's layout leaves room for them and their signs, which each client works out alike from the home cluster's layout (`tomori-protocol`'s `cluster`), clear of every slot it lays out at any of its steps, at full size and compact, of each worker and its sign, a metre across, turned any way, drawn larger beyond 3.2 m from the audience as a sign is, and of one another, so that nothing a worker does moves them, and clear of the slots of every wall the space shows, its status wall among them, wherever it stands: the Tomori wall beneath the agent, or one of the environment's own walls. They also keep clear of the agent's reach, above and below it: for the swarm, its halo, 0.7 m above and below its middle, and its agents, 0.64 m as it speaks, so 0.75 m, and 0.05 m more; for another avatar, the radius of its bounds, taken at once as it grows and only once it has shrunk by 0.25 m, so that an animated avatar's changing bounds do not move them back and forth. The first of these that is clear is taken: in rows of at most 4 above the agent, the first row's faces just above its reach, the next 0.6 m above that; in one row of up to 8 above it at 0.7 of their size; in one row beneath it, its signs just below its reach, at full size, 0.7, or half, its faces no nearer the floor than a worker stands, 0.3 m; or in one row above it at half; and where none is, as the first. Each row is centred on the agent along the arc about whoever the workers gather about, the eyes at home, as far from them as the agent, the dials 0.65 m apart at full size, each facing them; where something stands in the way of a centred row, such as the Tomori wall beneath the agent, each dial stands as near the agent along the row as it is clear, trying half that spacing at a time to either side in turn, within 3 m of it, the row then read left to right; where home stands further than 3.2 m from them, all of it is larger in proportion, as a worker's sign is. A layout's own height rule holds for them too: a ring keeps nothing above 2.5 m. So on today's arc they stand in rows above the agent, within the 40° cone the arc keeps clear and above the status wall beneath it, and in a ring such as the farmyard's, whose workers stand about the agent up to 2.5 m, in one row beneath it, at 0.7 of their size, or, over the Tomori wall, to either side of it. There, at knee height and about 3.5 m from the arrival, a sign's row is about 9 pixels tall on the headset, and the ground among them can be landed on: the ring's height rule, the agent's halo and the floor leave a band of 0.4 m, which full-size signs do not fit, and dials, like workers, are not solid, so this is accepted rather than breaking the ring's rule; pointing at a dial names it to the agent whatever its size. An environment's registered placement and workers placed at other anchors are not taken into account. They stay at home wherever the agent goes, as its workers and status wall do (`environments.md`). A dial grows in over 0.35 s as its timer is set, glides to its new place as another before it goes, and shrinks away over 0.5 s as its timer is cancelled or ends.
- **Its face.** It is made of cubes, as a worker's flock is: sixty marks round a face 0.2 m across, every fifth longer, on a dark, nearly round plate. Its sector, lit in its state's colour from the top clockwise, the rest dim, is the time left to its next firing: the minutes left of an hour, and all of it from an hour on, so that it fills smoothly across the hour, the countdown saying how many hours; a hand points from its hub to the sector's end, and, while it waits, a mote goes round its rim once a minute as the seconds count down. A dial that first sees a firing within a minute, as of a timer set seconds ahead or one every minute, counts the seconds left of a minute instead, so that 30 seconds fill half its face and drain, its hand counting them without the mote; one that sees a longer firing counts the hour until it fires, so that its face never jumps as the last minute begins.
- **Its sign.** Above it stands its sign, as a worker's does, its bottom edge 0.1 m above the face, a leader down to it, turning to face each client's viewer, drawn at 0.8 of a worker's sign's size and at most 22 cells across, so that the signs of a row stand clear of one another: the timer's name, on at most two rows, its schedule, then its condition, on at most two rows, both muted, as the server words them, `every hour` and `when present`, and a pill in its state's colour, with its state's symbol, saying its countdown.
- **The countdown.** Each client works it out from the timer's `next` and its own clock each frame, so that it ticks without the server sending anything more: minutes and seconds within the hour, as `12:05`, or `0:27` for a timer set seconds ahead, rounded up to the second, so that it never says `0:00` before the firing; hours and minutes within the day, as `3h 05m`; then days and hours, as `2d 3h`; and `due now` once the firing is due, as while it waits for the agent's turn. The clock is reckoned from the steady clock between frames, so that it ticks smoothly, and read again from the system's clock whenever the two drift more than a second apart: forward, as after a headset or laptop slept while the steady clock stood still, or back, as the system's clock is corrected. A client whose clock differs from the server's counts down that much off.
- **Its states, each a look of its own.** Waiting: its sector in blue, the countdown in its pill under a clock symbol. Paused, while nobody is present for a timer that fires only while someone is: its marks grey, no hand and two bars across its hub, and its pill `Paused` with the countdown under two bars. Awaiting an arrival: every mark lit amber, no hand and its hub breathing, and its pill `On arrival` under an arrow through a doorway. Fired, the minute after it fires: every mark lit green, its hand at the top and its pill `Fired` under a burst; as it fires, the dial swells by a third and back over 1.2 s, its marks paling and back, once for each firing, as the client sees the timer turn from another state to fired while the space's state is current: not for a timer first seen just fired, as on joining, or joining again, within the minute, nor for a firing it learns of only as its state is current again after it reconnects; and again for each later firing, a one-off set again under the same name included. A state a newer server sends: as waiting, in violet, under a diamond.
- **Pointed at.** The ray, or the desktop's mouse, points at a dial as at a worker: within 0.2 m of its middle, growing with its distance beyond 3 m as a worker's sphere does, the nearer of a dial and a worker taken, a dial leaving never. The dial pointed at is drawn paler and a quarter larger, the ray ends on it where it passes nearest its middle, a hover ticks, the recording indicator says the words are about `timer NAME`, and words begun then name it as `Resource::Timer`, as above.
- **Cancelling.** Workers have no control of a participant's to remove them, so neither do timers: a participant cancels one by pointing at it and asking the agent, which cancels it with `cancel_timer`, needing nobody's `requested_by`, even in a turn a timer began, and everyone in the space, visitors too, sees its dial go.

The spectator draws the dials it works out itself, as the headset does, and sends them in its frames as `timer_dials`, which a frame from an older sender lacks.

## Rationale

In November 2026 the maintainer found that the agent coordinating a space, which answers participants aloud, cannot wake itself, and fakes a watchdog with a background `sleep`. They asked for timers on Tomori's server and tools, attached to presence and cron-like: "fire every morning at 9am when someone is present, or after 9am when they join for the first time", "fire every hour if someone is present", or just "fire every hour"; for a visualisation in the space like the workers'; and for delivery through a generic event queue that other sources will use next, such as a worker that failed, went idle without reporting or stalled, proposed separately as `events.md` on the `worker-events` branch.

**Structured fields and cron both.** Structured fields cover what people say, "every hour", "weekdays at nine", "at half past two", "in twenty minutes", each typed and checked, and are what an agent reads off a request most reliably; a cron string alone would make the agent translate every request into a notation it may get subtly wrong, and refuse nothing helpful. Cron earns a small place for what the fields cannot say, such as every half hour in working hours on weekdays, and its subset is the one every cron reader agrees on. Alignment to the day for an interval that divides it makes "every hour" mean on the hour, which participants expect and a countdown shows plainly; elapsed time keeps an interval from doubling or skipping at daylight saving. Wall-clock forms resolve gaps forward and overlaps to the first, so that a time of day fires once a day whatever the clocks do.

**Seconds.** During a workout in November 2026 the maintainer asked for a 30-second timer, and for timers with a granularity of a second. A single firing costs one turn whenever it falls, so `in_seconds` and `once_at` to the second may be as soon as a second ahead; times are kept in whole seconds, as before, and a timer set some seconds ahead counts from the nearest second, so that it is within half a second of what was asked, and the server looks at its timers as the soonest falls due rather than only about every second, so that it fires within milliseconds of that second. A repeating timer costs a turn at every firing, so its occurrences stay at least a minute apart, whatever its form, lowered from 5 minutes so that `every_seconds` and `every_minutes` agree: the event queue tells notes alone at most every 30 seconds and 10 seconds after a turn ends, so firings closer than a minute would mostly arrive late and coalesced, and a turn of the agent's own takes seconds; the space's limit of 96 firings a day bounds the rest, a timer every minute reaching it in an hour and a half, enough for a workout. A lower minimum only with a small `count` was considered and not taken, since the queue's pacing, not the count, is what would make such firings late. A minute rather than 5 costs no more in a day, since the 96 firings bound it either way, the default `present` stops it while nobody is there, and `always` keeps its 15 minutes; a forgotten timer every minute can spend the day's firings in an hour and a half, which its dial shows and cancelling it ends. A one-off's firing is told without the queue's 30-second gap, though still after a quiet moment, so that a 30-second timer set during a workout, perhaps 20 seconds after a note about a station, is not told 20 seconds late; a one-off costs the turn it was set for, so it cannot make a burst. `every_seconds` counts elapsed time rather than the clock, since intervals in a workout start when asked; `every_minutes` keeps the clock. Seconds are said only for schedules that have them, so that an hourly timer still reads `fired 10:00`, and a dial counts a minute on its face for a firing it first saw within a minute, where an hour's face would show a sliver.

**What present means.** Engagement is the only signal a client gives of someone actually being there (`spaces.md`), and `distracted` is still someone wearing the headset. A client that reports nothing counts as listening for speech, but the spectator, the one client that reports nothing on purpose, watches for an audience and is no one to tell, so timers count only a client that says someone is there. The grace keeps a headset lifted for a moment, or a reconnection, from skipping an hourly firing or counting as a fresh arrival; a skipped occurrence is not saved for later because a stale note on arrival is what `present_or_arrival` is for, and only when the agent asked for it.

**Coalescing and pacing.** An agent woken many times for one thing wastes its turns and talks over people, so occurrences missed while nobody could be told arrive as one, saying how many, and a burst of notes arrives as one prompt. Waiting a quiet moment after the agent spoke, and while anyone speaks, keeps the agent from interrupting itself or a participant; participants' words carry the notes so that the agent hears both at once rather than in two turns.

**Data, not instructions.** The note text is the agent's own, but a timer fires when no one is asking; marking it as Tomori's, with square brackets that participants' words never have, and superseding the words given last, keeps a timer from being taken for, or standing behind, a request. The limits bound what a timer can cost, which is the agent's turns, since an `always` timer can wake it with nobody present.

**A generic queue.** The queue knows sources and keys, not timers, so that the worker events of `events.md` push notes of their own source, one for each condition, and share the pacing, the framing and the supersession above, without another path into the agent's session; they add a pace of their own, at most once every 2 minutes for stalls, a failure told at once, and caps of their own, as `events.md` describes.

**Drawing in two increments.** Workers are drawn by a layout the server seats, their stations, signs and swarm flights, in `tomori-scene` and `tomori-render`; a timer's dial follows none of that closely enough to add in the same change without building most of a second layout. The first increment carried everything a client needs, the state and the pointing, so that the dials were a client change on a server that already sends them.

**Dials above the agent.** The maintainer asked for timers to be shown "in the same way we show sub-agents: there could be a timer". A timer belongs to the agent and its space, not to any work, so its dial stands at the agent's home rather than in a cluster. Room for it is worked out from the home's own layout, which every client and the server share, at every step it may take, so that the server need seat nothing more, every client stands them alike and no worker arriving moves them: on today's arc, the 40° cone above the agent, with the status wall beneath it, holds eight dials, but a ring such as the farmyard's stands workers about the agent up to its 2.5 m height, leaving room only beneath it. The agent's own reach, its halo above all, is kept clear, so that neither hides the other. A face that fills with the minutes of an hour and stays full beyond it reads like a kitchen timer, and never jumps at the hour. A pulse on the change it saw marks a firing once, not the state, nor a reconnection. Cubes and a sign above, at a worker's distances and sizes, make a dial read as one of the space's things beside the workers' flocks and signs, at the headset's twenty pixels to the degree. A countdown worked out by each client from one time ticks smoothly with no traffic. Distinct shapes as well as colours tell the states apart, as workers' symbols do (`feedback.md`).

## Scope

`tomori-scene`'s `dials.rs`, with `delegation::Scene::set_timers`, `dials` and `pointed_timer`, the frame step's pointing and clock in `scene.rs`, and `Conversation`'s naming of a timer pointed at; `tomori-render`'s timer signs in `workspace_graphics.rs` and `Renderer::set_dials`; and `tomori-client-spectator`'s `Frame::timer_dials`. `crates/server`'s `timers.rs` and `timers/schedule.rs`, the pure scheduler; `events.rs`, the event queue; `space.rs`, which holds both and gives a prompt of notes; `hub/agent_timers.rs`, the tools and the hub looking at timers; the `set_timer`, `timers` and `cancel_timer` definitions in `mcp.rs`; `kept.rs`'s `timers`; the server's `look_at_timers` in `server.rs`; and `hub/provenance.rs`'s refusal after a prompt of notes. `tomori-protocol`'s `Timer`, `TimerState`, `SpaceState::timers` and `Resource::Timer`.

## Open questions

- Whether a participant may cancel a timer from the ray or the hand menu, rather than by asking the agent, and who may: workers have no such control yet either.
- Whether the agent may place a timer's dial where its work is, as it places workers, rather than at its home; whether the dials should keep clear of an environment's registered placement and of workers placed at other anchors near home; and whether a client should correct its countdown for a clock that differs from the server's.
- Whether a timer may be one participant's, "remind me", firing only while they are present.
- Whether notes waiting in the queue should survive a restart, rather than only occurrences not yet looked at.
- Whether the limits, 8 timers, 96 firings a day and 24 unattended, suit real use; they are to be tuned from the logs.
