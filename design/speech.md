# Speech

This describes the target design for the Rust server that replaces `bridge/`, and for the headset client, which uses it. The current Node bridge differs; changes to it are not reviewed against this document. The headset sends push-to-talk (`agent`) audio and plays other participants and agent speech; its live microphone is described in `live-audio.md`.

## Requirement

Speech recognition and synthesis run on the PC server.

**The engines run in the server process, from Rust.** Recognition and synthesis are performed by sherpa-onnx, a native library called from the server's Rust code on threads the server owns, not by separate worker processes or an interpreter such as Python. Each engine is loaded when the server starts. A recognition or synthesis error fails only the utterance or sentence that saw it, and is reported. A native abort, such as a fault in the native library, takes the server down with it. A Rust panic in an engine disables that engine, visibly: it is logged once at error level, and until the server restarts, every open and later utterance fails, or every later sentence in the space is skipped, with a message to participants that recognition or synthesis is unavailable until the server restarts, rather than one inviting them to try again. When the server shuts down, it stops the engine threads before the process exits; see `server.md`. Transcript punctuation, below, is an exception: its errors and panics never fail an utterance or reach participants.

**Recognition starts as audio arrives.** Audio addressed to the agent is decoded incrementally while the participant is still speaking, with partial transcripts sent back during recording. The recognizer is a streaming model that decodes each part of the audio once, as it arrives, so finishing an utterance only decodes the audio received since the last decode rather than the whole recording. Utterances of several participants share the recognizer and are decoded in turn; an utterance that has ended is finished before further partial decodes. An utterance whose audio waits more than 5 seconds to be decoded fails visibly rather than queueing audio without bound, as its partial words would be stale and its transcript late by as much. Recognition never silently discards spoken audio; if it cannot produce a transcript, the participant is told and nothing is sent. The server closes an utterance when it reports the failure, even mid-recording, so the client forgets it without cancelling and the failure is not reported twice. An utterance whose agent audio has a gap fails visibly: sequence numbers skipped between two of its `agent` frames. Frames of another gate within an utterance are not recognized, so numbers skipped next to them do not fail it. The client never drops frames of an open utterance, of any gate, and sends them in order with the utterance's start and end, so its first frame is the first one after the start; the client may skip sequence numbers before an utterance, by not sending audio while the gate is off and by dropping congested live audio, so the first frame is not checked against earlier ones. A client that cannot deliver an utterance's audio cancels the utterance and tells the participant. Push-to-talk audio that arrives while no utterance is open is relayed to the other participants but not transcribed; the server counts and logs it.

**Final transcripts are punctuated.** The streaming recognizer produces lowercase words without punctuation, so the server restores capitals and punctuation in each utterance's final transcript with a sherpa-onnx punctuation model, on the recognition thread, before the transcript is reported, and ends a punctuated transcript with a full stop, question mark or exclamation mark. Punctuation changes only case and punctuation: if the model changes the words, or fails, the transcript is sent as recognized, and the utterance does not fail. A Rust panic in the punctuation model disables punctuation, logged once at error level, and later transcripts are sent as recognized until the server restarts; it does not disable recognition, and participants are not told, as their transcripts are still delivered. The punctuation model is unloaded with the recognizer, on the recognition thread. Partial transcripts are not punctuated, as their committed words must not change while the participant is speaking. If the punctuation model is not installed or cannot load, transcripts are lowercase and unpunctuated, and the server says so when it starts.

**The offline backend transcribes voice messages of up to 5 minutes whole.** With `--speech-backend offline`, each utterance is buffered and decoded with Whisper, whose window is 30 seconds: a short one once it ends, and a long one a segment at a time, as below, while it is spoken. The Whisper model is a configured choice, `small.en` by default; the server's operator may choose another with `--speech-offline-model`, such as `tiny.en`, which is smaller and faster but less accurate. An utterance of at most 28 seconds is decoded in one piece, as before. A longer one is split into consecutive segments of at most 28 seconds whose boundaries cover the whole utterance, with no audio left out or repeated, and every segment is decoded; each segment but the last ends at a pause in the last 10 seconds before its limit: the quietest point where the Silero voice activity detector hears no speech, or, when the detector is not installed or hears speech either throughout those 10 seconds or nowhere in them, the quietest point by loudness if it is markedly quieter than most of those 10 seconds. Only audio with no pause at all, such as a steady tone, is cut at the limit. The segments are decoded in order by the same recognizer, and their transcripts are joined in order, separated by single spaces, with empty ones left out. A segment's end depends only on the audio up to 1.125 seconds past its limit, the most that the search and the detector hear, so each segment but the last is cut and decoded as soon as that audio has arrived, while the participant is still speaking, at exactly the point where it would be cut once the utterance had ended; the detector resets for each search, so it hears the same samples either way, and only a panic that disables it partway through an utterance can change a later cut, as searches made while it is spoken and once it ends would fall on either side of the panic. Once the utterance ends, only the rest is left: its last segment, or its last two if it ends within 1.125 seconds past a segment's limit, besides a segment still being decoded. After each segment but the last, while it is spoken and after it ends, the participant is sent the words of the segments decoded so far as a partial transcript, as the streaming recognizer's partials are, so that the recording card shows the words of a long message while it is spoken and until its outcome; the final transcript is those words followed by the rest, so words shown never change. They are Whisper's words, capitalized and punctuated as the final transcript is. An utterance cancelled or abandoned while it is spoken, including one failed by a gap in its audio, is decoded no further, and nothing more is reported of it, not even the words of a segment being decoded as it was cancelled. A segment's transcript is left out if it is only bracketed notes of noise, such as "[BLANK_AUDIO]", "(static)" or "[ Silence ]", or if it is at most two words, such as "you", and the detector heard no speech in the segment; each such transcript is logged at info level. A longer transcript of a segment the detector heard no speech in is kept, in case the detector missed quiet speech, as are words the detector heard as speech, however short, such as "yes". This applies to an utterance that fits in one segment as to each segment of a longer one, so an utterance with no speech in it, such as a press to talk by accident, has an empty transcript. An error in any segment fails the whole utterance visibly, and nothing is sent; a detector failure or panic only falls back to loudness, logged, and never fails an utterance. An utterance that passes 5 minutes of audio fails at once, while the participant is still speaking, saying that it was longer than 5 minutes and nothing was sent, rather than transcribing its start and dropping the rest; this bounds the audio buffered per utterance to about 10 MB. Utterances of several participants share the recognizer: those that have ended take turns a segment at a time, the one that has waited longest, since it ended or since its last segment was decoded, first, so an utterance that ends while another's long one is being decoded waits for at most one of its segments, and no utterance waits for more than one segment of each other one per segment of its own. Utterances that have ended go before segments of utterances still being spoken, which take turns a segment at a time, the one whose segment has been decided longest, or since its last segment was decoded, first; so decoding while one participant speaks holds up words that have ended for at most the segment already being decoded, and an utterance that ends meanwhile is told that it waits. A segment of an utterance still being spoken waits while utterances that have ended are transcribed, and if its turn has not come when the utterance ends, it is decoded after the end, as before. The 5 second backlog rule above applies to the streaming recognizer, whose tentative words would go stale and whose decoding cannot be put off; the offline backend's partial words are whole segments that never change, and decoding them while the participant speaks only gives the final transcript a head start, so a segment that waits longer is decoded later rather than failing the utterance, and the length limit bounds its buffering instead.

**Words with no speech in them are not sent.** An utterance whose final transcript is empty, with either backend, is not sent to the agent: the participant's recording card says "Not sent: no speech heard", with the cue of other words that are not sent, for a few seconds, and the server logs it at info level. Nothing the participant said was lost, so it is not a problem: the problem line, the status pill and the right wrist's cue do not show it (see `interaction.md`). The server's reason is `tomori_protocol::NO_SPEECH`, by which clients tell it from other failures. The streaming recognizer writes nothing for audio without speech, so its transcript is empty; the offline backend leaves out what Whisper writes for it, as above. Notes of noise among words, such as "[Music] deploy it", are kept in the transcript, as they may say something of what was heard, and the queue shows them as heard; in what the agent reads, their square brackets are made parentheses, as Whisper's other notes are, so that they cannot pass for the context block Tomori puts before the words (see `spaces.md`).

**Synthesis starts before the reply completes.** The server consumes the agent's streaming text and begins synthesizing as soon as a document-level Markdown block of it has closed, rather than waiting for the message to complete. Following segments are synthesized while earlier ones play.

**The server says when it has an utterance, and what it is doing with it.** As an utterance's end arrives, and with it the last of its audio, since frames and control share one ordered stream, the server tells the participant that it is being transcribed, before anything recognition reports of it. Until its outcome, it then says when the utterance waits for the shared recognizer behind other words, another participant's or the participant's own earlier ones, and, when it is transcribed in more than one segment, which part is being transcribed, counted among all of its segments, as part 3 of 3, as each segment left after its end starts; an utterance that waited is said to be transcribed again when its turn comes. Segments decoded while it was spoken are not announced, as nothing is said of an utterance's progress before it ends; their words are shown instead, as above. The offline backend says these; the streaming backend, which finishes an utterance almost at once, says nothing after the acknowledgement, and the outcome follows. This is `transcript_progress`, with a stage, `transcribing` or `waiting`, and an optional part, sent only for an utterance whose `utterance_begin` set `progress`, and only on the connection that sent it, as older clients would refuse a message they do not know: a participant who reconnects while their words are transcribed, perhaps with an older client, is told only their outcome; older servers ignore the field and send none, so a client shows the words as being sent until their outcome. A stage a client does not know is shown as transcribing. Nothing is said of an utterance that fails before it ends. The server logs at info level, for each utterance that ends, how long its outcome took after its end arrived, and how much of that it waited for the recognizer, so that where the time goes can be seen.

**The server says how long an utterance may be.** A server whose recognition has a limit on the length of an utterance, as the offline backend's 5 minutes, gives it in seconds in its `Welcome`, as `utterance_limit`; it is absent without a limit and from older servers. The headset shows a recording's time against it and warns 30 and 10 seconds before it is reached (see `hand-menu.md`); without it, the headset shows the time alone. The limit is not a client constant, since it belongs to the recognition the server runs.

**Speech consumes whole document-level blocks.** The agent's streaming Markdown is divided into document-level blocks (paragraphs, headings, lists, block quotes, tables, code blocks and the like), and a block is passed on only once it has closed and no later text can change it, together with every block before it. A block is never passed on in part, and nothing from a block is spoken before it closes. Lists are passed on whole, with all of their items, as are block quotes, tables and code blocks. Each block is available with its complete structure (its kind, its source span and its Markdown events, including nested blocks) alongside the sentences spoken for it, so that a later step can show a table or code block, or run a command, as a unit. Each closed block is queued as a single segment, its speakable sentences joined, so the voice keeps the cadence of the paragraph, list or other block it came from; the sentences of a block are split at a clause or word break only as a guard against the pathological block, a single line of several thousand characters, which is split at the limit so synthesis of most of a reply is not deferred. A block with nothing speakable, such as code, is silent. How the streamed text is divided into deltas does not change the blocks or the segments. A breath of 400 ms of silence follows each spoken segment, so that back-to-back blocks, such as the items of a list or a series of paragraphs, do not run together; the silence is real in the stream, not a stall, and is short enough to keep the voice sounding like one speaker taking pauses between thoughts. The default, `block`, speaks each closed block in one go; `--speech-segmentation sentence` speaks each sentence of a block separately instead, so that the first sentence of a long block starts while the rest of the block is still closing.

**The agent speaks with one English voice.** Synthesis uses Kokoro's American English `af_heart` voice, speaker 3 of the Kokoro v1.0 voice table.

**Text is phonemized in Rust, and the server links no copyleft speech code.** The server turns each segment's text into Kokoro's phonemes itself, with a permissively licensed grapheme-to-phoneme library whose phonemes are misaki's, the set Kokoro was trained on, and gives sherpa-onnx the phonemes, one sentence at a time; neither the server nor any library it links for speech is under the GPL or another copyleft licence, so espeak-ng and piper-phonemize are not linked. A word that the library's lexicons lack, such as a name like "Tomori", is pronounced, by the library's own model for unknown words, never dropped or spelled as an unknown marker. Before it is phonemized, text is normalized for what the agent writes: a time such as "3:45" is read as a time, with no pause for its colon, and only ASCII digits are read as times and dates, as no text may make the phonemizer fail; ISO dates, a unit after a number, such as "180 ms", Rust paths and generics, identifiers in snake or camel case, file names and paths are read as a person would read them; a number of four digits that cannot be a year, such as a port, is read as a number rather than as a year; and emoji are not read. Words that the library gets wrong are given their pronunciations, from a dictionary of whole words, matched in any case and within punctuation and code formatting: Tomori's own, in `crates/speech/assets/pronunciations.toml`, such as "README" as "read me", and "id", which in Tomori is an identifier, as its letters, "I D", as are acronyms that it reads as words in lowercase, such as "apk", and, adding to and replacing them, the operator's, in `pronunciations.toml` in the server's state directory, read when synthesis starts. Each is a respelling that the library reads correctly, such as "git hub", or Kokoro's phonemes between slashes, in misaki's notation; an entry that cannot be used, or a file that cannot be read or is not TOML, is logged and left out, and the rest are used. A word is added to Tomori's only once the library is found to get it wrong, and `crates/speech/readme.md` says how. Heteronyms whose part of speech the library's tagger misreads are decided by the words next to them: "live" is "laɪv" after a form of "go" or "be", such as "go live" and "is live", a determiner, or "make it", and before a noun it describes, such as "live log", and "lɪv" after a subject, a modal or "to", such as "I live"; and "use", "record" and "present" that start a sentence before their object are verbs. Elsewhere the tagger decides. Sentences are divided where they were before phonemization moved into the server, at `.`, `!`, `?` and `;`, joining a very short sentence to the one before it, so that segmentation, streaming and the time to the first audio are unchanged; a sentence longer than Kokoro accepts is split at a clause or word break. A segment with nothing to pronounce, such as an emoji alone, is silent, not a failure. The phonemizer is reached through an interface inside the speech crate, so that a language pack (#103) can supply its own for another language; only American English is supplied now.

**Only speakable text is spoken.** Speakable text is selected from Markdown structure: code blocks, tool output, internal reasoning, raw HTML and Markdown syntax are not spoken, and tables are not read cell by cell. A line that later becomes a table header or setext heading is never spoken as a paragraph, because its block has not closed before the line's role is known.

**Agent speech is not interrupted for the space by participant input.** Each space has one agent speech stream that everyone in the space listening live receives, participants and visitors alike. Recording, sending or queueing input does not stop, pause or skip it for the space, nor for anyone else in it. A participant may mute their own playback locally; muting applies to agent speech played again for them as well as to live speech, and other participants still play. A client silences its own playback while its participant records push-to-talk, as `live-audio.md` requires, so that the reply is not transcribed as their input; the stream continues meanwhile. A reply that starts while a participant records, however, is held for them: they are not sent it live, and once they stop recording, however the recording ends, by sending, cancelling or failing, they hear it from its start, then everything after it, as a catch-up from `REPLAY_SOURCE`, until they reach live speech, so that they are not talked over by something else while they answer and do not miss the start of it. When they stop by letting go of X, ending their words, what was held starts after a brief pause, 600 ms (`RELEASE_PAUSE`), so that the agent does not launch in the moment they let go; nothing is sent to them during it, and pressing X again during it holds it again, for as long as they record. A recording that ends otherwise, cancelled, as on losing focus, or failing, plays what was held at once, and live speech, a catch-up on joining or returning and a replay have no pause. While a reply is held for them, as they record and through the pause, the server tells their client so (`ServerMessage::SpeechHeld`, to a client whose Hello says it reads it), and the status pill says `reply waiting`, with a violet ring, `○`, over what the agent is doing and over speech already playing, silenced, so that they know something is queued for them rather than taking the agent for idle; the swarm shows the agent preparing to speak, as it does for speech about to come. Once the server no longer holds it, the pill still says a reply is waiting until it plays on their device, for up to the swarm's hold, as it takes a moment to arrive, so that it goes straight to `speaking`; the pill says the agent is speaking only once the held speech starts playing, since it follows playback, as the swarm does, and nothing plays during the pause; nor is it their turn meanwhile, since they spoke last (see below). A reply already playing as they begin goes on live, silenced, and skipped on their device, as below; a catch-up or replay they are hearing goes on until it reaches a reply that started since they began, and waits there. A replay asked for while they record plays once they stop. Going away, leaving and returning keep what is held, as they keep any position in the kept speech. Everyone else in the space hears the reply live, as before, and its text shows on the panel and the transcript as usual. Only a participant whose client reports engagement is held, since such a client plays speech played again; one whose client reports nothing hears live speech, as before.

**Speaking over the agent skips the rest of what it is saying, on that device.** When a participant presses X to talk while the agent is speaking on their device, live or played again, as the swarm shows it, including the swarm's hold after speech stops (below), their client takes the turn rather than talking over it: playback fades out over a few milliseconds and is silent before the microphone opens, as for any push-to-talk, the agent's speech fading out over the same 10 ms rather than stopping on a step, which would click, and the rest of the assistant message the agent was saying stays silent on that device after they let go, rather than resuming mid-sentence. A replay or catch-up they are hearing is stopped, so that they then follow live speech. Skipped speech is still consumed in real time and measured, as muted speech is, so it plays and ends as it would aloud, and the swarm still speaks for as long as it plays, as the others still hear it; the status pill says the agent is speaking, skipped (see `interaction.md`). The skip ends as soon as the agent says something else, when speech of another message is announced, live or played again, so that its answer to what the participant said, or the next message of its turn, is heard from its start; when the participant asks for a reply to be played again with the right stick, which hears what was skipped; and on leaving the space or losing the connection. The skip is local: other participants, the stream and the kept speech are unchanged, and nothing is sent to the server but the stop of a replay. The words skipped stay in the transcript on the right wrist, and the right stick plays the reply again. Pressing X when the agent is not speaking skips nothing. Push-to-talk is captured with the recognition input preset, which has no echo cancellation; the agent's speech is kept out of the transcript by silencing playback before the microphone opens, as `live-audio.md` requires, not by an echo canceller, which the Quest is asked for only on the live microphone, and which is not yet verified there.

**It is the participant's turn once the agent has finished speaking to them.** A client tells its participant that it is their turn when the agent has finished speaking a reply they heard: when agent speech that was heard on their device, neither muted, skipped nor silenced while they recorded, has stopped playing for longer than the swarm's hold, and the agent is idle, with no approval or question waiting, nothing queued and none of their own words being recorded or on their way. The status pill's dot then settles to idle with one gentle pulse (see `interaction.md`), with a soft cue and a very light tap (see `feedback.md`). It is told once for each reply heard: not again until more speech is heard, not after a reply that spoke nothing, nor after speech they muted or skipped, since they heard nothing new; and pressing X takes the turn, so that what they speak over is no turn of theirs. Visitors, who cannot speak to the agent, are not told.

**The swarm speaks while agent speech plays.** On the headset, the desktop window and the spectator, the swarm shows the agent speaking for as long as agent speech plays on that device, live or played again, a catch-up or replay, rather than while the agent's session is busy, which is often idle by then: synthesis runs ahead of playback, so most of a reply is heard after the session has finished it. Speech plays from when the client's player starts consuming it until its buffer runs dry; the breaths between segments are real silence in the stream, so they play. The swarm keeps speaking for 0.6 s after speech stops playing, longer than a breath or a buffer running dry for a moment, so that a reply is spoken throughout without flickering back; after that it shows the agent as it is, and a session that is ready shows ready at once, without settling through preparing again. Speaking shows over everything the session is doing, working, waiting on a request or ready, so that a participant sees the agent speak whatever it has gone on to do; only the participant's own recording, shown as listening, is shown over it, as push-to-talk silences playback meanwhile. A participant who has muted agent speech locally, or skipped it by speaking over it, still sees the swarm speak while it plays, at the level of the speech itself, measured before muting, since the others in the space hear it; muted and skipped speech is still consumed in real time, so it plays and ends as it would aloud. The swarm's ripple follows the level of the speech playing, falling with it. The status pill on the headset and the desktop window says that the agent is speaking for exactly as long as the swarm does, with the same hold, so that the two never disagree (see `interaction.md`).

Only newly produced assistant text is spoken; history from before the space was created or attached is never read aloud.

**Spoken replies are kept for those who missed them.** The server keeps the audio of each space's agent speech, the announcements, frames and breaths exactly as sent live, for the latest two replies and at most 5 minutes of audio, dropping the oldest first; a reply is the speech of one agent turn, from its first assistant message after the agent was idle, so that a busy state the backend repeats, as when it reads a session again, does not start another. Speech is synthesized and kept whether or not anyone is listening. A space that closes keeps no speech, so that the closed spaces the server still lists hold no audio: what it still speaks as it closes is heard live by everyone in it, and nothing is played again. A participant who is in a space and listening hears live speech; one who has left, or whose client reports them away (see `spaces.md`), is not sent it, and the server keeps the position up to which they heard. When a participant joins a space or returns to listening, having missed any part of the latest reply, they hear that reply from its start, then everything after it, at the rate it plays, until they reach live speech, which they then follow. Meanwhile they are not sent live speech, so nobody hears the same reply twice at once, and other participants are unaffected. Someone who heard the whole latest reply hears nothing again. A participant whose client never reports engagement, such as an older client or the spectator, follows live speech as before: joining mid-segment, they hear it from that point. Visitors (see `security.md`) always follow live speech, the same way.

**A reply can be played again to one participant.** A participant who is listening can ask for the latest reply, or a kept reply by its message, to be played again from its start to them alone; one who is away is refused, as nothing is played to them. It plays as a catch-up does, and stops as soon as a later reply starts live, which they then hear live from its start. A participant may stop a replay or catch-up at any time and follow live speech. Agent speech played again is sent from its own source, `REPLAY_SOURCE`, so that a client can tell it from live speech; its segments are announced again, marked as played again.

**Replies can be brief.** Each space has a speech setting, `full` or `brief`, shared by everyone in it and changeable by any participant, from the next reply on. A new space is `full`, and a space is `brief` only when a participant chooses it; the choice is kept with the space across a server restart (see `spaces.md`), and a space kept before the setting was stored is `full`. A brief reply speaks each assistant message's whole blocks while they total at most about 20 seconds of speech, estimated as 300 characters of speakable text, and leaves out the rest of that message; a block that does not fit but starts within the first 150 characters, such as the paragraph after a heading or a short lead-in, is cut after the last sentence that fits instead, and the first block always speaks at least one sentence. The first message of a reply that is cut says "More on the panel." where it is cut, once per reply. What is left out is shown, and not spoken. The limit applies to each message rather than the whole turn, so that a turn's closing answer is not left unspoken after earlier messages about tool use. A full reply is spoken in full, as before.

## Rationale

Conversation latency matters most at both ends of a turn: participants should see their words transcribed while speaking, and hear the agent as soon as it has something to say. Replies are in English, so they are spoken with an English voice; participants preferred `af_heart` to the Japanese `jf_alpha` used before. Participants can queue further input instead of interrupting; see `spaces.md`. Agreed in the October 2026 design discussion.

Running the engines in Rust in the server process was decided in November 2026. The user expected speech to be "all implemented in Rust and real time / streaming as early as possible", and accepted native libraries ("Rust, native libs OK"); sherpa-onnx was chosen for both recognition and synthesis. Removing the Python workers removes their setup and the cost of passing audio between processes, and lets recognition use a streaming model instead of re-decoding a sliding window with Whisper, so the window recognition path was removed. In-process inference gives up the workers' process isolation: a native crash in an engine stops the server instead of one worker that could be restarted. The user accepted that trade-off. A Rust panic can be contained, so it disables only its engine; an engine left running after an unexpected panic could be in an inconsistent state, so it is not restarted, and participants are told it is unavailable so that they do not retry in vain. The 5 second recognition backlog allows for the model holding back about a second of audio, and for decoding at well under real time on the reference Mac even under heavy load. The streaming model was chosen as the default over Whisper `small.en` by the benchmark in `crates/speech/readme.md`, where it showed partial words sooner and finished almost at once with similar accuracy. In November 2026 the user found the streaming transcripts "way less accurate" in the headset and asked to buffer the whole voice message and decode it with a higher quality model, so the server gained an opt-in offline backend (`--speech-backend offline`) that buffers each utterance and decodes it whole with Whisper `small.en`, which the benchmark measured at 0.6% WER against the streaming transducer's 1.2%. The offline backend is not the default: it cannot show a transcript while the participant is still speaking, beyond the words of each finished part of a long message, and the final one arrives a couple of seconds after they let go rather than almost at once, so the streaming backend stays the default and the offline one is chosen by a flag. Later in November 2026 the user asked for longer voice messages with the same accuracy, as the offline backend failed any utterance longer than Whisper's 30 second window. Cutting a message into windows at arbitrary points would split words, which each window would then decode without the other half, so segments end at pauses. sherpa-onnx ships the Silero voice activity detector, so it is used to hear the pauses, with loudness as the fallback when its model is missing and to choose between points it hears as silent; the quietest point is a better place to cut than a fixed one even in speech without a clear pause. The detector decides only when it hears a pause: it merges silences shorter than a tenth of a second into the speech around them, so where it hears speech throughout, loudness can still find a pause it missed. Segments are at most 28 seconds because sherpa-onnx keeps the end of Whisper's window for padding after the audio, and the 10 second search keeps segments long, so few are needed, while leaving room to find a pause. On a 59 second LibriSpeech recording with breaths of 0.4 seconds between sentences, segmenting with either the detector or loudness alone gave a transcript identical to the reference apart from British spellings, decoding at about 0.15 times real time on a 32-core Linux PC; a 5 minute message would take about 45 seconds there after it ends. The 5 minute limit keeps that wait, and the audio buffered for each participant, bounded; a longer message fails visibly rather than losing its end, as recognition never silently discards spoken audio. Whisper writes words for a clip without speech, such as "you" for silence and "(water running)" for faint noise, so a segment of a long message that holds only a long pause would add words the participant did not say; the detector heard no speech in 28 seconds of silence or faint noise and takes about 40 ms for 24 seconds of audio, so checking each segment of a long message costs little. Each segment is still decoded, and only a note or a word or two is dropped on the detector's word, so a detector that misses quiet speech loses at most two words of a segment rather than all of it. Short messages were at first left as they were, as the change was asked for long ones. Later in November 2026, a press of X by accident with the offline backend sent "[BLANK_AUDIO]" to the agent as the participant's message, and the agent had to reply that it had not caught it; the maintainer agreed that such a recording say on its card that no speech was heard, rather than be sent, so the same rule now applies to an utterance of one segment, and both behave alike. A detector that misses a quiet "yes" in a short message loses it, but the participant is told that nothing was sent and can say it again, which costs less than every silent recording reaching the agent; the detector takes a few milliseconds for a short message. Taking turns a segment at a time, in the order utterances have waited, keeps one participant's long message from holding up another's short one for longer than one segment, and keeps a stream of short messages from holding up a long one indefinitely. Segments were at first found and decoded once the utterance ended, so a long message waited after release for every segment, about 2.9 seconds each with `small.en` on the reference PC. In November 2026 the maintainer agreed to decode them while the participant is still speaking: each cut depends only on the audio up to a little past its limit, so the server makes the same cuts during recording and the transcript is unchanged, while the wait after release is at most about one segment's decode; on the minute of LibriSpeech, spoken into `small.en` in real time on the reference PC, the transcript, unchanged, came about 2 seconds after release rather than about 8, with one of its three segments left. The recording card shows the words of each part as it is transcribed, which the offline backend could not show before. The existing partial transcript carries those words, so the protocol is unchanged and older clients show them too. Utterances that have ended go first, as they do before the streaming recognizer's partial decodes, so that decoding ahead for one participant never delays another's finished words by more than the segment in progress; deciding whether to decode ahead and whether an utterance has ended under the same lock keeps an end that is on its way from waiting unannounced. A short message is still decoded once it ends, as its one segment is not decided until then. Also in November 2026, the user asked for the model tests to run in CI, where `small.en` (a 640 MB download, 1.3 GB unpacked) was too large to fetch on every run, and decided that a smaller Whisper model for the tests be an ordinary configured choice of model rather than a special case for tests, as models are to become configurable per space with language packs (#103). The tests and CI therefore configure `tiny.en`, through the same configuration the server uses, while the server keeps `small.en` by default. On the minute of LibriSpeech, `tiny.en` gave the same words as `small.en`, in about a quarter of the time.

Saying when an utterance has arrived was asked for in November 2026. Speaking from the headset, the user found the recording card saying "Sending…" for a while after releasing X, and could not tell network transfer from the server working, so sending seemed slow. Audio streams while the participant speaks, so by the release most of it is already on the server, and with the offline backend most of the wait is Whisper decoding the utterance once it ends. The acknowledgement is sent as the end arrives because that is when the server has all of the audio, whatever the backend; the stages and parts come from the recognizer, which alone knows them. Parts are counted by finding every segment's end once the utterance's first segment is due, rather than estimated from its length, since segments end at pauses and an estimate would be wrong; the detector resets for each search, so the segments are the same, and finding them costs about 40 ms for each 24 seconds of audio. An utterance that is told it waits is told so before the recognizer can report anything of it, so the stages arrive in order. The client asks for progress per utterance, as answers are marked per utterance, so that the protocol stays at the same version and neither an older client nor an older server is affected; progress goes only to the connection that asked, since the participant's next connection may be an older client's, and a newer client gains nothing from it then, as it forgets words on their way when it disconnects.

Punctuating final transcripts was approved in November 2026, after the streaming model's transcripts proved to be lowercase and unpunctuated because its vocabulary has neither; the larger streaming model that punctuates was too slow on the reference Mac. Punctuated transcripts are easier for participants to read and closer to typed input for the agent. The English CNN-BiLSTM model (`sherpa-onnx-online-punct-en-2024-08-06`) was chosen over sherpa-onnx's Chinese and English CT-Transformer, which was faster but writes Chinese full-width punctuation and does not restore capitals. It costs about 8 ms of processor time per transcript, small beside the time to finish an utterance, so it runs on the recognition thread rather than its own. Punctuation is an improvement to a transcript that is already correct, so a punctuation failure falls back to the recognized words rather than failing the utterance or disabling recognition. Partials are left lowercase because the model decides each mark and capital from the words that follow, so punctuating partials would change words already shown as committed, and would cost a punctuation per decode on the thread every utterance shares; the line changes once, when the final transcript replaces it. The model rarely punctuates the last word of its input, so the server punctuates the transcript followed by a probe word, "i", which usually starts a sentence, and removes it; any utterance still without a closing mark ends with a full stop.

Keeping speech for those who missed it was decided in November 2026. The user came back to a space mid-reply and could not follow the speech without what came before, and asked that, while nobody is in a space, its replies be kept and played from the start of the latest reply when someone joins, instead of joining mid-sentence. The interaction proposal (PR #65) generalized this to anyone who was not listening, including someone who took their headset off, and the user chose that a returning participant hears the latest reply from its start, rather than jumping to live speech with a notice. Two replies are kept so that a participant still catching up on one when the next starts keeps hearing it; 5 minutes of 24 kHz audio is about 14 MB per space. A replay is local listening, not input, so live speech of a later reply wins over it, while a catch-up continues behind live speech so that the reply it started is heard whole. Clients that do not report engagement stay at the live edge, because a server cannot tell whether they are listening; the spectator follows live speech for its audience. The user relies on hearing replies and asked for them to be briefer; brief speech was made the default by the user's decision, cut at block boundaries so that a thought is not stopped mid-paragraph, with the rest on the panel. Later in November 2026, speaking from the headset, the user found the panel hard to read and asked for replies to be spoken in full by default: "Why do you keep on saying more on the panel? It's better if you speak it, because the panel is just terrible." By then the agent's own instructions asked it to reply briefly (see `spaces.md`), so its replies were already short. Brief speech stays available as a space's choice. The setting was not kept across a restart before, so every kept space returns as `full`, and only a choice made since keeps a space `brief`.

Consuming whole document-level blocks was decided in November 2026. Tables, code blocks and lists are units that may later be shown, or run in the case of commands, as well as spoken, so each must arrive with its complete structure rather than as characters or partial items; the user chose this over speaking text as soon as each part of it is stable. The accepted cost is latency until the first block closes: a paragraph followed by a blank line is spoken at the blank line, a list once the next block starts, and the last block of a message when the message completes. A block that follows another without a blank line, such as a list directly after an introductory "Here's how:", does not prove that the earlier block has ended, so the introduction waits until the list ends too. While one block is spoken, the next has usually arrived, so speech continues without gaps. Splitting a closed block into sentences keeps the time to first audio of that block to the synthesis of one sentence. Releasing list items one at a time may be tried later if the latency of long lists proves too high. The blocks come from base-markdown's incremental parser, which emits a document-level block once appended text can no longer change it and makes the result independent of how the text is divided; a reference or footnote definition therefore applies only to uses in its own or a later block, and an earlier use is read by its text.

In November 2026 the maintainer, told that the server statically linked GPL espeak-ng, approved phonemising in Rust so Kokoro's voice stays and the speech stack is permissively licensed. sherpa-onnx 1.13 linked espeak-ng and piper-phonemize (GPL-3.0) into the server, which used them for words missing from Kokoro's lexicon; sherpa-onnx 2.0 removed them so that it is purely Apache-2.0, and from then on dropped such words, "Tomori" among them, unless the caller passes phonemes, which is why the server had stayed on 1.13. Replacing Kokoro would have changed the voice participants chose, so the text is phonemized in the server instead, with `loqui-g2p` (MIT), a Rust port of misaki, Kokoro's own G2P, with misaki's Apache-2.0 lexicons and an Apache-2.0 neural model trained on them for unknown words. `misaki-rs` (MIT), more widely used, was tried beside it: without espeak-ng it marks every unknown word unknown, so "Tomori", "Silero", "fmt" and file paths were lost, and a fallback model would have had to be added to it, while `loqui-g2p` pronounced them all. `loqui-g2p` is young, so it is pinned to an exact version, and needs Rust 1.92, which became the workspace's minimum. Through the upgrade, recognition was unchanged: the speech tests passed, Whisper `tiny.en`'s transcripts of the minute of LibriSpeech were identical, by Silero, by loudness and decoded while spoken, and the recognition benchmark's LibriSpeech results were identical for the streaming transducer and `small.en`. The library left a colon in "3:45", which Kokoro reads as a pause, and read port 8443 as a year and file paths as one run-on word, so the server normalizes such text first; the cases are those an agent's replies were seen to contain. Sentences are divided as 1.13 divided them so that the voice's pacing and the time to first audio stay as they were: over 22 segments typical of replies, the mean time to the first audio was 0.70 seconds against 0.77 before, and the real-time factor 0.182 against 0.179, within the noise of the machine (see `crates/speech/readme.md`). Language packs (#103) are to be pluggable per language, an optional espeak-ng pack among them for languages without a permissive phonemizer, so the phonemizer was put behind an interface now, although packs themselves are not built yet. Emoji were read by name before, such as "party popper", which says nothing a listener needs, so they are now left out.

Later in November 2026 the maintainer listened to pairs of the same sentences spoken by the old pipeline and the new, judged the new one better overall, and asked for what it got wrong to be fixed: "go live" and "goes live" were said "liv", and "README", which espeak-ng had read as "read me", was spelled letter by letter, while "lives" as a verb, past-tense "read", "the live preview" and "crates.io", which espeak-ng had got wrong, were already right. The maintainer asked for a dictionary of whole words, such as technical words and names, kept as data so that words can be added without changing code, and for rules from context for the heteronyms the library gets wrong. Every word was tried with the library first, and only those it got wrong were added, so that the dictionary does not drift from what the library says; "GitHub", "OpenCode", "macOS", "iOS", "OAuth", "CLI", "API" and "URL" were right. Respellings are preferred to phonemes, as they are easier to read and check, while phonemes give exact stress where it matters, as in "README". An operator's file in the state directory lets a server say its own names rightly without a new build; it is read once, as the server's other state is, rather than watched, which would cost more than restarting. The library's tagger read "go live" as "go" and a verb, as in "go see", and a verb that starts a sentence, such as "Use JSON.", as a name because it is capitalised; it was right for "read" in each tense of the listening test, and for "close", "content", "object" and "wind", so rules decide only the cases it got wrong, from the words next to them, and leave the rest to it. "lead" as the metal and "minute" as "tiny" were also misread, but are rare in replies, and were left. The dictionary and the rules give the library the phonemes with misaki's own markup, which it honours, so that they need nothing from it beyond its public interface and survive an upgrade; they are tested with the sentences the maintainer listened to.

In November 2026 the maintainer found, on the headset, that the swarm left its speaking animation almost at once and went idle while a reply was still being heard: since voice moved to the server, the swarm had followed only the agent's session, which is idle once its reply is written, long before it has been spoken. The maintainer asked that the swarm speak for as long as speech is audibly playing, on each device from its own playback, and that it not flicker between segments. Following playback rather than the server's speech events shows what the participant hears, including a catch-up that only they hear; the hold is in the swarm's signals, by frame time, so that it is tested deterministically. A participant who mutes speech has chosen not to hear it, not to stop the agent speaking to the others, so the swarm still speaks for them.

In November 2026, speaking from the headset, the maintainer found the pill showing idle while speech still played, and talked over the agent: speech takes longer to play than the agent took to write it, so the agent is idle on the server while its reply is still heard, and the pill, which followed only the server, said idle, which they took to mean it was their turn. They asked for better cohesion between replying and idle, so that they know when they can speak without interrupting. The pill now says the agent is speaking from the same playback the swarm follows, with the swarm's hold, and over whatever the agent is doing, as the swarm shows it: both say that it is not yet the participant's turn, and the agent's work shows again in the pauses and once speech ends. A turn is told only once speech heard has ended and the agent is free, since an agent still working, or waiting on a request, has more to say or needs something else first; it is told softly, as the end of speech is a change worth marking but must not become a chime after every reply. Speaking over the agent skips the rest of what it was saying rather than pausing it, to resume after, or ducking it: push-to-talk already silenced playback, so that the agent's words were never recorded over the participant's, but the reply then resumed mid-sentence once they let go, over the start of the answer to what they had just said. Someone who speaks over the agent means to take the turn, the text stays on the right wrist, and the right stick plays it again, so nothing is lost. The skip ends at the next message the agent announces rather than at a pause, since a reply's speech can stall between blocks while the agent writes, and a pause would let the rest of what was skipped resume; the answer to what the participant said is always another message. Ducking is to replace silencing only once echo cancellation is verified (see `live-audio.md`).

In November 2026, speaking from the headset, the maintainer found a spoken "yes" to one question delivered after a sub-agent's completion had prompted another (see `interaction.md`). Holding a reply that starts while a participant records keeps them from being talked over, while they answer, by the agent asking something else, and keeps what they had heard as it was as they began, which the note before their words describes; the kept speech already plays a missed reply from its start, so the hold is a catch-up that waits for the recording to end. A reply already playing is not held, since the participant chose to speak over it; how push-to-talk ducks or stops speech playing on the device is the client's, and unchanged by the hold.

Also in November 2026, the maintainer found on the Quest 3 that held speech waited until X was released but then launched straight in the moment they let go, which felt a little jarring, and asked for a brief pause after release, of about 100 to 500 ms, for continuity. Held speech released by letting go of X was made to start 300 ms later, a breath shorter than the one between segments; only then, since the pause is for the hand-over from the participant to the agent, and nothing else they hear follows their own words. The pause is on the server, which sends nothing during it, so the pill and the swarm, which follow playback, show the agent speaking only once its speech is heard, rather than during a silence; a press of X during it keeps the speech held, as the participant has more to say. The maintainer then tried 300 ms on the headset and found the beat noticeable, but asked for about twice as long, so the pause is now 600 ms, a little longer than the breath between segments, as befits the hand-over from one speaker to another. The maintainer also found that, while a reply was held for them, the pill said idle and the swarm looked idle, so that they took it that nothing was queued, and suggested saying that it waits instead. The server, which alone knows that it holds speech, now tells the participant's client, which says `reply waiting` on the pill, close to `speaking` in its colour and dot, as speech is about to follow, but told from it, as nothing plays yet; the swarm shows the agent preparing, the pose it already had for speech about to come. An older client is not told, and shows the pill as before.

## Scope

The server's recognition and synthesis pipelines, the speakable-text filter and segmenter, the phonemizer and its normalization of text, the speech stream sent to clients and the spectator mixer, and how clients show the agent speaking, from their player in `tomori-client` to the swarm's signals in `tomori-scene`, and when it is the participant's turn and what speaking over the agent skips, in `tomori-scene`'s `turn.rs` and the conversation.
