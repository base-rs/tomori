# Speech

This describes the target design for the Rust server that replaces `bridge/`, and for the headset client, which uses it. The current Node bridge differs; changes to it are not reviewed against this document. The headset sends push-to-talk (`agent`) audio and plays other participants and agent speech; its live microphone is described in `live-audio.md`.

## Requirement

Speech recognition and synthesis run on the PC server.

**The engines run in the server process, from Rust.** Recognition and synthesis are performed by sherpa-onnx, a native library called from the server's Rust code on threads the server owns, not by separate worker processes or an interpreter such as Python. Each engine is loaded when the server starts. A recognition or synthesis error fails only the utterance or sentence that saw it, and is reported. A native abort, such as a fault in the native library, takes the server down with it. A Rust panic in an engine disables that engine, visibly: it is logged once at error level, and until the server restarts, every open and later utterance fails, or every later sentence in the space is skipped, with a message to participants that recognition or synthesis is unavailable until the server restarts, rather than one inviting them to try again. When the server shuts down, it stops the engine threads before the process exits; see `server.md`. Transcript punctuation, below, is an exception: its errors and panics never fail an utterance or reach participants.

**Recognition starts as audio arrives.** Audio addressed to the agent is decoded incrementally while the participant is still speaking, with partial transcripts sent back during recording. The recognizer is a streaming model that decodes each part of the audio once, as it arrives, so finishing an utterance only decodes the audio received since the last decode rather than the whole recording. Utterances of several participants share the recognizer and are decoded in turn; an utterance that has ended is finished before further partial decodes. An utterance whose audio waits more than 5 seconds to be decoded fails visibly rather than queueing audio without bound, as its partial words would be stale and its transcript late by as much. Recognition never silently discards spoken audio; if it cannot produce a transcript, the participant is told and nothing is sent. The server closes an utterance when it reports the failure, even mid-recording, so the client forgets it without cancelling and the failure is not reported twice. An utterance whose agent audio has a gap fails visibly: sequence numbers skipped between two of its `agent` frames. Frames of another gate within an utterance are not recognized, so numbers skipped next to them do not fail it. The client never drops frames of an open utterance, of any gate, and sends them in order with the utterance's start and end, so its first frame is the first one after the start; the client may skip sequence numbers before an utterance, by not sending audio while the gate is off and by dropping congested live audio, so the first frame is not checked against earlier ones. A client that cannot deliver an utterance's audio cancels the utterance and tells the participant. Push-to-talk audio that arrives while no utterance is open is relayed to the other participants but not transcribed; the server counts and logs it.

**Final transcripts are punctuated.** The streaming recognizer produces lowercase words without punctuation, so the server restores capitals and punctuation in each utterance's final transcript with a sherpa-onnx punctuation model, on the recognition thread, before the transcript is reported, and ends a punctuated transcript with a full stop, question mark or exclamation mark. Punctuation changes only case and punctuation: if the model changes the words, or fails, the transcript is sent as recognized, and the utterance does not fail. A Rust panic in the punctuation model disables punctuation, logged once at error level, and later transcripts are sent as recognized until the server restarts; it does not disable recognition, and participants are not told, as their transcripts are still delivered. The punctuation model is unloaded with the recognizer, on the recognition thread. Partial transcripts are not punctuated, as their committed words must not change while the participant is speaking. If the punctuation model is not installed or cannot load, transcripts are lowercase and unpunctuated, and the server says so when it starts.

**The offline backend transcribes voice messages of up to 5 minutes whole.** With `--speech-backend offline`, each utterance is buffered and decoded once it ends, with Whisper `small.en`, whose window is 30 seconds. An utterance of at most 28 seconds is decoded in one piece, as before. A longer one is split into consecutive segments of at most 28 seconds whose boundaries cover the whole utterance, with no audio left out or repeated, and every segment is decoded; each segment but the last ends at a pause in the last 10 seconds before its limit: the quietest point where the Silero voice activity detector hears no speech, or, when the detector is not installed or hears speech either throughout those 10 seconds or nowhere in them, the quietest point by loudness if it is markedly quieter than most of those 10 seconds. Only audio with no pause at all, such as a steady tone, is cut at the limit. The segments are decoded in order by the same recognizer, and their transcripts are joined in order, separated by single spaces, with empty ones left out. In an utterance longer than one segment, a segment's transcript is left out if it is only a bracketed note of noise, such as "[BLANK_AUDIO]" or "(static)", or if it is at most two words, such as "you", and the detector heard no speech in the segment; each such transcript is logged. A longer transcript of a segment the detector heard no speech in is kept, in case the detector missed quiet speech. An utterance that fits in one segment is decoded and reported as it is. An error in any segment fails the whole utterance visibly, and nothing is sent; a detector failure or panic only falls back to loudness, logged, and never fails an utterance. An utterance that passes 5 minutes of audio fails at once, while the participant is still speaking, saying that it was longer than 5 minutes and nothing was sent, rather than transcribing its start and dropping the rest; this bounds the audio buffered per utterance to about 10 MB. Utterances of several participants share the recognizer: those that have ended take turns a segment at a time, the one that has waited longest, since it ended or since its last segment was decoded, first, so an utterance that ends while another's long one is being decoded waits for at most one of its segments, and no utterance waits for more than one segment of each other one per segment of its own. The 5 second backlog rule above applies to the streaming recognizer, whose partial words would go stale; the offline backend shows no partials, and the length limit bounds its buffering instead.

**Synthesis starts before the reply completes.** The server consumes the agent's streaming text and begins synthesizing as soon as a document-level Markdown block of it has closed, rather than waiting for the message to complete. Following segments are synthesized while earlier ones play.

**The server says how long an utterance may be.** A server whose recognition has a limit on the length of an utterance, as the offline backend's 5 minutes, gives it in seconds in its `Welcome`, as `utterance_limit`; it is absent without a limit and from older servers. The headset shows a recording's time against it and warns 30 and 10 seconds before it is reached (see `hand-menu.md`); without it, the headset shows the time alone. The limit is not a client constant, since it belongs to the recognition the server runs.

**Speech consumes whole document-level blocks.** The agent's streaming Markdown is divided into document-level blocks (paragraphs, headings, lists, block quotes, tables, code blocks and the like), and a block is passed on only once it has closed and no later text can change it, together with every block before it. A block is never passed on in part, and nothing from a block is spoken before it closes. Lists are passed on whole, with all of their items, as are block quotes, tables and code blocks. Each block is available with its complete structure (its kind, its source span and its Markdown events, including nested blocks) alongside the sentences spoken for it, so that a later step can show a table or code block, or run a command, as a unit. Each closed block is queued as a single segment, its speakable sentences joined, so the voice keeps the cadence of the paragraph, list or other block it came from; the sentences of a block are split at a clause or word break only as a guard against the pathological block, a single line of several thousand characters, which is split at the limit so synthesis of most of a reply is not deferred. A block with nothing speakable, such as code, is silent. How the streamed text is divided into deltas does not change the blocks or the segments. A breath of 400 ms of silence follows each spoken segment, so that back-to-back blocks, such as the items of a list or a series of paragraphs, do not run together; the silence is real in the stream, not a stall, and is short enough to keep the voice sounding like one speaker taking pauses between thoughts. The default, `block`, speaks each closed block in one go; `--speech-segmentation sentence` speaks each sentence of a block separately instead, so that the first sentence of a long block starts while the rest of the block is still closing.

**The agent speaks with one English voice.** Synthesis uses Kokoro's American English `af_heart` voice, speaker 3 of the Kokoro v1.0 voice table.

**Only speakable text is spoken.** Speakable text is selected from Markdown structure: code blocks, tool output, internal reasoning, raw HTML and Markdown syntax are not spoken, and tables are not read cell by cell. A line that later becomes a table header or setext heading is never spoken as a paragraph, because its block has not closed before the line's role is known.

**Agent speech is not interrupted by participant input.** Each space has one agent speech stream that everyone in the space listening live receives, participants and visitors alike. Recording, sending or queueing input does not stop, pause or skip it. A participant may mute their own playback locally; muting applies to agent speech played again for them as well as to live speech, and other participants still play. A client silences its own playback while its participant records push-to-talk, as `live-audio.md` requires, so that the reply is not transcribed as their input; the stream continues meanwhile, and they hear it again, live, when they stop recording.

Only newly produced assistant text is spoken; history from before the space was created or attached is never read aloud.

**Spoken replies are kept for those who missed them.** The server keeps the audio of each space's agent speech, the announcements, frames and breaths exactly as sent live, for the latest two replies and at most 5 minutes of audio, dropping the oldest first; a reply is the speech of one agent turn, from its first assistant message after the agent was idle, so that a busy state the backend repeats, as when it reads a session again, does not start another. Speech is synthesized and kept whether or not anyone is listening. A space that closes keeps no speech, so that the closed spaces the server still lists hold no audio: what it still speaks as it closes is heard live by everyone in it, and nothing is played again. A participant who is in a space and listening hears live speech; one who has left, or whose client reports them away (see `spaces.md`), is not sent it, and the server keeps the position up to which they heard. When a participant joins a space or returns to listening, having missed any part of the latest reply, they hear that reply from its start, then everything after it, at the rate it plays, until they reach live speech, which they then follow. Meanwhile they are not sent live speech, so nobody hears the same reply twice at once, and other participants are unaffected. Someone who heard the whole latest reply hears nothing again. A participant whose client never reports engagement, such as an older client or the spectator, follows live speech as before: joining mid-segment, they hear it from that point. Visitors (see `security.md`) always follow live speech, the same way.

**A reply can be played again to one participant.** A participant who is listening can ask for the latest reply, or a kept reply by its message, to be played again from its start to them alone; one who is away is refused, as nothing is played to them. It plays as a catch-up does, and stops as soon as a later reply starts live, which they then hear live from its start. A participant may stop a replay or catch-up at any time and follow live speech. Agent speech played again is sent from its own source, `REPLAY_SOURCE`, so that a client can tell it from live speech; its segments are announced again, marked as played again.

**Replies can be brief.** Each space has a speech setting, `full` or `brief`, shared by everyone in it and changeable by any participant, from the next reply on. A new space is `full`, and a space is `brief` only when a participant chooses it; the choice is kept with the space across a server restart (see `spaces.md`), and a space kept before the setting was stored is `full`. A brief reply speaks each assistant message's whole blocks while they total at most about 20 seconds of speech, estimated as 300 characters of speakable text, and leaves out the rest of that message; a block that does not fit but starts within the first 150 characters, such as the paragraph after a heading or a short lead-in, is cut after the last sentence that fits instead, and the first block always speaks at least one sentence. The first message of a reply that is cut says "More on the panel." where it is cut, once per reply. What is left out is shown, and not spoken. The limit applies to each message rather than the whole turn, so that a turn's closing answer is not left unspoken after earlier messages about tool use. A full reply is spoken in full, as before.

## Rationale

Conversation latency matters most at both ends of a turn: participants should see their words transcribed while speaking, and hear the agent as soon as it has something to say. Replies are in English, so they are spoken with an English voice; participants preferred `af_heart` to the Japanese `jf_alpha` used before. Participants can queue further input instead of interrupting; see `spaces.md`. Agreed in the October 2026 design discussion.

Running the engines in Rust in the server process was decided in November 2026. The user expected speech to be "all implemented in Rust and real time / streaming as early as possible", and accepted native libraries ("Rust, native libs OK"); sherpa-onnx was chosen for both recognition and synthesis. Removing the Python workers removes their setup and the cost of passing audio between processes, and lets recognition use a streaming model instead of re-decoding a sliding window with Whisper, so the window recognition path was removed. In-process inference gives up the workers' process isolation: a native crash in an engine stops the server instead of one worker that could be restarted. The user accepted that trade-off. A Rust panic can be contained, so it disables only its engine; an engine left running after an unexpected panic could be in an inconsistent state, so it is not restarted, and participants are told it is unavailable so that they do not retry in vain. The 5 second recognition backlog allows for the model holding back about a second of audio, and for decoding at well under real time on the reference Mac even under heavy load. The streaming model was chosen as the default over Whisper `small.en` by the benchmark in `crates/speech/readme.md`, where it showed partial words sooner and finished almost at once with similar accuracy. In November 2026 the user found the streaming transcripts "way less accurate" in the headset and asked to buffer the whole voice message and decode it with a higher quality model, so the server gained an opt-in offline backend (`--speech-backend offline`) that buffers each utterance and decodes it whole with Whisper `small.en`, which the benchmark measured at 0.6% WER against the streaming transducer's 1.2%. The offline backend is not the default: it cannot show a transcript while the participant is still speaking, and the final one arrives a couple of seconds after they let go rather than almost at once, so the streaming backend stays the default and the offline one is chosen by a flag. Later in November 2026 the user asked for longer voice messages with the same accuracy, as the offline backend failed any utterance longer than Whisper's 30 second window. Cutting a message into windows at arbitrary points would split words, which each window would then decode without the other half, so segments end at pauses. sherpa-onnx ships the Silero voice activity detector, so it is used to hear the pauses, with loudness as the fallback when its model is missing and to choose between points it hears as silent; the quietest point is a better place to cut than a fixed one even in speech without a clear pause. The detector decides only when it hears a pause: it merges silences shorter than a tenth of a second into the speech around them, so where it hears speech throughout, loudness can still find a pause it missed. Segments are at most 28 seconds because sherpa-onnx keeps the end of Whisper's window for padding after the audio, and the 10 second search keeps segments long, so few are needed, while leaving room to find a pause. On a 59 second LibriSpeech recording with breaths of 0.4 seconds between sentences, segmenting with either the detector or loudness alone gave a transcript identical to the reference apart from British spellings, decoding at about 0.15 times real time on a 32-core Linux PC; a 5 minute message would take about 45 seconds there after it ends. The 5 minute limit keeps that wait, and the audio buffered for each participant, bounded; a longer message fails visibly rather than losing its end, as recognition never silently discards spoken audio. Whisper writes words for a clip without speech, such as "you" for silence and "(water running)" for faint noise, so a segment of a long message that holds only a long pause would add words the participant did not say; the detector heard no speech in 28 seconds of silence or faint noise and takes about 40 ms for 24 seconds of audio, so checking each segment of a long message costs little. Each segment is still decoded, and only a note or a word or two is dropped on the detector's word, so a detector that misses quiet speech loses at most two words of a segment rather than all of it. Short messages are left as they were, as the change was asked for long ones. Taking turns a segment at a time, in the order utterances have waited, keeps one participant's long message from holding up another's short one for longer than one segment, and keeps a stream of short messages from holding up a long one indefinitely. Segments are found and decoded once the utterance ends, which keeps recording unchanged; decoding earlier segments while the participant is still speaking would shorten the wait after a long message and may be considered later.

Punctuating final transcripts was approved in November 2026, after the streaming model's transcripts proved to be lowercase and unpunctuated because its vocabulary has neither; the larger streaming model that punctuates was too slow on the reference Mac. Punctuated transcripts are easier for participants to read and closer to typed input for the agent. The English CNN-BiLSTM model (`sherpa-onnx-online-punct-en-2024-08-06`) was chosen over sherpa-onnx's Chinese and English CT-Transformer, which was faster but writes Chinese full-width punctuation and does not restore capitals. It costs about 8 ms of processor time per transcript, small beside the time to finish an utterance, so it runs on the recognition thread rather than its own. Punctuation is an improvement to a transcript that is already correct, so a punctuation failure falls back to the recognized words rather than failing the utterance or disabling recognition. Partials are left lowercase because the model decides each mark and capital from the words that follow, so punctuating partials would change words already shown as committed, and would cost a punctuation per decode on the thread every utterance shares; the line changes once, when the final transcript replaces it. The model rarely punctuates the last word of its input, so the server punctuates the transcript followed by a probe word, "i", which usually starts a sentence, and removes it; any utterance still without a closing mark ends with a full stop.

Keeping speech for those who missed it was decided in November 2026. The user came back to a space mid-reply and could not follow the speech without what came before, and asked that, while nobody is in a space, its replies be kept and played from the start of the latest reply when someone joins, instead of joining mid-sentence. The interaction proposal (PR #65) generalized this to anyone who was not listening, including someone who took their headset off, and the user chose that a returning participant hears the latest reply from its start, rather than jumping to live speech with a notice. Two replies are kept so that a participant still catching up on one when the next starts keeps hearing it; 5 minutes of 24 kHz audio is about 14 MB per space. A replay is local listening, not input, so live speech of a later reply wins over it, while a catch-up continues behind live speech so that the reply it started is heard whole. Clients that do not report engagement stay at the live edge, because a server cannot tell whether they are listening; the spectator follows live speech for its audience. The user relies on hearing replies and asked for them to be briefer; brief speech was made the default by the user's decision, cut at block boundaries so that a thought is not stopped mid-paragraph, with the rest on the panel. Later in November 2026, speaking from the headset, the user found the panel hard to read and asked for replies to be spoken in full by default: "Why do you keep on saying more on the panel? It's better if you speak it, because the panel is just terrible." By then the agent's own instructions asked it to reply briefly (see `spaces.md`), so its replies were already short. Brief speech stays available as a space's choice. The setting was not kept across a restart before, so every kept space returns as `full`, and only a choice made since keeps a space `brief`.

Consuming whole document-level blocks was decided in November 2026. Tables, code blocks and lists are units that may later be shown, or run in the case of commands, as well as spoken, so each must arrive with its complete structure rather than as characters or partial items; the user chose this over speaking text as soon as each part of it is stable. The accepted cost is latency until the first block closes: a paragraph followed by a blank line is spoken at the blank line, a list once the next block starts, and the last block of a message when the message completes. A block that follows another without a blank line, such as a list directly after an introductory "Here's how:", does not prove that the earlier block has ended, so the introduction waits until the list ends too. While one block is spoken, the next has usually arrived, so speech continues without gaps. Splitting a closed block into sentences keeps the time to first audio of that block to the synthesis of one sentence. Releasing list items one at a time may be tried later if the latency of long lists proves too high. The blocks come from base-markdown's incremental parser, which emits a document-level block once appended text can no longer change it and makes the result independent of how the text is divided; a reference or footnote definition therefore applies only to uses in its own or a later block, and an earlier use is read by its text.

## Scope

The server's recognition and synthesis pipelines, the speakable-text filter and segmenter, and the speech stream sent to clients and the spectator mixer.

## Validation

- Test that losing a frame within an utterance fails the utterance instead of queueing a cut transcript, that sequence numbers skipped before an utterance, or next to its `live` frames, do not, and that the client queues the `live` frames of an open utterance without dropping them under congestion.
- Inspect the server's dependencies and process tree: speech must run through sherpa-onnx in the server process, with no speech worker processes or Python.
- Test, with the real streaming model, that partial transcripts arrive while audio is still being fed in real time, before the utterance ends, and that the final transcript is correct.
- Test that an engine error, including one when a recognition stream cannot be opened, fails only its own utterance or sentence, with a visible message, while other participants' utterances continue; that a cancelled utterance reports nothing; and that an ended utterance is finished before another's partial decode.
- Test that a panic in the recognizer or synthesizer is reported to participants as unavailable until the server restarts, and that later utterances or sentences fail the same way without reaching the engine.
- Test that an utterance whose audio waits more than 5 seconds to be decoded fails visibly, and that a longer utterance that keeps up does not.
- Compare recognizers with the benchmark in `crates/speech/readme.md` before changing the default model.
- Test the offline backend's segmentation: an utterance of at most 28 seconds is decoded whole, once; a longer one is cut at the quietest pause of the last 10 seconds before each limit, preferring a point the detector hears as silent to a quieter one it hears as speech, and by loudness when the detector hears speech throughout; audio with no pause is cut at the limit; the segments are consecutive and cover the utterance; and their transcripts are joined in order, without empty ones. Test that an error in a segment fails the utterance without decoding the rest, that a panic in the detector falls back to loudness, that an utterance passing 5 minutes fails while recording without being decoded or queued, that an utterance that ends while a long one is being decoded is decoded before the long one's next segment, and that two long utterances alternate segments. Test that every segment of a long utterance is decoded; that a transcript of at most two words for a segment the detector hears no speech in is left out, and a longer one, or one without a detector, kept; that a bracketed note of noise in a long utterance is left out; that a silent short utterance is reported as before; and that a short utterance that ends after a long one's segment was decoded waits for its next segment. With the Silero model, test that it hears no speech in a segment of silence or faint noise. With the Whisper and Silero models, transcribe a recording of about a minute and compare the transcript with its reference.
- Test that final transcripts are punctuated and end with a closing mark, that partials are not, and that a punctuation failure, a change of words or a panic in the punctuation model delivers the transcript as recognized without failing the utterance, with a panic disabling only punctuation, and that stopping recognition unloads the punctuation model. Test with the real punctuation model that it restores capitals and a question mark. Run the benchmark with `--punctuation` before changing the punctuation model. Start the server without the punctuation model installed: its startup output says transcripts are lowercase and unpunctuated.
- Test that the first synthesized audio for a streamed reply with more than one block is produced before that reply's message completes.
- Test that a paragraph is spoken only once it closes, in one segment in `block` mode (the default) or as separate sentences in `sentence` mode; that a list is passed on as one block with all of its items; that a table, code block and list arrive with their kind, span and complete events; that a 400 ms breath follows each spoken segment; and that feeding a message character by character, in random pieces or in one push yields identical blocks, segments and sentences in each mode.
- Test that the server synthesizes with speaker 3.
- Test the speakable filter with streamed Markdown containing fences, inline code, tables, links, headings and a line that later becomes a table header or setext heading, which must never be spoken as a paragraph.
- Test that participant recording and queueing during playback leave the speech stream unchanged.
- Test that the kept speech is bounded to two replies and 5 minutes of audio; that a participant who joins or returns having missed part of the latest reply hears it from its start, from `REPLAY_SOURCE`, at its playing rate, and then follows live speech without receiving any of it twice; that someone who heard it whole does not hear it again; that a participant who is away is sent no speech; that a client that never reports engagement stays at the live edge; and that a replay plays to the participant who asked only, and stops when a later reply starts live.
- Test that the server's `Welcome` gives the recognition's utterance limit when it has one and none otherwise, and that an older `Welcome` without it is read as having none.
- Test that a new space speaks a reply whole; that, once a participant chooses brief, a brief reply speaks its first blocks up to the limit, cuts at a block boundary or, for a long first block, a sentence, and says "More on the panel." once; and that only a space chosen brief is brief after a restart, a space kept before the setting was stored being full.

Latency targets for transcript finalization and time to first audio are not yet agreed. Time to first audio includes the wait for the first block to close.

Delegated workers speak nothing yet. In November 2026 the user decided that workers may later speak short updates, such as finishing or needing an approval, in the agent's voice for now, as `docs/proposals/audio-and-haptics.md` proposes for its second stage; how they are scheduled is not yet agreed.
