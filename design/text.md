# Text

This describes how the headset, the desktop window and the spectator draw text: the fonts, the glyph atlas they are drawn from, and the one rule for laying text out in cells.

## Requirement

**Fonts.** Text is drawn from fonts bundled in `tomori-text`, under licences that allow bundling and subsetting, the SIL Open Font License: Noto Sans Mono for Latin, including Vietnamese, Greek, Cyrillic, punctuation, box drawing and most symbols; Noto Sans Symbols 2 and Noto Sans Math for the dingbats and arrows it lacks, such as ✓ and ↵; and Noto Sans JP for Japanese, cut to the kana, punctuation and first and second level kanji of JIS X 0208. A character is drawn from the first of these that has it. Each font is a subset that `scripts/subset-fonts.sh` rebuilds from a pinned, checksummed upstream file, and its licence is shipped with it, shown by `tomori --licenses` and carried in the APK's assets. Together they are 1.74 MB, and the APK, which stores the native library uncompressed, grew by 2.1 MB with them and the code that reads them.

**The glyph atlas.** Glyphs are drawn from one single-channel atlas of signed distance fields, a tile per glyph, shared by every panel, label and sign: a glyph is drawn into it the first time it is shown, and never moves or leaves it, so that a mesh built from it stays valid. The atlas is 2,048 pixels wide and grows downwards, doubling, to at most 8,192 rows, 16 MiB, the largest texture wgpu's default limits allow, holding about 9,000 glyphs, nearly all the bundled fonts have, JIS X 0208's 6,355 kanji among them; once full, a new character is drawn as `?`, found not to fit before it is rasterised. A glyph is one quad. The shader draws its edge where the field crosses one half, antialiased by how fast the field changes across the pixel, so that text stays crisp at any distance and tilt; bold moves the edge outwards. Where a pixel spans more than a texel and a half of the atlas, as on a worker's sign a few metres away, the shader averages a grid of up to four by four samples across the pixel, so that thin strokes neither vanish between samples nor shimmer as the head moves; such pixels are few, as text that far away is small, so the atlas needs no mipmaps. Box drawing and blocks are grown by half a screen pixel, so that where two meet no seam shows. Every target text is drawn into is sRGB, and so blends in linear light, where a partly covered pixel of light text on a dark panel shows far brighter than its share: small text, whose strokes and counters are all partly covered, grew bolder as it shrank, by a third, as the eye sees it, from two screen pixels to a font pixel to a half, and the counters of "e" and "o" filled in. Light text's coverage is therefore raised to the power 1.8 before it is blended, and dark text's complement, mixed by the colour's luminance, so that text keeps one weight at every size, within a tenth. Backgrounds, bars and other filled quads sample the atlas's solid block, so that one pipeline draws both. The GPU texture uploads only the rows the atlas has gained since the last frame.

**Drawing glyphs within a frame.** Drawing a glyph into the atlas, rasterising it and computing its field, takes about 40 µs on a desktop, so a first paragraph of Japanese could take a frame or more. The headset's frame thread therefore draws new glyphs for at most about 1.5 ms a frame; a glyph not drawn yet within that is left blank, and the panel, label or sign that needed it is built again the next frame, until it has every glyph, so that a burst of new text fills in over a few frames rather than dropping any. Other threads, such as the console's and tests', have no budget. Each frame that draws glyphs logs how many and how long they took, at info where it took a millisecond or more or left any for the next frame, so that the headset's log shows what it costs on a Quest 3.

**Text in a scenery.** A scenery draws text from the same atlas, never a copy of its own: the renderer gives it, in its `Target`, a `SharedGlyphs` handle to the renderer's atlas texture, with the layout to build a text pipeline, such as `ui_panel::Pipeline::shared`, which draws a `ui_panel::Panel` in the eye's pass, and the bind group as last synced, which it takes again each frame it draws, as the atlas growing replaces it. The handle cannot sync the atlas: the renderer syncs it once a frame, before the eyes, which ends the frame of drawing glyphs, so that a scenery's new glyphs count against the same frame's budget as every panel's and are uploaded in the same sync. A scenery builds its text before that, in `Scenery::animate`; a glyph its text waits for is drawn in a later frame, as for any panel.

**Cells.** Text is laid out on a grid of monospaced cells, 9 × 12 font pixels, as before. Each grapheme takes a cell, drawn as the one character its letter and marks compose to, or two for a wide character, such as a CJK ideograph, kana or a full-width form, as East Asian Width says; a glyph is centred in its cells, and box drawing and blocks fill theirs, so that lines join. A wide character is always drawn across two cells, never shrunk into one. Its em is seven eighths of its row, with the em box centred in the row, so that lines of Japanese keep about a font pixel and a half apart in rows of 12 and 11. Every row budget, on every surface, is counted in cells as drawn, its display width: the hand menu's rows, the wrist transcript and its queue's names, the recording card, the status pill, arrival's labels, workers' signs and result cards' titles and sources. Control characters, variation selectors and zero-width spaces and joiners take no cell; a mark that composes with nothing is left out. A character no bundled font has is drawn as its base letter, or the first letter of its compatibility decomposition, such as "f" for "ﬁ", where a font has that, and otherwise as `?`, as for emoji, Hangul and kanji outside JIS X 0208. The console prints the same characters.

**Breaking lines.** `ui_text`'s one rule wraps and cuts every panel, label, sign, card title and menu row, ending text that is cut with "…": at spaces, never at a non-breaking one; between CJK characters, which have no spaces, wherever Unicode's line breaking rules (UAX #14) allow, so never before closing punctuation such as "。" or after opening punctuation such as "「"; and, for a word longer than a row, at the row's end. A wide character is never split between rows, or by a cut and its "…". Elsewhere, such as after a hyphen, a Latin word is not broken.

**Size.** On a Quest 3, Tomori draws each eye's image at the runtime's recommended 1,680 × 1,760, about 13 pixels a degree at its middle, half the display's 25, so the image, not the shader, sets the smallest text that can be read. Text reads comfortably at about one pixel of that image to a font pixel, an x-height of about 0.45°, and can just be made out at three quarters of one; at a half, as on a projection wall from its 2.8 m, the counters of "e" fill in, and words are guessed from their shapes.

**Not supported.** Text is not shaped: scripts that need it, such as Arabic, Devanagari or Thai, are drawn a character at a time, and right-to-left text is not reordered. Kerning, ligatures, proportional text and vertical Japanese are out of scope, as is colour emoji.

## Rationale

The headset's text was `font8x8` bitmaps drawn as one quad per run of lit pixels: legible for short labels, but it shimmered as the head moved, could not show Japanese (#103), and cost 50 to 80 vertices a glyph, too many for code panels (#151). #146 asked for antialiased, Unicode-capable text from a glyph atlas. A distance field stays sharp when magnified and antialiases itself when minified, from one tile per glyph at one size, which suits text seen from varying distances in VR; one channel, rather than a multi-channel field, keeps CJK tiles small, and its rounder corners do not show at the sizes drawn. The bitmap path was removed rather than kept as a fallback: the fonts are compiled in and parsed by tests, so loading them cannot fail at run time, and an atlas that is full still draws `?`.

The cell grid is kept, so that no layout, row budget or hand menu width changes: the monospaced face fits it, and CJK takes two cells as in a terminal. Noto Sans CJK is 16 MB a weight; JIS X 0208's kanji cover nearly all everyday Japanese in about 1.5 MB. Other CJK can be loaded on demand later, with language packs (#103): a face for the space's language from the server, cached by its hash, or the system's Noto Sans CJK where Android has one. Proposed in #146, and asked for by the maintainer in November 2026.

In November 2026 a downstream environment that draws labels in its scenery had to build and sync an atlas texture of its own, which duplicated the texture and, syncing it, ended the frame's glyph budget early; the maintainer asked for the scenery to be handed the renderer's own, as narrowly as drawing needs, so that one atlas and one budget serve every surface.

## Scope

`tomori-text` (fonts, coverage, the atlas and its per-frame budget), `ui_text` and `canvas` in `tomori-scene` (cells, wrapping and glyph placement), and the panel, label and sign pipelines, `glyphs` and the atlas a scenery is given in `tomori-render`. Result cards' content keeps its own ASCII layout, which escapes other characters as code points; their titles and sources are cut by `ui_text`'s rule.

## Validation

- Test that the bundled fonts parse, cover the characters Tomori draws, including accented Latin, Vietnamese, Greek, Cyrillic, the interface's symbols and Japanese, and that their licences name every face.
- Test the atlas's packing: tiles never overlap, it grows to its limit and then draws `?` without rasterising what does not fit, a glyph is drawn once, and a CJK glyph is finer and an em wide; and the distance field's values against shapes with known distances.
- Test the frame budget: a frame draws glyphs until its budget is spent and the rest wait for the next, a thread with no budget draws every glyph, and the hand menu's and transcript's panels, arrival's labels and workers' signs built while glyphs waited are built again until they have them.
- Test that a scenery given the renderer's atlas draws from the bind group the renderer syncs, and that the glyphs its text needs, built as it is animated, count against the frame the renderer ends, the rest waiting for the next frame; and that a handle from an atlas someone else holds draws from what they sync.
- Test the GPU texture's uploads: the rows of a shelf filled and closed since the last sync are uploaded with the open shelf's.
- Test the layout: graphemes are one cell, CJK takes two, lines break between CJK characters but not before closing or after opening punctuation, a wide character is never split or drawn shrunk, and lines of Japanese keep apart; and that with Japanese, Latin mixed with Japanese, and emoji, every hand menu row, the wrist transcript's rows, workers' signs and result cards' titles and sources fit their widths in cells, end with "…" where cut, and draw every glyph they keep.
- Render Latin with accents, Japanese and code with symbols on lavapipe, and check that every glyph draws within a fifth of the ink its tile holds and none is the replacement, that edges are antialiased, that Latin letters share a baseline, that Japanese is centred in its two cells, that code's symbols stay within their cells, that bold draws more ink, and that text at a third or half of a screen pixel to a font pixel keeps its ink as it moves by fractions of a pixel rather than shimmering. Render "projection" as the headset draws a panel, into an sRGB target, and check that it draws as much ink, as the eye sees it, at half a screen pixel to a font pixel as at two, within a tenth, and that at three quarters the counter of "e" is less than about a third filled and those of "o" and "p" are open.
- On the headset, confirm that the hand menu, the wrist transcript, the recording card, the status pill, workers' labels and signs are legible and steady as the head moves, and log the atlas's size, which the headset's log reports when the atlas grows, and the time each frame spends drawing glyphs, which it reports when a frame takes a millisecond or more or leaves glyphs for the next.
